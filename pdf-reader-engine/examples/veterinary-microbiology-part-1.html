Chapter 01

History of Microbiology

Microbiology is the study of microscopic organisms (microbes) — bacteria, fungi, algae, protozoa, and viruses — too small to be seen with the naked eye. The field began when people learned to grind lenses from glass and combine them for sufficient magnification.

Father of Microbiology
Antony Van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723) of Holland — a merchant by profession who ground lenses as a hobby. Achieved ~300–400× useful magnification. Observed bacteria, protozoa, yeasts and all microbial forms now known except viruses. Called microbes "animalcules" (little animals). Communicated findings to the Royal Society of London. Elected member of the society in 1680.

Theory of Spontaneous Generation

This theory held that living beings arise spontaneously from non-living matter (abiogenesis). Several scientists worked to disprove it:

ScientistYearExperimentResult
Francesco Redi1688Placed decaying meat in 6 jars — 3 open, 3 sealed with lidsMaggots only in open jars; sealed jars had no maggots → disproved spontaneous generation for maggots
Spallanzani1769Boiled and sealed brothsNo microbes developed when done carefully; Needham criticised — said exclusion of air invalidated results
Louis Pasteur1861Swan-neck flask experiment — broths closed with cotton but allowed air inGerms accumulated on cotton; air entered but no microbial growth. Final disproof of spontaneous generation

Theory of Biogenesis

Rudolph Virchow (1858) stated that living cells can only arise from pre-existing living cells. This concept (biogenesis) was proved by Louis Pasteur and other scientists.

Germ Theory of Disease

States that infectious diseases are caused by microbes. Proposed during the Golden Age of Microbiology (1857–1914) against the belief that disease was punishment or caused by demons. Joseph Lister proved pathogenic microbes could be transmitted from patient to patient if hands were unwashed, instruments unsterilised, and wounds undisinfected.

Koch's Postulates EXAM HOT
  1. The agent must be present in every case of the disease.
  2. The agent must be isolated and cultured in vitro.
  3. The disease must be reproduced when a pure culture is inoculated into a susceptible host.
  4. The agent must be recoverable from the experimentally infected host.

Structures External to Cell Wall

Flagella

Flagella are hair-like helical appendages that protrude through the cell wall. Responsible for bacterial motility. Diameter = 0.01–0.02 µm (thinner than eukaryotic flagella/cilia).

TypeDescriptionExample
MonotrichousSingle polar flagellumPseudomonas aeruginosa
LophotrichousCluster of polar flagellaPseudomonas fluorescens
AmphitrichousSingle or clusters at BOTH cell polesAquaspirillum serpens
PeritrichousSurrounded by lateral flagellaSalmonella typhi
AtrichousNo flagella—
Structure of Flagellum (3 Parts)
1. Basal body — associated with cytoplasmic membrane and cell wall; Gram-negative bacteria have 4 basal rings (L, P, S, M); Gram-positive have only 2 rings
2. Short hook — connecting piece
3. Helical filament — several times the length of the cell; composed of protein subunits (flagellin) in helical fashion

Movement = rotation of helical flagella (like a corkscrew). Energy from proton motive force (pmf).

Pili (Fimbriae)

Pili are hollow, non-helical, filamentous appendages. Shorter, thinner and more numerous than flagella. Not involved in motility. Composed of a protein monomer called pilin.

  • Found in both motile and non-motile bacteria
  • Major role: attachment of bacteria to epithelial cells lining respiratory, intestinal, and genitourinary tracts — prevents washing away by mucus/body fluids, allowing infection to be established
  • Sex pili — involved in conjugation (DNA transfer)

Capsule EXAM HOT

Some bacteria are covered by a viscous substance called glycocalyx. Can be a capsule (tightly attached, definite boundaries) or slime layer (loosely associated, easily washed off).

Functions of Capsule
  • Protection against temporary drying (binds water molecules)
  • Blocks attachment of bacteriophages
  • Antiphagocytic — inhibits engulfment by leukocytes → contributes to virulence
  • Promotes attachment of bacteria to surfaces
  • Increases stability of bacterial suspension (electric charges prevent cell aggregation)

Sheath, Prosthecae & Stalk

Sheath
Hollow tube enclosing chains or trichomes; found in some aquatic bacteria; may be impregnated with ferric or manganese hydroxides for strength.
Prosthecae
Semi-rigid extensions of cell wall and cytoplasmic membrane; diameter less than cell; found in many aerobic bacteria in aquatic environments.
Stalk
Non-living ribbon-like or tubular appendages excreted by the cell; aid in attachment.

Cell Wall Structure

The cell wall is present external to the cytoplasmic membrane. It is a rigid structure that gives shape to the cell and prevents it from bursting in hypotonic environments. Constitutes 10–40% of the dry weight of the cell.

Peptidoglycan (Murein) EXAM HOT

The shape-determining component. An insoluble, porous, cross-linked polymer found only in prokaryotes. Forms a "bag-shaped macromolecule" surrounding the cytoplasmic membrane. Exists in a dynamic state — continuously degraded and rebuilt.

Peptidoglycan Composition
Polymers of: N-acetylglucosamine + N-acetylmuramic acid + L-alanine + D-alanine + D-glutamate + a diaminoacid (lysine or diaminopimelic acid)

Gram-Positive Cell Wall EXAM HOT

  • Higher amount of peptidoglycan — may be 50% or more of dry weight
  • Cell wall of Streptococcus pyogenes has polysaccharides covalently linked to peptidoglycan
  • S. aureus and S. faecalis contain teichoic acids — acidic polymers of ribitol phosphate or glycerol phosphate, covalently linked to peptidoglycan
  • Teichoic acids bind magnesium ions → protect bacteria from thermal injury by stabilising cytoplasmic membrane
  • Very little lipid in most Gram-positive cell walls (exception: Mycobacterium and Corynebacterium)
  • Mycobacterium and Corynebacterium have high lipid content; mycolic acids give acid-fast character; cord factor (trehalose dimycolate) is toxic and plays a role in pathogenesis

Gram-Negative Cell Wall EXAM HOT

  • More complex than Gram-positive; main difference = presence of outer membrane
  • Outer membrane surrounds a thin layer of peptidoglycan (only ~10% of dry weight)
  • Rich in lipids (11–22% of dry weight) due to outer membrane
  • Outer membrane functions: impermeable barrier preventing escape of enzymes from periplasmic space
  • The space between cytoplasmic membrane and outer membrane = periplasmic space
  • Outer membrane attached to peptidoglycan by Braun's lipoprotein
  • Outer membrane is a bilayer of phospholipids, proteins, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)
  • LPS = endotoxin; toxic; present in outer layer; made of Lipid A + core polysaccharide + polysaccharide O antigens

Structures Internal to Cell Wall & Spores

Cytoplasmic Membrane

  • 7.5 nm thick; composed of phospholipids (20–30%) and proteins (60–70%)
  • Phospholipids form a bilayer; lipid part has fluidity — components can move laterally
  • Two protein types: Integral (removed only by detergent destruction) and Peripheral (removed by mild treatments like osmotic shock)
  • Acts as a hydrophobic barrier to water-soluble molecules; specific proteins facilitate small molecule passage
  • Contains enzymes for respiratory metabolism and synthesis of capsular/cell wall components
  • Impermeable to protons → site of proton motive force generation
  • Damage by physical/chemical agents = death of cell

Mesosomes

Membrane invaginations in the form of convoluted tubules and vesicles. Present in many bacteria, especially Gram-positive.

Central Mesosomes
Penetrate deeply into cytoplasm; located near middle of cell; attached to nuclear material.
Peripheral Mesosomes
Shallow penetrations; not restricted to central location; not associated with nuclear material.

Believed to play a role in cell division. However, electron microscopic studies suggest mesosomes may be an artifact.

Cytoplasm & Inclusions

  • Ribosome sedimentation coefficient = 70S (50S + 30S subunits); eukaryotes = 80S (60S + 40S)
  • Some ribosomes free; others associated with inner surface of cytoplasmic membrane (secretory proteins)

Nuclear Material

Prokaryotes have NO membrane-enclosed nucleus. DNA is concentrated in an area called the nucleoid (also: chromatin body, nuclear equivalent, bacterial chromosome). Seen by Feulgen staining in light microscope or as light area with delicate fibrillar structure in EM. Nuclear material is highly coiled to fit within the cell (sometimes >1000× cell size).

Key Nutrients

  • Nitrogen — some bacteria fix atmospheric N₂; others use nitrates, nitrites, ammonium salts, or amino acids
  • Water — universal solvent; all nutrients must be in aqueous solution before entering cells; involved in hydrolytic reactions; high specific heat = resistance to temperature changes
  • Oxygen — obtained from water, organic nutrients, or molecular O₂
  • Sulphur — needed for cysteine, cystine, methionine synthesis
  • Phosphorus — component of nucleotides, nucleic acids, phospholipids, teichoic acids; supplied as phosphate
  • Metal ions — K, Ca, Mg, Fe required; Zn, Cu, Mn, Ni, Co needed at trace levels (cofactors)
  • Vitamins — function as coenzymes or building blocks for coenzymes; some bacteria synthesise them, others require them

Chapter 07

Bacterial Growth & Cultivation

Bacterial Cell Division

  • Primary mode = binary fission — transverse septum formation; asexual reproduction
  • Some reproduce by budding (eg: Rhodopseudomonas acidophilia)
  • Actinomyces and Nocardia reproduce by fragmentation of filaments
  • Septum formation triggered by completion of DNA replication
  • In Gram-positive: mesosome attached to cytoplasmic membrane at division site — may aid new membrane synthesis
  • In Gram-negative: outer membrane pleb or fold forms; outer membrane does NOT invaginate until final septum stages

Continuous Culture

Fresh sterile medium enters culture vessel at the same rate as spent medium containing cells is removed → cell concentration kept constant. Rate of new cell production = rate of cell loss. The widely used system is the chemostat.

Chapter 08

Distribution, Transmission & Toxins

Sources of Infection

Animal Sources
  • Normal flora — commensals that can become pathogenic
  • Incubation period animals — may excrete pathogen before showing signs
  • Animals with overt disease
  • Convalescent carriers — shed pathogen for weeks/months after clinical recovery
  • Contact/subclinical carriers — acquire pathogen from infected animals without contracting disease; carrier state may last days to months
Inanimate Sources (Fomites)
Contaminated utensils, feed and water troughs, vehicles, instruments.

Routes of Transmission

  • Direct contact: Contact with discharges from infected host; coitus; vertical transmission (mother to offspring)
  • Indirect contact: Via contaminated vehicles (water, milk, food, litter, air, dust = fomites); contaminated instruments

Portals of Entry

Inhalation | Ingestion | Inoculation through skin or mucous membrane | Coitus / contaminated instruments / AI catheters | Transplacental via umbilicus

Important Terminology
Nosocomial infections = hospital-acquired infections | Iatrogenic infections = physician-induced infections

Bacteremia, Septicemia & Toxemia

Bacteremia
Presence of bacteria in blood. Organisms gain entry from initial infection site; can spread to various organs via bloodstream. Eg: Leptospira reaching kidneys via bacteremia.
Septicemia
Bacteria in blood that actively multiply and produce toxic products. Severe form: anthrax — bacteria may exceed RBC count. Often starts as localised infection (eg: streptococcal pharyngitis, bubonic plague).

Toxemia = presence of toxins in blood.

Embden–Meyerhoff Pathway (Glycolysis) EXAM HOT

The most common pathway of glucose metabolism. Found in microorganisms, animals, and plants. Does NOT require oxygen — can occur in both aerobic and anaerobic cells.

Net Reaction of Glycolysis
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 2 NAD + 2 ADP + 2 Pi → 2 CH₃COCOOH (pyruvic acid) + 2 NADH₂ + 2 ATP

Key enzymes: Hexokinase → Glucose-6-phosphate | Phosphofructokinase → Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate | Fructose biphosphate aldolase | Glyceraldehyde-3-P-dehydrogenase | 3-phosphoglycerate kinase | 3-phosphoglycerate mutase | Enolase | Pyruvate kinase

Pentose Phosphate Pathway (Hexose Monophosphate Shunt)

  • Present in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes
  • Also called phosphogluconate pathway
  • Functions as a shunt of glycolysis (involves some glycolytic reactions)
  • Provides: NADPH₂ (reducing power) and pentose phosphates (for nucleotide synthesis)
  • Also provides energy from 5-carbon sugars
  • Feeds into glycolytic pathway via fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
Net Reaction — Pentose Phosphate Pathway
6 glucose-6-phosphate + 12 NADP⁺ → 5 glucose-6-phosphate + 6 CO₂ + 12 NADPH₂ + Pi

Entner-Doudoroff Pathway

  • Found in aerobic and anaerobic prokaryotes only (NOT in eukaryotes)
  • Glucose → glucose-6-phosphate → 6-phosphogluconic acid → 2-keto-3-deoxy-6-phosphogluconic acid (KDPG)
  • KDPG cleaved to pyruvic acid + glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate → second pyruvate via EMP enzymes

Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) Cycle — Krebs Cycle EXAM HOT

An amphibolic cycle — functions in both catabolism AND anabolism. Intermediates are precursors for biosynthesis of amino acids, purines, pyrimidines, etc.

TCA Cycle — Net Reaction
Acetyl CoA + 3 H₂O + 3 NAD + FAD + ADP + Pi → 2 CO₂ + CoA + 3 NADH₂ + FADH₂ + ATP

Two acetyl CoA produced per glucose → overall reaction is twice the above.

Electron Transport & ATP Synthesis (Chemiosmotic Hypothesis)

  • Energy generated by passage of electrons through a series of electron donors and acceptors
  • NADH₂ → FAD → Cytochrome → Cytochrome oxidase → ½O₂ → H₂O
  • Biologically useful ATP generated via oxidative phosphorylation
  • Chemiosmotic Hypothesis proposed by British biochemist Peter Mitchell (1961)
  • Electron flow drives H⁺ (protons) across membrane → acidification of medium → pH gradient + electric potential gradient = Proton Motive Force (pmf)
  • H⁺ re-entry via membrane-bound adenosine triphosphatase → drives ATP synthesis

Bacterial Metabolism — II (Lipids, Proteins & Transport)

Catabolism of Lipids

  • Lipids converted to intermediates of glycolytic/TCA cycle using minimum additional enzymes
  • First: triglycerides split into glycerol + fatty acids by lipases
  • Glycerol → glycerol-3-phosphate → dihydroxyacetone phosphate (enters glycolysis)
  • Fatty acids oxidised by beta oxidation — successive removal of 2-carbon fragments as acetyl CoA
  • Acetyl CoA enters TCA cycle; H atoms/electrons enter electron transport chain → oxidative phosphorylation
  • More energy per gram of fat than per gram of carbohydrate
  • Relatively few microbial species effectively break down lipids (limited solubility of lipids)

Catabolism of Proteins

  • Proteins too large to pass into cell → bacteria secrete exoenzymes called proteases
  • Proteases hydrolyse proteins to peptides → transported into cytoplasm
  • Peptidases break peptides into individual amino acids
  • Carbon skeletons enter TCA cycle via: acetyl CoA, α-ketoglutaric acid, succinic acid, fumaric acid, or oxaloacetic acid

Chemical mutagens: nitrous acid (direct base action), base analogues (mispairing). Physical: UV, X-rays.

Types of Viral Mutant Phenotypes

  • Temperature-sensitive (ts) mutant — grows at 31°C but not 39°C; altered protein cannot maintain functional conformation at high temperature; conditional lethal mutant
  • Host range mutant — grows only in a subset of cell types
  • Plaque size mutant — plaques larger or smaller than wild type
  • Drug resistant mutant
  • Attenuated mutant — causes much milder symptoms or none compared to parental virus; basis of live attenuated vaccines
  • Hot mutant — grows better at elevated temperature than wild type

Viral Interactions

Defective Viruses & DI Particles

  • Defective viruses — lack full complement of genes; need a helper virus to provide missing functions
  • Defective interfering (DI) particles — compete with helper virus for functions → decrease helper virus replication (interference)
  • DI particles can modulate natural infections
  • Not all defective particles cause interference
Antigenic Drift vs Antigenic Shift EXAM HOT
Antigenic drift = minor changes in surface antigens due to point mutations → gradual change; responsible for seasonal influenza epidemics
Antigenic shift = major change in antigenicity due to reassortment of genome segments between two different influenza strains → sudden large change; responsible for influenza pandemics
