Introduction to Systematic Veterinary Virology

Systematic Veterinary Virology

The study of individual viruses and the diseases they cause in domestic and wild animals, organised by viral family, with emphasis on classification, morphology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and control.

Scope of systematic virology EXAM HOT

The course covers RNA viruses — Paramyxoviridae, Orthomyxoviridae, Rhabdoviridae, Picornaviridae, Togaviridae, Flaviviridae, Coronaviridae, Reoviridae, Birnaviridae, Retroviridae — and DNA viruses — Poxviridae, Asfarviridae, Herpesviridae, Adenoviridae, Parvoviridae, Papovaviridae, Circoviridae.

OIE List A diseases (notifiable)

Several diseases covered in this course are OIE List A notifiable diseases, including Foot-and-Mouth Disease, Newcastle Disease, Avian Influenza (HPAI), Rinderpest, Classical Swine Fever, African Swine Fever, and African Horse Sickness. These are transboundary animal diseases (TADs) — capable of rapid spread with serious socioeconomic impact.

Key concept — pathogenicity determinants

  • Viral factors — surface proteins enabling attachment and entry
  • Host factors — species susceptibility, age, immune status
  • Environmental factors — stress, co-infections
Chapter recap
  • RNA vs DNA virus families — the two broad divisions of the course.
  • Baltimore Group I–VI — classifies every family covered by nucleic-acid type and replication strategy.
  • OIE List A / TADs — FMD, ND, HPAI, rinderpest, CSF, ASF, AHS are the transboundary notifiable diseases in this course.
  • 3 pathogenicity determinants — viral, host, and environmental factors together decide disease outcome.

Newcastle Disease (Avulavirus)

Newcastle Disease Virus

Avian paramyxovirus type 1 (APMV-1), genus Avulavirus, family Paramyxoviridae, order Mononegavirales — one of only two poultry diseases OIE-listed as List A, alongside HPAI.

Classification & morphology EXAM HOT

  • Order Mononegavirales
  • Family Paramyxoviridae
  • Subfamily Paramyxovirinae
  • Genus Avulavirus — 9 species APMV-1 to APMV-9; NDV = APMV-1, cross-reacts with APMV-2 and APMV-3

Enveloped, helical, single-stranded negative-sense non-segmented RNA virus, ether-sensitive, 150–300 nm. Six structural proteins — NP, P, M, F, HN, L — plus accessory V and W. The HN protein handles attachment/release and serologic ID; the F protein is critical to pathogenesis (cleavability of F0 to F1+F2 determines virulence).

Five clinical pathotypes: viscerotropic velogenic (Doyle's, haemorrhagic gut lesions, "Asiatic/exotic ND"), neurotropic velogenic (Beach's, resp+neuro signs, no gut lesions), mesogenic (Beaudette's), lentogenic (Hitchner's, mild respiratory), and asymptomatic enteric (gut replication only). All strains are immunologically one serotype. Synonyms: Pseudo fowl-pest, Ranikhet disease, Vellaikalichal.

Agglutinates chicken and human-O RBCs; elutes after ~30 min. Grows in CEF, pig kidney cells and BHK-21.

Pathogenesis

Reported in 241+ bird species across 27 of 50 avian orders; occasional mammalian/reptile spillover. Spread by aerosol, direct contact, fomites, contaminated meat/water, wild birds (crows, sparrows); not classically vertical, though hatchery shell contamination can infect chicks. In layers, egg-drop is often the first sign, followed by high mortality within 24–48 h; velogenic disease in unvaccinated flocks can kill 10–15% of a flock in a day.

Diagnosis

  • Virus isolation in 9–11 day embryonated eggs; AAF tested by HA, confirmed by HI with known-positive serum.
  • Pathotyping in vivo: chick inoculation (cloacal/nasal/air-sac) — VVND kills in 3–7 days with visceral lesions.
  • RT-PCR (F gene primers), antigen-capture/competitive ELISA.
  • ICPI (day-old chicks, intracerebral, 8-day score 0–2) — velogenic ≈2.0, lentogenic ≈0.
  • IVPI (6-wk SPF chickens, IV, 10-day score 0–3) — velogenic ≈3.0.
  • MDT in eggs — velogenic <60 h, mesogenic 60–90 h, lentogenic >90 h.
  • Molecular typing of F-gene aa 112–119; MAb antigenic panels.

Serology

HI test (log2 3 = protective, log2 6+ = recent infection) and ELISA are the standard antibody assays; MAb-based HI avoids cross-reaction with other APMV serotypes.

Vaccines & control

  • Live lentogenicHB1, LaSota, F, V4, Ulster 2C — safe priming vaccines via eye/nostril.
  • Live mesogenicKomarov, Mukteswar — boosters in endemic/village settings only.
  • Inactivatedformalin/BPL-treated AAF + mineral-oil adjuvant, given after live priming.
  • Thermostable liveheat-tolerant cloned enteric strains for cold-chain-free village use.
  • Recombinant/markerF or HN gene in turkey herpesvirus vector; NP-epitope marker vaccines allow DIVA.

Eradication rests on biosecurity: isolated hatcheries, single-species sites, bird-proofing, disinfected traffic, and restricted visitor/personnel movement. In humans, APMV-1 causes only self-limiting conjunctivitis (4–7 days), mainly in lab/vaccination crews — no known transmission via poultry products.

Chapter recap
  • APMV-1 — genus Avulavirus, 9 species (APMV-1–9); NDV cross-reacts with APMV-2/3.
  • List A (OIE) — ND and HPAI are the only two poultry List A diseases.
  • 3 pathotyping tests — ICPI (day-old chicks), IVPI (6-wk SPF), MDT (eggs).
  • F protein cleavability drives virulence; HN handles attachment/serology.
  • HI titre — log2 3 protective, log2 6+ recent infection.
  • Vaccine strains — LaSota/F/V4 (lentogenic priming), Komarov/Mukteswar (mesogenic boost).
  • Public health — self-limiting conjunctivitis only, in lab/vaccination personnel.
Chapter 03

Rinderpest (Morbillivirus)

Rinderpest Virus

Genus Morbillivirus, family Paramyxoviridae — "cattle plague" of cattle, buffalo and yaks; with measles and canine distemper forms the MEDIPEST group. Eradicated worldwide (declared 2011) via the FAO's GREP programme.

Classification & morphology EXAM HOT

Pleomorphic, enveloped, helical, negative-sense ssRNA, 100–300 nm, ether-sensitive. Six structural proteins (N, P, M, F, H, L) + two non-structural (C/V). H protein attaches to host cell, F protein mediates fusion/entry — both essential for infectivity. Differentiated from PPR virus by N-gene nucleic-acid probes. Single serotype; strains vary in virulence (e.g. Nakamura, Kabete O).

Resistance & cultivation

Unstable virus — destroyed at 50–60% RH, and by heat, light, ultrasound, pH extremes and lipid solvents (enveloped). 5% NaOH or 50% lysol in dung; 25g/100ml MgSO4·7H2O slows inactivation in water. Cultivable in calf kidney, Vero and B95a lymphoblastoid cells — CPE (rounded/stellate cells, syncytia) by day 3, cell sheet sloughing by day 9–10. Rabbits are the laboratory animal of choice; also grows on CAM without lesions.

Pathogenesis & clinical course

Cattle, buffalo, sheep, goats, pigs and wild ungulates (bushbuck, gaur, nilgai) susceptible. Spread by droplet/direct contact and fomites — no vertical transmission, no arthropod vectors, no carrier state. Incubation 3–15 days (usually 4–5). Selectively destroys T- and B-lymphocytes, permitting latent-pathogen activation.

  • Peracute — sudden death in 2–3 days, before erosions, mainly calves/exotics
  • Acute — 5 phases: incubation → prodromal fever → erosive mucosal → diarrhoeic ("shooting diarrhoea") → convalescence; death 6–12 days post-fever onset
  • Subacute — young adults, milder, longer incubation (up to 15 d)

Lesions

Affinity for lymphoid tissue, secondary affinity for alimentary/upper-respiratory/urogenital epithelium. Grey necrotic pinhead foci on gums/palate/tongue coalesce into red erosions; Peyer's-patch necrosis in jejunum; congested colonic ridges give the classic tiger-striping of the colon; swollen oedematous lymph nodes; pulmonary emphysema/pneumonia.

Diagnosis

  • Samples: EDTA/heparin blood, serum, lacrimal swabs, oral necrotic tissue, superficial lymph-node aspirates (live); spleen, lymph node, tonsil (dead).
  • Isolation in calf kidney/Vero cells; AGID, counter-immunoelectrophoresis, passive HA, IF/IP, antigen-capture ELISA, RT-PCR, N-gene DNA probes.
  • Differentials: BVD-MD, IBR, malignant catarrhal fever, FMD, vesicular stomatitis, salmonellosis, paratuberculosis, arsenic poisoning.

Control & eradication

No effective treatment. India used goat-tissue vaccine (GTV, discontinued — severe reactions in crossbreds) then tissue-culture RPV vaccine (TCRV); all cattle/sheep vaccination in India stopped 1996–97 after provisional freedom. Ring vaccination surrounds outbreak foci; recovered animals are not carriers, so ELISA screens imports. RP vaccine must not be used against PPR — it interferes with serological RP surveillance.

  • MEDIPEST group — rinderpest, measles, canine distemper share structure/antigenicity.
  • H and F proteins — attachment and fusion respectively, both essential to infectivity.
  • Tiger striping — congested colonic ridges, classic rinderpest lesion.
  • Shooting diarrhoea — profuse fetid diarrhoea of the erosive-diarrhoeic phase.
  • No carrier state, no vertical/vector transmission.
  • GREP/OIE Pathway — FAO programme; rinderpest globally eradicated by 2011.
  • RP vaccine ≠ PPR protection — and interferes with RP serosurveillance in small ruminants.

Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR)

PPR Virus

"Goat plague" — genus Morbillivirus, closely related to rinderpest, canine distemper and measles; first described 1942, Côte d'Ivoire. Acute/subacute disease of sheep and goats (goats usually more severely affected).

Morphology EXAM HOT

Pleomorphic, enveloped, helical, negative-sense ssRNA, ~150 nm; hollow coiled rod-shaped nucleocapsid with herring-bone pattern. Six structural proteins (N, P, L, M, F, H) + C/V non-structural; H and F glycoproteins handle attachment and fusion. Differentiated from rinderpest by N-gene nucleic-acid probes. Four lineages (3 African, 1 Asian; one African lineage also found in Asia); Southern Indian vaccine/field strains: AR-87 (Arasur) — the vaccine strain developed at Madras Veterinary College — plus Coimbatore (CBE) and Poondi (92) isolates.

Resistance & cultivation

56 °Cheat inactivation
pH 4–10stable range
pH 6.8optimum for HA of chicken RBCs

Killed by alcohol, ether, detergents, phenol, NaOH; survives well chilled/frozen. Grows in lamb kidney and Vero cells — CPE by day 5: rounding, aggregation, syncytia with a clock-face nuclear arrangement (rare/small in Vero), plus intracytoplasmic/intranuclear inclusions.

Pathogenesis

Primarily sheep/goats; cattle, buffalo, camels, pigs infect subclinically and can't transmit. Endemic in Sub-Saharan Africa, Indian subcontinent, Middle East. Not highly contagious — needs close contact; aerosol/fomite spread, no carrier state, no vectors. Incubation 4–5 days; morbidity up to 100%, mortality 20–90%.

Clinical signs & lesions

Sudden fever (40–41°C), depression, erect coat ("bloated" look), oculonasal discharge (watery→purulent), pinpoint grey erosive stomatitis progressing to cheesy necrotic membrane, foul mouth odour, diarrhoea 2–3 days post-fever (foul, blood-streaked), dyspnoea with rocking chest movements, and late nodular lip/muzzle lesions. Necropsy: false membranes/erosions on gums and palate, pneumonia (anterior/cardiac lobes), and haemorrhagic zebra striping along the large-intestinal folds (rinderpest's colonic analogue).

Diagnosis

  • Samples: conjunctival tear swabs, gum-debris scrapings, chilled + formalin-fixed mediastinal/mesenteric lymph node, spleen, lung; EDTA/heparin blood; serum.
  • Isolation in lamb kidney/Vero cells, CPE by day 5.
  • Sandwich (immunocapture) ELISA, CIEP (most rapid antigen test), AGID (simple, 1-day, insensitive to mild PPR), IF/IP, RT-PCR, nucleic-acid probes.
  • Serology: competitive ELISA and virus neutralisation test (VNT is the OIE-prescribed test for international trade).
PPR vs Rinderpest
  • PPR: small ruminants only affected clinically
  • RP: cattle/buffalo primarily; both species involved suggests RP
Key differentials
  • Bluetongue — lip/muzzle oedema, coronitis, sheep>goats
  • Orf — proliferative not necrotic, lips only, no diarrhoea
  • FMD — milder, lameness prominent

Control

Historically tissue-culture rinderpest vaccine (102.5 TCID50) cross-protected goats for ≥12 months but confounded RP-eradication serosurveillance. Homologous attenuated PPR vaccines (e.g. AR-87 in South India) avoid this problem. Eradication mirrors RP measures: quarantine, slaughter/disposal, decontamination, import restriction, and ring vaccination. No treatment exists; oxytetracycline/chlortetracycline control secondary bacterial pneumonia. No public-health significance in humans.

Chapter recap
  • "Goat plague" — goats more severely affected than sheep.
  • AR-87 (Arasur) — South Indian homologous vaccine strain, developed at Madras Veterinary College.
  • Zebra striping — large-intestinal haemorrhage, PPR's analogue of RP's tiger striping.
  • Clock-face syncytia — characteristic CPE in lamb kidney cell culture.
  • VNT — OIE-prescribed test for international trade serology.
  • RP vaccine cross-protects against PPR but confounds RP-eradication serology.
  • No human infection — no public health concern.

Canine Distemper (Morbillivirus)

Canine Distemper Virus

Genus Morbillivirus (with rinderpest, PPR, phocine distemper, dolphin morbillivirus, measles) — multisystemic, often-fatal viral disease of dogs and many carnivores (raccoons, skunks, foxes). Synonyms: Carré's disease, Hard pad disease.

Morphology & resistance EXAM HOT

Spherical, 100–250 nm, negative-sense non-segmented ssRNA; six proteins NP, P, M, F, H, L. Stable for weeks in shaded material at room temperature and for months when frozen, but rapidly inactivated above 32°C and by routine disinfection/sunlight — the fragile lipid envelope means transmission needs dog-to-dog contact or very fresh (<30 min) secretions. Vaccine strain used in India: Rockborn strain.

Cultivation

Grows in dog kidney cells, ferret cells, chick fibroblasts — acidophilic intracytoplasmic/intranuclear inclusion bodies are the characteristic CPE. On CAM, no lesions until 10th passage, then thickening/whitish plaques. Also grown in live dogs, ferrets, suckling mice.

Pathogenesis

Airborne spread from respiratory secretions; recovering dogs may shed for weeks. Virus is carried inside macrophages (not killed by them) — reaches pulmonary lymph nodes by 24 h, then spleen/stomach/intestine/liver by day 6 (fever onset). After systemic clearance, virus can persist in CNS and skin, driving chronic neurological and dermal disease. Incubation 5–7 days. In-utero infection is rare but can cause abortion, persistent neonatal infection, or pups that die at 4–6 weeks.

Clinical signs

  • Biphasic fever 103–106°F, peaking day 3–6, often missed, may recur.
  • GI/respiratory phase — conjunctivitis, diarrhoea, cough/dyspnoea, rhinitis, vomiting (worsened by secondary bacterial infection).
  • Neurological phase (encephalomyelitis) — ataxia, hyperesthesia, myoclonus, paresis/paralysis, seizures including the pathognomonic "chewing-gum fits".
  • Ocular — keratoconjunctivitis, chorioretinitis, optic neuritis (blindness).
  • Chronic — enamel hypoplasia in puppies, hyperkeratosis of nose/foot pads ("hard pad").

Diagnosis

  • Inclusion bodiesEosinophilic intracytoplasmic clumps in bladder epithelium (PM) or WBCs/conjunctival cells (live); also in salivary gland, CNS, adrenal, bile duct, urinary tract, lymph node, spleen, skin.
  • Immunocytologyfluorescent-tagged anti-CDV antibodies enhance detection; false-negatives occur once host antibody coats the inclusion.
  • Isolation/IDAGPT, antigen-capture ELISA, PCR.

Serology: IgM = recent infection/vaccination, IgG = later phase (hard to separate vaccine vs infection titres). CSF antibody is diagnostic for neurologic distemper since vaccine-induced antibody does not cross the blood-brain barrier.

Treatment & control

No antiviral exists; symptomatic care only (antibiotics, antiallergics, antispasmodics, fluids) — once nervous signs appear, treatment is largely futile. Attenuated live vaccines (bird/dog cell culture) give long immunity; must be timed against declining maternal antibody (6–10 wk; 20% transplacental, 80% colostral) — primary dose 6–8 wk, booster 12–14 wk, annual revaccination, often combined with Leptospira vaccine. Rare post-vaccinal neurodistemper can appear 10–21 days after vaccination. Measles virus can cross-vaccinate but immunity is short-lived. No human hazard.

Chapter recap
  • Chewing-gum fits — pathognomonic myoclonic head seizure of CNS distemper.
  • Rockborn strain — vaccine strain used in India.
  • Macrophage-borne spread — lung nodes (24 h) → spleen/GI/liver (day 6, fever).
  • CSF antibody — diagnostic for neurologic CDV; vaccine antibody doesn't cross BBB.
  • Maternal antibody — 20% transplacental, 80% colostral; vaccinate as it declines (6–10 wk).
  • Hard pad disease — hyperkeratosis of nose/footpads in chronic infection.
  • No public health risk in humans.

Orthomyxoviruses — Avian Influenza

Avian Influenza Virus

Type A Influenzavirus, family Orthomyxoviridae — "fowl plague" (Italy, >100 yrs ago), OIE List A. Severity ranges from mild to Highly Pathogenic Notifiable AI (HPAI). Tamil name: Paravai Koichal.

Morphology & genome EXAM HOT

Pleomorphic (spherical/ovoid, 80–120 nm; filamentous forms up to 2000 nm), enveloped with HA and NA glycoprotein spikes, matrix protein lining the envelope, NP-bound genome (RNP). Genome = 8 segments of negative-sense ssRNA: segments 1–3 transcription enzymes, 4/5/6 → HA/NP/NA, 7 → matrix, 8 → non-structural proteins. Killed within hours at room temperature; not resistant to drying or disinfectants.

Replication — cap snatching

HA binds mucoprotein receptors → endocytosis → endosome acidification uncoats the virus. Uniquely among RNA viruses, replication occurs in the nucleus: viral RNA uses the methylated 5' cap of host mRNA as a primer (cap snatching) to make polyadenylated mRNA and full-length cRNA templates. NP re-enters the nucleus to package new vRNA into nucleocapsids before budding.

Subtypes

15 HA (H1–H15) × 9 NA (N1–N9) subtypes possible; avian disease is dominated by H5, H7 (both can be HP or LP, and both can infect humans — H5N1 causing severe/fatal illness, H7 usually conjunctivitis/mild URT signs) and H9 (LP only, human infection possible).

Pathogenesis

Waterfowl are the natural reservoir; migratory birds introduce virus to poultry, then flock-to-flock spread via infected birds, fomites, egg flats, feed trucks, shared water, and (in close confinement) air. No confirmed vertical transmission, no vectors. Incubation 3–7 days; HPAI morbidity/mortality can reach 100% within 2–12 days of first signs.

Clinical signs & lesions

  • Layers — depression, anorexia, egg-drop (shell-less last eggs), watery-green→white diarrhoea, cyanotic swollen combs/wattles with haemorrhage/necrosis, head/neck oedema, leg haemorrhage; death in 24–48 h up to a week
  • Broilers — mortality spike often the first sign; facial/neck oedema, torticollis, ataxia
  • Turkeys — similar to layers, 2–3 days longer course, swollen sinuses
  • Ducks/geese — depression/diarrhoea/swollen sinuses; young birds show neurologic signs

Necropsy: subcutaneous head/neck oedema, haemorrhagic tracheitis, petechiae on keel fat/serosa, urate-laden kidneys, haemorrhagic/degenerated ovary with yolk peritonitis in layers, proventricular–gizzard junction haemorrhages, and haemorrhagic caecal tonsils.

Diagnosis

  • Samples: cloacal/oro-nasal swabs, trachea, lung, air sac, gut, spleen, kidney, brain, liver, heart.
  • Inoculate 9–11-day eggs, incubate 4 days at 35–37°C; confirm via HA activity, AGID (NP/M antigen), RT-PCR, antigen-capture ELISA.
  • Serology: AGID, HA/HI.
  • Differentials: VVND, infectious laryngotracheitis, fowl cholera, colibacillosis.

Treatment & control

Amantadine HCl in water gives symptomatic relief but drives resistance. Control relies on inactivated oil-emulsion vaccines, recombinant fowl-pox-vectored H5 vaccines, biosecurity (traffic control, quarantine, fomite disinfection), and avoiding free-range rearing near waterfowl.

Public health & antigenic shift

Human infection is via direct contact with infected birds/contaminated environments, or via an intermediate host (pig). Strains implicated: H5N1, H7N7, H9N2. The 8-segment genome allows reassortment when two influenza A strains co-infect one host (e.g. a pig with human + avian flu), producing antigenic shift — a novel HA/NA combination against which humans have little immunity, the mechanism behind pandemics.

Chapter recap
  • 8 gene segments — enables reassortment → antigenic shift → pandemic potential.
  • Cap snatching — nuclear replication using host mRNA 5' caps as primers.
  • H5, H7, H9 — H5/H7 can be HP or LP and zoonotic; H9 is LP-only, zoonotic.
  • Waterfowl — natural reservoir, introduce virus to poultry.
  • Yolk peritonitis — haemorrhagic/necrotic ovary in layers, classic HPAI lesion.
  • Amantadine — symptomatic only, resistance emerges quickly.
  • H5N1, H7N7, H9N2 — documented human-infecting strains.

Orthomyxoviruses — Equine Influenza

Equine Influenza Virus

Type A Influenzavirus, family Orthomyxoviridae — two subtypes, H7N7 (formerly equi-1) and H3N8 (formerly equi-2). Acute contagious respiratory disease: pyrexia, harsh dry cough, mucopurulent nasal discharge. Synonyms: horse flu, epizootic cellulitis, pinkeye, stable pneumonia, shipping fever.

Morphology & cultivation EXAM HOT

Typical influenza A structure: 8 ssRNA(–) segments, RNP + polymerase complex (PA, PB1, PB2) inside M1/M2 matrix, lipid envelope bearing HA and NA (the major neutralising antigens). Unstable at room temperature, drying, disinfectants. Grown in 10–11 day eggs (allantoic/amniotic, 2–3 days) or MDCK cells (7 days); confirmed by HA with chicken/guinea-pig RBCs and haemadsorption.

Pathogenesis

OIE List B; affects horses/equines (young horses at tracks, shows, transport especially hit), with rare human infection reported. Worldwide except Pacific region; seasonal peak summer/autumn. Spread by direct contact, coughed aerosol (up to 30 m), wind (up to 8 km), fomites; no chronic carrier state, no vertical transmission, no vectors. HA attaches to respiratory epithelium → endocytosis → 1–3 days to epithelial necrosis, ciliary clumping (mucociliary impairment up to 4 weeks), predisposing to secondary bacterial infection. Shedding lasts 8 days. Incubation is very short, 1–5 days.

Clinical signs & lesions

Poor performance, lethargy, harsh cough (~10 days), fever (38.9°C), inappetence, nasal discharge (later mucopurulent), watery eyes, submandibular lymphadenopathy, limb oedema/stiffness, dyspnoea. Stress or lack of rest predisposes to secondary pneumonia, chronic cough, or viral myocarditis (occasionally fatal). Lesions: bronchopneumonia, bronchitis, pharyngitis, pleuritis, guttural pouch infection; bronchiolitis with bilateral serous discharge is the key PM finding.

Diagnosis

  • Nasopharyngeal swabs/tracheal washings → embryonated eggs or MDCK isolation.
  • HA demonstration, neuraminidase typing, antigen-capture ELISA (anti-NP MAb), RT-PCR.
  • Serology on paired sera: HI test, single radial haemolysis (antigen-coated RBCs in agarose + guinea-pig complement, haemolytic zone size ∝ antibody titre), CFT (uncommon).
  • Differentials: equine rhinopneumonitis, EVA, equine rhino/adenovirus, pasteurellosis, strangles.

Control

Both live (intranasal modified-live AE-2, 6 months' protection) and inactivated vaccines exist; killed vaccine given as 3 doses 3–6 weeks apart from 6 months of age, revaccination every 3–12 months. Vaccination is often restricted to competition/international-race horses in disease-free countries. Isolation of infected horses is key biosecurity. No known human illness, though rare infection is possible.

Chapter recap
  • Two subtypes — H7N7 (equi-1) and H3N8 (equi-2).
  • Bronchiolitis with serous exudate — the hallmark PM lesion.
  • Mucociliary impairment can last up to 4 weeks, inviting secondary bacterial disease.
  • Single radial haemolysis — distinctive serological test unique to this chapter.
  • Shedding — up to 8 days; no chronic carriers.
  • AE-2 intranasal vaccine — modified live, 6-month protection.

Orthomyxoviruses — Swine Influenza

Swine Influenza Virus

Type A Influenzavirus, family Orthomyxoviridae — highly contagious respiratory infection of pigs; common field strains H1N1, H3N2, H1N2 (from 13 H and 9 N types overall). WHO-surveyed zoonosis; SPC List D disease.

Morphology & cultivation EXAM HOT

Standard influenza A structure — pleomorphic 80–120 nm, ether-sensitive envelope with HA/NA spikes, 8-segment ssRNA(–) genome (segment 4 = HA, segment 6 = NA). Agglutinates chicken and turkey RBCs. Unstable outside the host. Grown in MDCK, swine kidney/testicle/lung epithelial cell lines (CPE by day 7) or 9–11-day eggs (4-day incubation); confirmed by HA with chicken/turkey RBCs, using 8 HA units (unlike NDV's protocol).

Pathogenesis

Pigs are the principal host; also infects avians and humans. Spread by direct contact and short-range aerosol from nasal secretions; shed up to 30 days, even from clinically normal pigs. Waterfowl/turkeys carry virus between farms. Endemic areas see year-round disease; elsewhere, seasonal outbreaks in late autumn/early winter, exacerbated by transport, mixing, crowding and diurnal temperature swings. Incubation just 1–3 days (as short as 4 h in severe epidemics). Morbidity up to 100%, mortality only ~1% (higher in piglets).

Clinical signs & lesions

Fever 40.5–41.5°C, puffy eyes, anorexia, depression/prostration/huddling, then sudden paroxysmal coughing, sneezing, abdominal breathing, oculonasal discharge; most recover by day 6. High rectal temperature in breeding stock causes abortion, infertility, weak litters, stillbirths. Lesions are mild: firm lobular lung consolidation with interlobular oedema, enlarged lymph nodes, frothy trachea with destroyed mucociliary lining.

Diagnosis

  • Lung tissue is the ideal isolation material → MDCK/swine cell lines or embryonated eggs.
  • HA test (0.5% chicken/turkey RBCs) confirmed by HI; neuraminidase-inhibition test, FAT, IP, antigen-capture ELISA, PCR.
  • Serology: HI on paired sera 10–21 days apart, ≥4-fold rise = recent infection.
  • Differentials: Aujeszky's disease, atrophic rhinitis, enzootic (mycoplasmal) pneumonia, Pasteurella/Haemophilus pneumonia.

Control

No specific treatment — supportive care only. Prevention: all-in/all-out husbandry, clean water, avoiding duck/turkey contact (including via staff), disinfection. Commercial vaccines available in Europe/North America. Rare human infection means infectious material should be handled in a Class II biosafety cabinet.

Chapter recap
  • H1N1, H3N2, H1N2 — the common field subtypes in pigs.
  • Shedding up to 30 days, even from asymptomatic pigs.
  • Incubation as short as 1–3 days (or 4 h in severe outbreaks).
  • Morbidity ~100%, mortality ~1% — contrast with high AI/HPAI mortality.
  • Waterfowl/turkeys spread the virus between pig farms.
  • Reproductive impact — high fever causes abortion/weak litters, not the virus directly.
  • Class II biosafety cabinet required — zoonotic potential.

Rhabdoviruses — Rabies

Rabies Virus

Neurotropic Lyssavirus, family Rhabdoviridae, transmissible to all mammals — one of the most consistently fatal infectious diseases. Synonyms: hydrophobia, mad-dog disease. OIE List A, Risk Group III.

Classification & morphology EXAM HOT

Seven Lyssavirus genetic lineages: classical rabies (RABV), Lagos bat virus, Mokola virus, Duvenhage virus, European bat lyssavirus (2 biotypes), Australian bat lyssavirus. Virion: bullet-shaped, 180 × 75 nm, enveloped (ether-sensitive), negative-sense ssRNA encoding 5 proteins — N, P, M, G, L. RNP core (N+P+L); surface G glycoprotein forms ~400 trimeric spikes; M links envelope to RNP. Does not haemagglutinate.

Resistance, replication & strains

Killed at 70°C/15 min, by UV, phenol, chloroform; survives >1 year frozen. G protein mediates receptor attachment; entry via clathrin-coated pinocytosis; L polymerase transcribes leader RNA + 5 capped/polyadenylated mRNAs. Street virus = wild, virulent, forms Negri bodies; fixed virus = lab-passaged (e.g. rabbits) — shorter incubation, faster replication, loses Negri-body formation and virulence, used for vaccines (Flury, SAD, Vnukovo, Kelev strains).

Cultivation

WI-38, BHK-21, CER cell lines (minimal CPE — needs antigen detection); infant-mouse intracerebral inoculation (paralysis/death by 28 days, brain IF); irregular lesions in chick embryos.

Pathogenesis & transmission

Bite wounds are the principal route (saliva/brain virus); rare mucosal/respiratory transmission. Virus replicates in muscle, ascends peripheral nerves to the CNS (brain, upper cord), then spreads back out to salivary glands. Reservoirs: dogs (canine rabies — Asia/Africa/Europe), bats/skunks (major US reservoir). Incubation is long and variable — most dogs 21–80 days, but from days to years. Once symptomatic, death is virtually certain.

Clinical course

Three phases: prodromal (1–3 days, vague CNS signs), excitative, paralytic — death within ~10 days of onset.

Furious ("mad-dog") form
  • Hyperexcitability, aggression, dilated pupils
  • Roaming, biting, pica (eats foreign objects)
  • Loses fear of natural enemies
Dumb (paralytic) form
  • Throat/masseter paralysis, profuse salivation, dropped jaw
  • Not vicious — rarely bites
  • Rapid generalised paralysis → coma → death

Cattle: abnormal bellowing, sudden lactation cessation. Horses/mules: agitation mimicking colic, self-inflicted wounds.

Lesions

Non-suppurative encephalomyelitis — perivascular cuffing, Babes nodules (glial), and Negri bodies: round/oval eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions (0.25–27 µm), most abundant in the pyramidal cells of Ammon's horn and cerebellar Purkinje cells; magenta with Sellers/Mann's/Giemsa stains, containing dark-blue basophilic granules.

Diagnosis

  • Ideal materials: brain (thalamus/pons/medulla), salivary gland, saliva.
  • Negri-body demonstration (Sellers = unfixed, Mann's = fixed).
  • FAT — WHO/OIE gold-standard rabies test.
  • IP, RREID (rabies ELISA), PCR/sequencing, DNA probe, EM, MAb typing.
  • If FAT uncertain: infant-mouse inoculation or neuroblastoma cell line (CCL-131) isolation, read by FAT.
  • Serology (rarely used, late seroconversion): FAVN and RFFIT (both OIE trade-accepted), mouse VNT, indirect ELISA (post-vaccination titres).

Vaccines — historical to modern

  • Pasteur typedesiccated/KOH-treated rabbit spinal cord; obsolete.
  • Nervous-tissue phenolisedSemple, Fermi, Umeno-doi — risk of demyelinating allergic encephalitis (4.6/1000, 3.13% case-fatality); Semple still used in some developing countries.
  • UV-treated (Webster)infected brain tissue inactivated by UV.
  • Duck embryo vaccine (Peck)BPL-inactivated, lower encephalitis risk but weaker immunogenicity, frequent local/systemic reactions.
  • Live (Hoegyes)fixed virus at sub-infective dose.
  • Avianised (Koprowsky/Cox)Flury strain in chick embryo — LEP (40–50 passages, dogs only) vs HEP (180 passages, used in humans).
  • HDCVWI-38/MRC-5 human diploid cells (1978); ~100% seroconversion, minimal reactions, but expensive; Vero-cell alternatives sought.

Public health, pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis

In humans incubation is highly variable (7 days–years, shorter for facial wounds). Prodrome (fever, malaise, sore throat) is followed by furious (hydrophobia, spasms) or dumb (ascending paralysis) forms; once established, rabies is essentially always fatal. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (vets, lab staff, animal handlers): 3 doses of cell-culture vaccine, boosters every 1–3 years. Post-exposure: 10-day observation of biting dogs/cats in endemic areas; wound debridement (no suturing), passive immunisation (HRIG or the cheaper ERIG around the wound), and active immunisation (HDCV, deltoid, multi-dose course) — combined passive+active is far more effective than active alone.

  • Bullet-shaped — 180 × 75 nm virion, family Rhabdoviridae.
  • Negri bodies — eosinophilic inclusions, Ammon's horn pyramidal cells & Purkinje cells.
  • Street vs fixed virus — fixed loses Negri-body formation & virulence, basis of vaccine strains.
  • FAT — WHO/OIE gold-standard diagnostic test.
  • Furious vs dumb rabies — hyperexcitable/biting vs paralytic/non-vicious.
  • HDCV — human diploid cell vaccine, ~100% seroconversion, minimal side effects.
  • Combined RIG + active vaccine — superior to active immunisation alone post-exposure.

Rhabdovirus — Bovine Ephemeral Fever

Bovine Ephemeral Fever Virus

Genus Ephemerovirus, family Rhabdoviridae — "three-day sickness" of cattle/buffalo. Enveloped, ether-sensitive, negative-sense ssRNA with five genes. No HA property; unlisted by OIE (SPC List D, Risk Group I).

Strains, resistance, cultivation EXAM HOT

Antigenically related to non-pathogenic Kimberley, Berrimah and Adelaide River viruses, and to Kotonkan/Puchong viruses which cause an EF-like disease. Inactivated below pH 5. Grown by IV inoculation of embryonated eggs (non-characteristic lesions, embryo death) — identified by IF in heart/brain/lung/liver of embryos (day 1–5) or one-day chicks (day 1–2); also suckling mice, Vero cells, and cattle.

Pathogenesis

Occurs across Africa, Asia, and northern/eastern Australia — absent from Europe/Americas. Spread only by insect bite (mosquitoes — Culicine & Anopheline — and biting midges); no cow-to-cow spread by contact, droplets, secretions or semen; epizootics follow rainfall; wind can carry vectors long distances. No carrier state — viraemia is brief and acute. Incubation 2–4 days (rarely up to 9). Morbidity high, mortality very low.

Clinical signs & lesions

Biphasic (occasionally triphasic) fever, 40–41.5°C, peaks 12–18 h apart. First phase: mild except a dramatic milk-yield crash. Second phase (the characteristic one): tachycardia/tachypnoea, anorexia, ruminal atony, serous/mucoid oculonasal discharge, salivation, muscle twitching/shivering, generalised stiffness or shifting lameness, submandibular/head oedema, lymphopenia with eosinopenia. Many go recumbent 12–24 h (sternal, head-to-flank, or lateral). Recovery begins 1–2 days later, usually complete without sequelae, though lameness/weakness may linger 2–3 days and milk yield takes 3 weeks to normalise; sudden death can occur in febrile or recovery phase. Lesions: fibrin-rich effusion in pleural/peritoneal/pericardial cavities and joint capsules, patchy pulmonary oedema, haemorrhagic/necrotic lymphadenitis.

Diagnosis & control

  • Paired blood samples (acute + 1–2 wk later); acute-phase clotted blood characteristically fails to contract and is streaked with fibrin.
  • Isolation via cattle inoculation; serology by VNT and ELISA.
  • Differentials: early Rift Valley fever, FMD, heartwater, bluetongue, botulism, babesiosis, blackleg.
  • Unusually treatable among viral diseases — anti-inflammatories, and calcium borogluconate for paresis (as in milk fever).
  • Live attenuated vaccine (2 doses, ≥12 months protection) or killed vaccine (~6 months); vaccinate from 6 months, annual boosters. Natural infection gives long/lifelong immunity.
  • Vector control (insect-proof housing, insecticides) supplements vaccination in endemic zones. No human infection reported.
Chapter recap
  • Insect-borne only — mosquitoes/midges; no direct cow-to-cow spread, no carrier state.
  • Biphasic fever — peaks 12–18 h apart; 2nd phase is the classic clinical picture.
  • Non-contracting, fibrin-streaked clot — diagnostic feature of acute-phase blood.
  • Treatable — anti-inflammatories + calcium borogluconate, unlike most viral diseases.
  • Rainfall-linked epizootics — vector population surges after rain.
  • No human infection reported.

Picornaviruses — Foot-and-Mouth Disease

FMD Virus

Genus Aphthovirus, family Picornaviridae ("pico" = small) — the first animal virus discovered; the most contagious disease of cloven-hoofed mammals (cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, buffalo). OIE List A.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Non-enveloped (ether-resistant), icosahedral; capsid proteins VP1–4 (all cleaved from precursor VP0); one linear positive-sense ssRNA (~7000–8500 nt, infectious genome) — 5' end has VPg protein, 3' end polyadenylated. No HA property.

Resistance

Killed by 2% NaOH, 4% Na2CO3, 0.2% citric acid; resistant to iodophores, QACs, hypochlorite and phenol (esp. with organic matter). Survives long in chilled/frozen bone marrow and lymph nodes.

Replication ("shut-off")

Rapid cytoplasmic replication (5–10 h). The (+) genome is translated directly into a polyprotein, cleaved by viral proteases into replication enzymes (incl. RNA-dependent RNA polymerase) and structural proteins; host macromolecular synthesis is switched off almost to nil (shut-off). Empty (defective) capsids are common. Cell lyses to release virions.

Serotypes

Seven serotypes — O, A, C, Asia 1, SAT 1, SAT 2, SAT 3 — with 60+ subtypes; no cross-protection between serotypes. O and A dominate Asia; SAT types dominate Africa; serotype C is now rare/eradicated in most regions.

Cultivation & pathogenesis

Grown in bovine thyroid cells, pig/calf/lamb kidney, BHK-21, IB-RS-2 (CPE 24–48 h); also 2–7-day-old unweaned mice. Endemic in Asia, Africa, parts of South America; free zones: EU (vaccination-stopped), N/C America, Australia, NZ, Japan, British Isles. Spread: direct/indirect contact, aerosol (up to 60 km overland, 300 km by sea in temperate zones), contaminated garbage feeding, fomites, AI, contaminated biologics. Human carriage of virus in the respiratory tract can persist 24 h.

Species roles in an outbreak
  • Sheep — maintenance hosts (mild, easily missed signs)
  • Pigs — amplifiers (30–100× more aerosol virus than cattle/sheep)
  • Cattle — indicators (fastest, most severe signs)
Incubation & outcome
  • Cattle: 24–48 h (experimental), 2–14 d natural; pigs 1–3 d
  • Morbidity ~100%, mortality <1% (higher in young stock)
  • Recovered ruminants can become pharyngeal carriers long-term

Clinical signs & lesions

Cattle: fever (39.4–40.6°C), drooling salivation, nasal discharge, lameness, then vesicles at tongue, dental pad, gums, palate, muzzle, interdigital space, coronary band, teats. Lesion progression: blanched epithelium → vesicle → rupture → red erosion → fibrinous coating → re-epithelialisation with a fading demarcation line. "Dry" lesions (necrotic, no vesicle) also occur. Complications: hoof deformation, mastitis, myocarditis, abortion, "panters" (loss of heat tolerance). Sheep/goats: much milder, easily missed. Pigs: severe foot lesions (can shed hoof), snout vesicles, mouth lesions usually dry/mild, screaming when forced to move. Fatal cases in any species show "tiger heart" — grey/yellow myocardial streaking (degeneration/necrosis).

Diagnosis

  • Materials: vesicular fluid/epithelium, anticoagulated blood (viraemic phase), oesophageal-pharyngeal (OP) fluid from convalescents, serum; PM epithelium, lymph node, thyroid, adrenal, kidney, heart. Ice-chilled samples reach lab within 24 h, or quick-frozen (no thaw in transit).
  • Isolation as above cell lines, CPE 24–48 h, confirm by ELISA/PCR.
  • Sandwich ELISA — serotype-specific capture with rabbit antisera to all 7 serotypes; preferred over CFT (more sensitive, unaffected by pro-/anti-complementary factors).
  • CFT — titre ≥36 positive; PCR (serotype-specific primers); in-situ hybridisation.
  • Serology (OIE trade tests): virus neutralisation (serotype-specific, 2–3 days) and ELISA (solid-phase competitive = trade test; liquid-phase blocking; NSP-based indirect ELISA distinguishes vaccinated from infected animals).
  • Differentials: vesicular stomatitis, swine vesicular disease, vesicular exanthema of swine, foot rot, chemical/thermal burns; in cattle also rinderpest, IBR, BVD, MCF, bluetongue; in sheep also bluetongue, orf, lip/leg ulceration.

Control

No effective treatment — supportive care only. Frenkel method: minced bovine tongue epithelium in broth infected with FMDV, formalin-inactivated, alum-adjuvanted — historically important, now replaced by BHK-21 cell-line production with BEI inactivation and alum-hydroxide-saponin or oil adjuvant. Alum-hydroxide vaccine: 4–6 months' immunity after two doses 1 month apart; double oil-emulsion vaccine: 1 year. Vaccine serotype/subtype must match the field strain, requiring ongoing surveillance since FMDV mutates readily during passage; imperfectly protected vaccinated animals can still shed virus subclinically. Eradication = stamping-out (quarantine, movement restriction, slaughter/disposal of affected + in-contact stock, disinfection). FAO/EMPRES regional programmes: EUFMD (Europe), SEAFMD (South-East Asia).

Chapter recap
  • 7 serotypes — O, A, C, Asia 1, SAT 1/2/3; no cross-protection.
  • Sheep maintain, pigs amplify, cattle indicate — classic epidemiological triad.
  • Tiger heart — myocardial streaking in fatal cases, all species.
  • Shut-off — near-total suppression of host protein synthesis by 1–2 h p.i.
  • Sandwich ELISA — preferred serotyping test over CFT.
  • NSP ELISA — differentiates infected from vaccinated animals (DIVA).
  • Stamping-out — the core eradication strategy; EUFMD & SEAFMD regional programmes.

Picornavirus — Duck Viral Hepatitis

Duck Hepatitis Viruses

Three unrelated agents cause duck viral hepatitis — DHV type I (Enterovirus, Picornaviridae, the classic/most severe form), DHV type II (Astrovirus), and DHV type III (Picornavirus, USA only). Not to be confused with duck hepatitis B virus (a Hepadnavirus of older ducks).

Morphology EXAM HOT

DHV I: non-enveloped, icosahedral, 28–30 nm, linear (+)ssRNA ~7400 nt with 5' VPg and 3' poly-A; empty capsids common. DHV II: non-enveloped, star-shaped (six-point) capsid symmetry with spikes at the 12 vertices, 6800–7900 nt genome, no poly-A tail. A serological variant, DHV type Ia, has been described. OIE List B; no known human infection.

Cultivation

DHV I: kills 1–7-day ducklings in 18–48 h (often <24 h) after SC/IM inoculation; in duck eggs (10–14 d) embryos die 24–72 h, chicken eggs (8–10 d) die 5–8 days — stunting, subcutaneous haemorrhage, oedema, red/yellow swollen necrotic liver. Duck-embryo-liver (DEL) cell culture gives rounding/necrosis CPE and ~1 mm plaques under agarose overlay. DHV II is hard to culture; DHV III grows in the lab but not in chicken eggs.

Pathogenesis

DHV I: N. America, Europe, Asia; DHV II: UK only; DHV III: USA only — none reported in the Pacific. Highly contagious via faecal-oral contact and fomites (brooders, waterers, feeders); recovered birds shed up to 8 weeks; rats act as a reservoir; also spread via imported live ducks or duck products. Incubation 18–48 h. Morbidity 100%, mortality 80–95% (DHV I); DHV II similar course, occurring in birds already immune to type I; DHV III milder, mortality rarely >30%, occurring despite type I immunity.

Clinical signs & lesions

Lethargy, loss of balance, spasmodic paddling, anorexia, sudden death with opisthotonos (head drawn back) — the whole course can be as short as 1–2 hours. DHV I: enlarged haemorrhagic liver (foci up to 1 cm), mottled enlarged spleen, congested swollen kidneys; histologically extensive hepatocyte necrosis and bile-duct hyperplasia. DHV II: multiple hepatic haemorrhages (punctate/confluent bands), pale swollen kidneys, enlarged spleen, occasional cardiac petechiae. DHV III: pale mottled liver with red banding and petechiae, pale non-enlarged spleen, patchy renal congestion.

Diagnosis

  • Liver is the ideal isolation material → ducklings, duck/chicken embryonated eggs, or DEL cell culture.
  • Neutralisation with type-specific antisera or inoculation of susceptible vs immune ducklings; types II and III are NOT neutralised by type I antiserum.
  • Serology is generally impractical (disease course too rapid), though VN assays exist.
  • Differentials: duck virus enteritis (herpesvirus), coccidiosis, mycotoxicosis, new duck disease (Riemerella anatipestifer).

Treatment & control

Hyperimmune-chicken-egg antibody against type I, given SC in the neck at first sign of loss, is an effective flock treatment. Vaccination: modified-live vaccines (types I, II, III) given to breeder ducks at 16/20/24 wk and every 12 wk through lay confer passive protection to ducklings; inactivated DHV I vaccine also usable in primed ducks; chick-embryo-origin modified-live type I vaccine can be given SC or by foot-web stab to day-old ducklings of non-immune breeders, with active immunity in 3–4 days. Prevention: strict isolation (especially first 5 weeks), avoid wild-waterfowl contact, and rat control (reservoir host).

Chapter recap
  • Three distinct viruses — DHV I (enterovirus), II (astrovirus), III (picornavirus, USA only).
  • Opisthotonos — classic death posture, disease can kill within 1–2 hours.
  • DHV I mortality 80–95% vs DHV III <30%.
  • Cross-immunity gaps — type I immunity doesn't protect against II or III.
  • Rats — reservoir host, key to biosecurity.
  • Hyperimmune egg antibody — effective emergency flock treatment.
  • Not DHBV — duck hepatitis B virus is a separate, older-duck hepadnavirus.

Togaviruses — Equine Encephalomyelitis (EEE/WEE/VEE)

Equine Encephalomyelitis Viruses

Genus Alphavirus, family Togaviridae — arbovirus-caused CNS disease of horses with moderate–high mortality: Eastern (EEE), Western (WEE), Venezuelan (VEE), and Highlands J. OIE List B, Risk Group III (zoonotic).

Morphology & HA EXAM HOT

Enveloped, spherical/pleomorphic, 65–70 nm, prominent spikes; linear (+)ssRNA, 11,700 nt, capped 5'/poly-A 3' — genome behaves like cellular mRNA. Agglutinates goose RBCs (males preferred).

Replication & cultivation

Cytoplasmic; spike glycoproteins bind receptors, genomic RNA is partly translated (NS proteins) which then drive synthesis of full-length and sub-genomic (+) RNAs (the latter → structural proteins); assembly/budding at the cell surface. Cultivated by intracranial newborn-mouse inoculation (10-day observation), 6–8-day chick-embryo yolk sac route (deaths day 2–4, no diagnostic lesions), or Vero/RK-13/BHK-21/duck-chick fibroblast cell lines (EEE & WEE produce visible CPE).

Strains

EEEV: strains VA33, Ten Broeck. VEEV: 6 antigenic subtypes (I–VI); subtype I has 5 variants (AB–F) — I-AB and I-C drive equine epizootics; the rest (I-D/E/F and subtypes II–VI) circulate in rodent-mosquito enzootic cycles and are essentially non-pathogenic for equids (though can affect humans).

Transmission & distribution

Bird-mosquito-bird amplification cycles; horses/humans are usually dead-end hosts (except VEE, where horses amplify due to very high viraemia). Range: Canada, US east of the Mississippi, Caribbean, Mexico, Central/South America.

  • EEE — primary vector Culiseta melanura (swamp-breeding); Aedes vexans/canadensis implicated in bird→mammal spillover
  • WEE — primary vector Culex tarsalis (sunlit marshes/irrigation pools) and Dermacentor andersoni; epizootics follow wet spring + warm weather
  • VEE — rodent-mosquito cycle (Aedes, Anopheles, Culex, Deinocerites, Mansonia, Psorophora); horses are key epizootic amplifiers, humans incidental

Clinical signs

Incubation 0.5–5 days (fever onset), or up to 5–14 days for overt EEE/WEE signs. EEE/WEE: fever, anorexia, depression → hyperexcitability, blindness, ataxia, recumbency, convulsions, death — EEE shows significant viraemia, WEE does not. Mortality: EEE 50–90%, WEE up to 30%, VEE 50–90% (morbidity 50–100%). VEE ranges subclinical → moderate → severe non-fatal → fatal, in fulminating (febrile) or encephalitic (CNS) forms, with muscle spasms, chewing movements, loss of cutaneous neck reflex and vision, circling, and a characteristic paddling motion in lateral recumbency; death can follow within hours of first CNS signs.

Lesions

EEE/WEE: gross lesions rare (brain/meningeal congestion); microscopically grey-matter inflammation, neuronal degeneration, gliosis, perivascular cuffing, neuronophagia. VEE: CNS findings range from none to extensive necrosis/haemorrhage; other-organ lesions non-diagnostic.

Diagnosis

  • Heparinised blood, paired sera, half-brain + pancreas (unfixed) plus formalin-fixed tissue set.
  • Isolation: newborn mice, embryonated eggs, Vero/BHK-21/RK-13.
  • Antigen ID: CFT, RT-PCR, antigen-capture ELISA, IF; mosquito pool RT-PCR for surveillance.
  • Serology: 4-fold titre change on paired sera (10–14 d apart) via CFT, HI (goose RBC), ELISA, and the plaque-reduction neutralisation (PRN) test — specific enough to distinguish EEE from WEE.

Control & public health

Formalin-inactivated mono/bi/trivalent vaccines; VEE vaccine strain TC-83 (originally a modified-live lab-worker vaccine). Two doses 30 days apart + annual/biannual boosters. Eradication: horse-movement restriction, mosquito control (ultra-low-volume aerial spraying), mass immunisation. In humans: EEE causes severe disease (30–70% mortality, frequent permanent sequelae); WEE is usually mild in adults but can be severe in children; both via mosquito bite or aerosol exposure, presenting as flu-like illness with high fever/frontal headache.

Chapter recap
  • Alphavirus, Togaviridae — EEE, WEE, VEE (and Highlands J).
  • EEE has significant viraemia, WEE doesn't — key differentiator.
  • VEE subtype I-AB/I-C — the epizootic equine variants; rest are enzootic/rodent-cycle.
  • Horses amplify VEE due to very high viraemia (unlike EEE/WEE dead-end role).
  • Paddling motion in lateral recumbency — characteristic VEE sign.
  • PRN test — distinguishes EEE from WEE serologically.
  • TC-83 — modified-live VEE vaccine strain.

Flaviviridae — Classical Swine Fever

Classical Swine Fever Virus

Genus Pestivirus, family Flaviviridae (pestiviruses, unlike arthropod-borne flaviviruses like dengue/yellow fever/KFD, are NOT arthropod-transmitted). Synonyms: hog cholera, English swine fever, peste du porc, colera porcina. OIE List A; no human infection.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Enveloped, spherical/pleomorphic, 40–60 nm, fringed ring-like surface spikes; C (nucleocapsid), M and E glycoproteins, NS protein. Linear (+)ssRNA, 12,500 nt, capped 5', no poly-A 3' tail. Antigenically close to BVDV and border disease virus — a key cross-reaction pitfall.

Resistance & replication

Stable to 56°C, pH 3–11; killed by ether, chloroform, BPL, cresol, 2% NaOH, 1% formalin, iodophors; survives refrigerated meat for months, frozen meat for years, and some curing/smoking. Cytoplasmic replication — genome translated as one polyprotein, cleaved into mature proteins; NS protein makes the (–) template; budding into cytoplasmic vacuoles (not the cell surface).

Strains & cultivation

Key strains: Alfort/187, Alfort-Tübingen, Brescia (virulent field types), and C-strain (vaccine). Grows slowly in PK-15 cells, confirmed by FAT/RT-PCR (minimal/no visible CPE).

Pathogenesis

Pigs and wild boar are the only natural hosts. Endemic in Asia, Central/South America, parts of Europe/Africa. Incubation 3–4 days (range 2–14). Acute-form morbidity/mortality up to 100%.

Clinical forms

  • Peracute — sudden death in piglets, no prior signs
  • Acute — high fever (up to 108°F/42.2°C), anorexia, constipation, conjunctivitis with crusted eyelids, staggering gait, recumbency, convulsions, purple skin discolouration, necrosis of ear/tail/vulva tips, neuro signs; death 5–15 days
  • Chronic — pyrexia/diarrhoea ≥1 month, weight/hair loss, dermatitis, disproportionate large head; occurs with vaccinated herds or low-virulence strains; may relapse and die
  • Congenital — virulent-strain infection → abortion/early death; low-virulence transplacental infection → mummification, stillbirth, "shaker" pigs, visceral/CNS malformation, or lifelong viraemic carriers that never seroconvert and later develop fatal chronic disease

Lesions

Acute: haemorrhage/purple skin discolouration, tonsillar necrosis, swollen haemorrhagic submandibular/pharyngeal nodes, splenic infarcts (raised-edge dark wedges), pinpoint–ecchymotic renal haemorrhages ("turkey-egg kidney"), serosal haemorrhages, straw-coloured effusions, bronchopneumonia, and CNS perivascular cuffing/microgliosis. Chronic: milder, complicated by secondary bacteria producing button ulcers. Congenital: cerebellar hypoplasia, thymic atrophy, ascites, head/limb deformity, skin/organ petechiae.

Diagnosis

  • Tonsil, lymph node, spleen, kidney, distal ileum, EDTA blood, refrigerated and shipped fast.
  • FAT/FATST on cryostat sections; IPT with a 3-MAb panel differentiates field vs vaccine strains and ruminant pestiviruses.
  • Antigen-capture (double-antibody sandwich) ELISA — simple, fast (half-day), but less sensitive, esp. in adults/mild cases.
  • RT-PCR — faster and more sensitive than ELISA or isolation.
  • Isolation in PK-15 cells, FAT read at 24–72 h.
  • Serology: FAVN, NPLA, ELISA (all OIE trade tests) — unreliable before 21 days post-infection due to CSFV's immunosuppressive effect.
  • Differentials: African swine fever (clinically indistinguishable — lab confirmation essential), BVDV, salmonellosis, erysipelas, acute pasteurellosis, other viral encephalitides, streptococcosis, leptospirosis, salt poisoning.

Control

Modified-live vaccines: lapinized Chinese C-strain, Japanese guinea-pig-adapted strain, French Thiverval strain — all safe in pregnant sows and piglets >2 weeks. Eradication: strict vaccination, garbage-cooking laws, serological surveillance of breeding sows, and (in outbreaks) stamping-out with disinfection, movement control, zoning, and up-/down-stream trace-back investigation.

Chapter recap
  • Pestivirus, not arthropod-borne — unlike true flaviviruses (dengue, yellow fever).
  • Antigenic cross-reaction with BVDV and border disease virus is a major diagnostic pitfall.
  • Turkey-egg kidney — classic acute-form renal lesion.
  • Button ulcers — secondary bacterial lesion of the chronic form.
  • Lifelong viraemic carriers — low-virulence 1st-trimester infection, no neutralising antibody, eventual fatal disease.
  • Antibody undetectable until day 21 — due to CSFV immunosuppression.
  • Clinically indistinguishable from ASF — lab diagnosis is mandatory.

Bovine Pestivirus Infections (BVD / Mucosal Disease)

Bovine Viral Diarrhoea Virus

Genus Pestivirus, family Flaviviridae, closely related to CSFV and border disease virus. Causes the BVD/mucosal-disease complex — abortion, congenital defects, poor growth, reproductive loss, and highly fatal mucosal disease. Not OIE List A/B; no human infection.

Morphology & antigenicity EXAM HOT

Enveloped, spherical/pleomorphic, 40–60 nm; linear (+)ssRNA, 12,300 nt; capsid (C) + glycoproteins Erns, E1, E2. No HA property. Two biotypes — cytopathogenic (CP) and non-cytopathogenic (NCP, the one that circulates in cattle populations) — and two antigenically distinct genotypes (BVD-1 classic/vaccine strains; BVD-2 newer, isolated since 1981), each containing both biotypes. Genotypes differentiated by MAbs to E2/Erns or genetic analysis.

Cultivation

Grows in bovine kidney/lung/testis/turbinate monolayers; must screen for NCP-BVDV contamination in bovine-origin cells/serum — overcome using BVD-free continuous cell lines. Detected by IP technique, ELISA, or nucleic-acid methods.

Transmission

Persistently infected (PI) cattle are the natural reservoir, shedding huge quantities of NCP virus in secretions/excretions. Oronasal entry → tonsillar epithelial replication → systemic spread (free virus + infected lymphocytes/monocytes) with lymphoid-tissue tropism. Incubation 7–14 days; morbidity high, mortality low (except mucosal disease).

Intra-uterine infection — the crux of the disease

  • 1st month — abortion or resorption
  • 2nd–6th month — abortion, or full-term malformed/weak/"dwarfed"/stillborn calf, or a clinically healthy but PI calf, often unthrifty with chronic scours/pneumonia
  • 3rd–5th month (nervous system developing) — cerebellar hypoplasia (can't stand/suck, convulsions), ocular defects (blindness, cataracts), bent forelegs, head tremor
  • 42–125 days gestation, NCP virus — foetus becomes immunotolerant → born persistently infected (PI), lifelong viraemia, no antibody response

Clinical syndromes

  • Acute BVD — usually subclinical/mild in immunocompetent cattle: biphasic fever ~104°F, depression, milk drop, diarrhoea, lacrimation; 1–3 days, self-limiting with seroconversion; can be immunosuppressive, predisposing to secondary pneumonia. Severe NCP strains: fever ~107°F, oral ulcers, coronary-band/interdigital eruptions, diarrhoea, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia.
  • Mucosal disease — PI cattle superinfected with a CP biotype, usually arising by internal mutation of their own NCP virus. Fever, depression, anorexia, salivation → profuse watery diarrhoea 2–4 days later; erosive oral lesions (hard palate, mouth corners, dental pad), mucopurulent nasal discharge, corneal oedema, lameness, WBC count up to 50% below normal — death in 5–7 days. A chronic form causes emaciation, recurrent diarrhoea/bloat, hoof deformity and non-healing skin erosions.
  • Unthrifty PI calves — stunted, no overt mucosal lesions, seronegative, lifespan typically ≤2 years.
  • Congenital defects — cerebellar/ocular lesions as above.
  • Abortion / early embryonic death — especially 1st-trimester exposure, or virus present in dam/semen at breeding.

Lesions

Acute BVD: conjunctival/scleral/oral petechiae, swollen lymph nodes, GI erosion/ulceration, serosal petechiae/ecchymoses, lymphoid depletion (absent in PI animals). Mucosal disease: GI-wide erosion/ulceration, haemorrhagic/necrotic Peyer's patch mucosa, extensive intestinal lymphoid necrosis.

Diagnosis

  • Whole blood, mucosal/nasal swabs, semen, lymph node → bovine monolayer culture; microplate IP, sandwich ELISA, RT-PCR (OIE trade-accepted).
  • Direct tissue ID: lymph node, thyroid, skin, brain, abomasum, placenta — IP with MAbs, RT-PCR, in-situ hybridisation.
  • Serology: paired sera 30 days apart, VNT or ELISA (indirect/blocking).
  • Herd screening: bulk-milk antibody or RT-PCR; serology on unvaccinated 6-month calves.

Treatment & control

No effective treatment; supportive care ± antiserum in acute outbreaks. Vaccination: killed or MLV, reduce acute-disease severity but don't reliably prevent in-utero infection; breeding females vaccinated 3–6 weeks pre-breeding; MLV must NOT be given to pregnant cows (risk of foetal infection) — killed vaccine needs two doses 2–4 weeks apart. Eradication: biosecurity/quarantine of replacement stock, PI-testing before herd entry (including embryo donors/recipients), culling PI animals to slaughter, and bull semen testing (PI bulls shed very high viral titres in semen).

Chapter recap
  • PI animals — the epidemiological key: NCP-infected 42–125 d gestation, lifelong viraemia, no antibody.
  • Mucosal disease — PI animal superinfected with a CP biotype (often mutated from its own virus) — near-100% fatal.
  • Two genotypes (1, 2) × two biotypes (CP, NCP) — NCP is what circulates naturally.
  • Gestational timing determines outcome — abortion, malformation, cerebellar/ocular defects, or PI carrier, depending on trimester.
  • MLV contraindicated in pregnancy — can infect the foetus.
  • PI bull semen — major transmission route; all breeding bulls should be blood-tested.
  • No human infection reported.

Coronavirus — Avian Infectious Bronchitis

Infectious Bronchitis Virus

Genus Coronavirus, family Coronaviridae — contagious respiratory disease of chickens, first described 1931 (USA). Major economic loss via poor weight gain, mortality, condemnation (broilers) and suboptimal/downgraded eggs (layers). OIE List B, no human risk.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Enveloped, pleomorphic/spherical, 60–220 nm, with widely spaced club-shaped peplomers giving the crown appearance (hence "corona"). Helical filamentous nucleocapsid; linear (+)ssRNA, 20,000–33,000 nt, capped/poly-A, doubles as mRNA. Five structural proteins: S (spike — attachment, HA, fusion), M (membrane), N (nucleocapsid), HE (haemagglutinin-esterase — receptor binding/HA/receptor-destroying), E (small envelope protein). No polymerase carried in the nucleocapsid (unlike (–)-sense RNA viruses).

Replication (nested transcription)

Slow cytoplasmic replication. Genome first makes RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, which makes a full-length (–) template; this produces a "nested set" of monocistronic mRNAs sharing a common 5' leader and 3' poly-A, generated during transcription (not splicing) via the intergenic sequence UCUAAAC. Assembly buds into the Golgi, virions exit via the secretory pathway.

HA property, strains & serotypes

Agglutinates chicken RBCs only after neuraminidase treatment. Extensive seropleurality/antigenic pleurality (S and HE variation) means serotype-specific immunity — antibody to one serotype may not protect against another. Common serotypes: Massachusetts, Connecticut, Arkansas; strains M5, M41, H151, Holte, Gray, Florida (Holte and Gray are nephrogenic, high renal affinity).

Cultivation

9–11-day embryonated eggs (allantoic route) — field strains need 2–3 passages to cause teratological changes: stunted/curled embryos, feather dystrophy, mesonephric urate deposits; allantoic fluid does NOT agglutinate RBCs directly. Tracheal organ culture (TOC, from 20-day embryos) gives ciliostasis within 24–48 h — useful for direct field isolation. Cell culture requires prior egg adaptation.

Pathogenesis

Chickens and reared pheasants only; young birds more susceptible. Worldwide. Spread by aerosol droplets, contaminated feed/water/fomites, live-bird movement; no vertical transmission, no vectors, no true carrier state (though some birds shed erratically long-term). Incubation 18–36 h; morbidity to 100%, mortality up to 50% (higher in renal form, up to 60%).

Clinical signs & lesions

Young birds: gasping, coughing, nasal discharge, wet eyes, swollen sinuses, depression, huddling, reduced growth. Layers: gasping/coughing, egg-drop, moult, and persistent misshapen/soft-shelled eggs of poor internal quality even after recovery. Secondary E. coli infection worsens outcomes.

  • Respiratory form — tracheal/nasal/sinus mucus, cloudy thickened air sacs
  • Renal form — swollen inflamed kidneys, urate-distended ureters (nephrogenic strains: Holte, Gray)
  • Reproductive form — damaged ovaries, shortened oviduct

Diagnosis

  • Tracheal mucus (live); trachea, kidney, oviduct, caecal tonsil, cloacal swabs (dead).
  • Isolation in eggs (teratology as above) or TOC (ciliostasis), confirmed by FAT, IHC/IPT, antigen-competitive ELISA, RT-PCR (S gene), cDNA probe.
  • Serotyping: VNT (eggs/TOC), HI, MAb-based ELISA/IHC, serotype-specific RT-PCR.
  • Serology: VNT, HI, AGID, ELISA; paired sera 3 weeks apart for infection tracking.
  • Differentials: Newcastle disease, infectious laryngotracheitis, infectious coryza.

Control

No effective treatment. Live Massachusetts-serotype vaccines give good local respiratory immunity and broader cross-protection but may not protect a layer flock lifelong given high field variant pressure, and carry a small risk of vaccine-strain back-passage pathogenicity; inactivated oil-emulsion vaccine after live priming gives a persistent antibody response. Outbreak strains should be serotyped and vaccines matched; strict biosecurity minimises spread; live vaccine use is minimised in IB-free areas.

Chapter recap
  • Crown-shaped peplomers — origin of the name "coronavirus".
  • Nested mRNA transcription — unique coronavirus replication strategy, common 5' leader/3' poly-A.
  • Seropleurality — many serotypes, poor cross-protection.
  • TOC ciliostasis — key isolation/diagnostic sign, 24–48 h.
  • Holte, Gray — nephrogenic strains, renal form mortality up to 60%.
  • Permanent egg-quality damage — even after apparent recovery.
  • No human infection.

Coronavirus — Transmissible Gastroenteritis (TGE)

TGE Virus

Genus Coronavirus, family Coronaviridae — acute, highly contagious porcine disease: profuse diarrhoea, vomiting, near-100% mortality in young piglets. A respiratory deletion mutant, porcine respiratory coronavirus (PRCV), complicates TGE serodiagnosis in Europe without itself being a significant primary pathogen. OIE List B.

Morphology & resistance EXAM HOT

Standard coronavirus structure — enveloped, pleomorphic, club-shaped peplomers, helical nucleocapsid, (+)ssRNA acting as mRNA, five structural proteins S/M/N/HE/E. Heat-labile and disinfectant-sensitive but tolerates cold — disease is therefore more common in winter.

Cultivation

Grows poorly in cell culture generally, but adapts to 3–4-day primary/secondary pig kidney monolayers and low-passage porcine thyroid/testis cultures — CPE (rounding, enlargement, syncytia) after 3–7 days; wild-type field isolates often need several blind subpassages before CPE appears. Confirmed by FAT, neutralisation, or nucleic-acid ID. Young piglets are the most susceptible lab host.

Pathogenesis

Clinical disease only in pigs; cats, dogs and foxes can be subclinical carriers. Present across Europe, the Americas, Asia, and West Africa. Faeco-oral spread, also via sow's milk, contaminated litter/husbandry material, carrier species, and infected swill; recovered pigs shed for 2–3 weeks. No vertical transmission, no vectors. Incubation just 18 h–3 days. Morbidity/mortality near 100% in piglets <21 days.

Clinical signs & lesions

Rapid farm-wide spread. Piglets: foul-smelling watery yellow-green diarrhoea with undigested milk flecks, vomiting, dehydration, weight loss — usually fatal under 3 weeks of age. Weaners become unthrifty; growers/finishers/adults are mild if hydration is maintained; adults may show agalactia. Lesions confined to the GI tract: distended stomach with curdled milk, diaphragmatic-surface congestion/petechiae, distended small intestine with yellow foamy fluid, enteritis, and marked villous atrophy thinning the gut wall.

Diagnosis

  • Faeces (live/dead) or ligated affected intestinal loops are the specimens of choice.
  • Isolation on pig kidney/thyroid/testis cultures as above; antigen ID via IF, reversed passive HA, ELISA, RIA, DNA probes, EM, RT-PCR.
  • Direct tissue detection: FAT on intestinal sections; double-antibody sandwich ELISA (3 MAbs — 2 anti-S, 1 anti-N); in-situ hybridisation & RT-PCR distinguish TGEV from PRCV.
  • Serology: VNT, indirect/competitive ELISA; blocking ELISA with MAbs differentiates TGEV from PRCV antibody.
  • Differentials: haemagglutinating encephalomyelitis (vomiting/wasting disease), classical swine fever, porcine rotavirus, swine dysentery, colibacillosis, arsenic poisoning.

Control

No effective treatment — manage dehydration/starvation supportively. No commercial vaccine yet reliably available (pregnant-sow vaccination being trialled). Prevention: source only from seronegative herds, sterilise swill, isolate newborn piglets during outbreaks, and deliberately exposing pregnant sows can build transplacental/lactogenic immunity for their litters.

Chapter recap
  • PRCV — a deletion mutant of TGEV, complicates serodiagnosis but is a minor pathogen itself.
  • Winter seasonality — virus tolerates cold, disintegrates with heat/drying/disinfectants.
  • Villous atrophy — the key intestinal lesion driving malabsorptive diarrhoea.
  • Near-100% mortality in piglets <21 days; milder in older/weaned pigs.
  • Non-pig carriers — cats, dogs, foxes can spread without disease.
  • Blocking ELISA with MAbs — differentiates TGEV from PRCV serologically.
  • No approved vaccine — management/biosecurity are the mainstay of control.

Reovirus — Bluetongue

Bluetongue Virus

Genus Orbivirus, family Reoviridae (Reo = Respiratory Enteric Orphan) — non-contagious, Culicoides-borne disease of sheep and other ruminants/camelids. Cattle and wild ruminants serve as reservoirs. Synonyms: sore muzzle, pseudo-FMD, muzzle disease. OIE List A; no human infection.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Non-enveloped, icosahedral, 70–85 nm, double-layered capsid: outer VP2 (major neutralising antigen, serotype-specific) + VP5, inner core VP7 (serogroup-specific, mediates insect-cell attachment) + VP3 + minor proteins. 10 segments of dsRNA, ~19,200 nt total, largest segment L1 (2800–3900 nt); methylated cap, no poly-A. Agglutinates mammalian RBCs.

Resistance & replication

Lipid-resistant but acid-labile; harmed by slow freezing (–10 to –20°C); inactivated at 50°C/3h or 60°C/15min, and by BPL/iodophores/phenolics. Very stable in protein-rich media — survives years in blood at 20°C. Cytoplasmic replication: partial uncoating only (complete uncoating inactivates the virus); primary/secondary transcription inside the sub-viral core produces capped (+) mRNAs; genome replicates conservatively with an excess of (+) strands made per (–) template.

Serotypes & cultivation

14 serogroups containing 24 serotypes total, distinguished immunologically; considerable cross-reaction with the related epizootic haemorrhagic disease (EHD) serogroup. Grows via yolk-sac inoculation of eggs (33.5°C humid incubation, embryo death 2–7 days, cherry-red haemorrhagic embryos) or BHK-21/Vero/Aedes albopictus cell culture (CPE confirmed by ELISA/IF/IP/VN).

Pathogenesis

Broad ruminant host range; clinical disease is most pronounced in sheep, rare in cattle/goats/deer (which mostly act as reservoirs). Distributed wherever Culicoides exists (Africa, Americas, Australia, southern Asia/Oceania). Culicoides is the biological vector (peak mid-summer/rainy season); ticks can mechanically/biologically transmit experimentally; also via infected semen/embryos, transplacentally, and mechanically via contaminated needles/equipment. Incubation: sheep 7–10 days (viraemia by day 4), deer 7–12 days. Sheep morbidity 100%, mortality 0–50%; most recover in days to 2 weeks.

Clinical signs

Sheep (most susceptible): fever 41.6–41.7°C → salivation/frothing, hyperaemic buccal/nasal mucosa → erosion/ulceration, grey necrotic tissue on dental pad and tongue (by day 4–7), coronary-band hyperaemia, lameness, arched-back stance, hoof sloughing (survivors show a hoof-wall dark line), weakness/weight loss/prostration/death; survivors may show a "wool break" 3–4 weeks post-fever. Reproductive form: abortion, stillbirth, weak "dummy lamb" births. Cattle: usually subclinical; when present — mild buccal/coronary hyperaemia, vesicular→ulcerative buccal lesions, erect hair over neck/thorax, hyperaesthesia, thickened dermis with folds, teat lesions; abortigenic/teratogenic experimentally but rare in the field, more commonly seen as early embryonic loss. Goats: inapparent, as in cattle.

Lesions

Facial/ear oedema, crusty nasal exudate; oral petechiae progressing to necrotic erosions/ulcers on lips, tongue, dental pad; cyanotic buccal mucosa; nasal/pharyngeal cyanosis and oedema; haemorrhage at the base of the pulmonary artery (characteristic vascular lesion); endocardial petechiae/ecchymoses; focal grey-white myocardial (especially papillary muscle) necrosis; dermal/subcutaneous head/ear oedema with exanthematous rash; gelatinous fascial exudate with focal muscle haemorrhage. Congenital BT in newborn lambs: hydranencephaly/porencephaly, cerebellar/spinal maldevelopment.

Diagnosis

  • Heparinised blood (clinical cases), spleen/bone marrow (dead), heparinised blood + spleen/lung/brain/serum (aborted/congenital cases) — ship refrigerated, NOT frozen (freezing impairs isolation).
  • Isolation: chicken embryo (yolk sac) or BHK-21/Vero/AA cells.
  • Serogrouping via anti-VP7 MAbs (IF, antigen-capture ELISA, dot ELISA).
  • Serotyping via VN assays (plaque reduction, plaque inhibition, microtitre neutralisation, fluorescent inhibition) in BHK-21/Vero/L929 cells; PCR is also an OIE trade test and can serotype.
  • Serology (OIE trade tests): CFT, AGID, competitive ELISA.
  • Differentials: contagious ecthyma, FMD, photosensitisation, pneumonia, polyarthritis, footrot, plant poisoning, PPR, coenurosis, EHD.

Control

Only supportive treatment (reduce stress, broad-spectrum antibiotics for secondary infection). Vector control is the mainstay: water management (reduce breeding sites), insecticides/larvicides, repellent dips. Vaccination only in endemic areas, matched to circulating serotypes — success is variable given serotype multiplicity and weak cross-protection; no inactivated/subunit vaccine is currently available. No human infection.

Chapter recap
  • 10-segment dsRNA genome — Orbivirus, Reoviridae.
  • VP2 = serotype-specific (neutralising); VP7 = serogroup-specific.
  • Culicoides midge — the biological vector; peak transmission mid-summer/rainy season.
  • Sheep suffer most; cattle/goats mostly subclinical reservoirs.
  • Pulmonary-artery-base haemorrhage — characteristic vascular lesion.
  • Wool break — delayed sequela in recovering sheep.
  • 24 serotypes, weak cross-protection — the central vaccination challenge.

Reovirus — African Horse Sickness

African Horse Sickness Virus

Genus Orbivirus, family Reoviridae — non-contagious, highly fatal, viscerotropic, insect-borne disease of horses, mules and donkeys. Synonyms: peste equina africana, perdesiekte. OIE List A.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Non-enveloped, icosahedral, 55–70 nm, three-layered capsid (outer, middle, inner). 10-segment dsRNA genome, ~18,000 nt, 7 structural (VP1–7) + 4 non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2, NS3, NS3A). Outer capsid: VP2 (highly variable, drives serotype) + VP5; middle/major inner capsid: VP3 + VP7; minor inner proteins VP1, VP4, VP6. Has HA property (unlike some orbiviruses).

Resistance

Comparatively stable — survives ~a month at room temperature, up to 20 years at 4°C (but degraded by freeze-thaw cycling); stable pH 6.3–12.0; inactivated at >50°C/3h, 60°C/15min, 70°C/5min, and by ether, BPL, phenol, iodophores, acetic acid, 2% formalin, chlorine dioxide.

Replication & cultivation

Standard orbivirus cytoplasmic replication (partial uncoating only — complete uncoating inactivates the virus; conservative genome replication as in bluetongue). Grown in BHK-21, MS, Vero cells; chicken embryos/CEF (no CPE/death); newborn mice and guinea pigs (intracerebral route).

Serotypes & pathogenesis

9 distinct serotypes (serotype 9 reported in India). Natural/severe hosts: horses (most susceptible) > mules > donkeys > zebras (mild); zebras, elephants, camels can be inapparent reservoirs; dogs can develop severe disease from eating contaminated meat. Endemic in central Africa, spreading south/occasionally north; also reported Middle East–India and Spain/Portugal; never in the Pacific. Vectors: Culicoides imicola, C. obsoletus, C. pulicaris (dusk/dawn peak activity); mosquitoes also biological vectors; Stomoxys/Tabanus mechanical vectors; long-distance spread follows infected-equid movement, not just wind. Incubation 7–14 days (as short as 2). Mortality: horses 70–95%, mules ~50%, donkeys 5–10%.

Clinical forms (horses)

  • Peracute pulmonary (dunkob) — incubation 3–5 d, course 3 d, mortality 95%; fever 40–41°C, congested mucosae, tachypnoea, head/neck extension, flared nostrils, heave lines, sweating, spasmodic cough, frothy blood-tinged nasal discharge; death by anoxia
  • Subacute cardiac (dikkob) — incubation 7–14 d, mortality ~60%; fever 39–41°C for 3–6 days, supraorbital fossa/head/eyelid/lip/jaw oedema, conjunctival/lingual petechiae, colic signs; death from cardiac failure
  • Acute/mixed — incubation 5–7 d; combined pulmonary-then-cardiac picture
  • Horse sickness fever — mildest, morning-low/afternoon-high fever pattern, mild conjunctival congestion, mild anorexia/depression

Donkeys/zebras typically show the horse-sickness-fever form; dogs show the pulmonary form.

Lesions

Pulmonary form: pulmonary/hydrothoracic oedema, alveolar oedema, mottled hyperaemia, periaortic/peritracheal oedema, gastric/intestinal mucosal hyperaemia and petechiae, enlarged oedematous thoracic/abdominal lymph nodes. Cardiac form: yellow gelatinous subcutaneous/intermuscular infiltration (head, neck, shoulders), extensive epicardial/endocardial petechiae/ecchymoses, especially left ventricle. Mixed form: combination.

Diagnosis

  • Early-febrile blood, spleen, lung, lymph nodes — ship refrigerated, NOT frozen.
  • Isolation: BHK-21, MS, Vero, newborn mice (intracerebral, nervous signs day 3–15), or ECE (IV route); antigen confirmed by ELISA, VNT, PCR.
  • Serology (OIE trade tests): indirect ELISA (VP7 antigen, highly sensitive/specific), CFT; also immunoblot, NS3 ELISA (distinguishes infection from inactivated serotype-4 vaccine — a DIVA test), HI, VNT (for serotyping).
  • Differentials: EIA, equine viral arteritis, equine encephalosis (often concurrent, but no oedema/lower mortality), anthrax, trypanosomiasis, piroplasmosis (Babesia caballi/equi), purpura haemorrhagica (more widely distributed lesions).

Control

No specific treatment; recovered animals get lifelong homologous and partial heterologous immunity; maternal antibody lasts ~6 months. Monovalent/quadrivalent/polyvalent vaccines used because serotypes are immunologically distinct. Mouse-brain-adapted live attenuated vaccine gives protection but occasionally causes encephalitis (esp. donkeys); safer Vero-cell-attenuated vaccine (two quadrivalent doses, 3 weeks apart) is used in South Africa — but live vaccines don't allow DIVA and can aid virus persistence. Eradication: vaccination, vector control (insecticides, repellents, screens), and stabling horses dusk-to-dawn to avoid peak Culicoides activity. No known human field infection, though certain neurotropic vaccine strains have caused transnasal encephalitis/retinitis in humans.

Chapter recap
  • Three-layered capsid — distinguishes AHSV structurally from BTV's two layers.
  • 9 serotypes; serotype 9 reported in India.
  • Mortality gradient — horses (70–95%) > mules (~50%) > donkeys (5–10%).
  • Dunkob (pulmonary) vs dikkob (cardiac) — the two classic clinical forms.
  • NS3 ELISA — DIVA test distinguishing infection from serotype-4 inactivated vaccine.
  • Vero-attenuated quadrivalent vaccine — the safer modern South African vaccine.
  • Stabling dusk-to-dawn — matches Culicoides' peak activity window.

Birnavirus — Infectious Bursal Disease (Gumboro)

Infectious Bursal Disease Virus

Genus Avibirnavirus, family Birnaviridae ("bi" = two-segment dsRNA) — immunosuppressive poultry disease, discovered 1962 in Gumboro, Delaware (hence "Gumboro disease"). Classified by pathogenicity into classical, very virulent (vvIBD) and variant forms. OIE List B; no human infection.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Non-enveloped, single-layer hexagonal icosahedral capsid (132 capsomers), 60 nm, no surface projections. Two segments of linear dsRNA (5850–6050 nt total): Segment A (~3100–3200 nt) codes VP2/VP3 (capsid, antigenic determinants on VP2) + VP4/VP5 (non-structural; VP5 is cytotoxic, lyses cells for virus release); Segment B (~2750–2850 nt) codes VP1, the viral polymerase. VPg on 5' ends, no poly-A. No HA property.

Resistance & replication

Stable pH 3–9; resistant to 60°C/1h and to ether/1% SDS. Cytoplasmic replication without full host shut-off; produces subgenomic mRNAs transcribed from segments A and B; VP5 accumulates at the plasma membrane, deforming and lysing the cell to release progeny.

Serotypes & cultivation

Serotype 1 causes clinical disease in chickens <10 weeks (further split into 6 molecular subgroups); serotype 2 infects chickens/turkeys/ducks but is non-pathogenic. Grown in chickens (eye-drop, death 72–80 h, yellowish turgid haemorrhagic striated bursa for serotype 1), embryonated eggs (yolk sac/CAM in SAN embryos — dwarfing, subcutaneous oedema, haemorrhage, mottled swollen liver/spleen/kidney for serotype 1; smaller pale embryos, no hemorrhage for serotype 2), or CEF culture (small round refractive CPE).

Pathogenesis

Domestic fowl is the natural host (subclinical in turkeys); disease peaks 3–6 weeks (Leghorns susceptible up to 18 weeks). vvIBD reported in Europe, Americas, SE Asia. Faecal-oral spread via contaminated feed/water/litter; the lesser mealworm (Alphitobius diaperinus) can carry virus; the very resistant virus is easily spread mechanically by people/equipment/vehicles. Incubation 2–3 days. Morbidity to 100%; mortality up to 25% (broilers), 60% (layers).

Clinical signs & lesions

Affects mainly 3–4-week chickens (when the bursa is well developed); younger birds are subclinical but develop permanent immunosuppression, while older infections are usually transient. Pallor, depression, dehydration, anorexia, shivering, ataxia, ruffled feathers, transient white/red/green-tinged diarrhoea, self-inflicted vent-picking; some show growth retardation post-recovery. No disease after 15–16 weeks (bursa regressed).

Lesions: swollen haemorrhagic cloacal bursa (fatal cases) or atrophied bursa (recovered), dehydrated carcass, haemorrhagic dark thigh/pectoral muscle, opaque gelatinous thymic capsule, yellow/pink fatty bone marrow, swollen liver/kidneys, mucoid intestines. Bursal timeline: apoptotic B-lymphocyte depletion within 2 days → peak swelling (5× normal size) with haemorrhage/gelatinous exudate at day 4–6 → atrophy to 1/8 normal size by day 10 → slow repopulation over 2 months; T-cells are also affected (macrophage-mediated suppression).

Diagnosis

  • Fresh bursa/spleen for isolation; formalin-fixed bursa/spleen/gut/caecal tonsil/liver/kidney for histopathology; blood for serology.
  • Isolation as under cultivation; direct antigen ID via AGID, IF, antigen-capture ELISA, RT-PCR.
  • Strain typing: cross-VNT or serotype-specific MAbs.
  • Pathotyping (SPF-chicken challenge): variant — minimal signs, no mortality, but marked bursal lesions; classical — 10–50% mortality; very virulent — 50–100% mortality.
  • Serology: AGID (most widely used, quantitative titration for maternal/vaccinal antibody), VNT (more sensitive, differentiates serotypes 1/2), ELISA (preferred for quantification).
  • Differentials: Marek's disease, mycotoxicosis, coccidiosis, haemorrhagic syndrome, avian adenovirus, infectious bronchitis.

Control

No effective treatment. Live vaccines (mild/intermediate/intermediate-plus "hot" strains, all serotype 1) — mild/intermediate given to parent stock ahead of point-of-lay inactivated boost; must be given after maternal antibody wanes; in-ovo delivery (antibody-complexed vaccine at 18 days incubation) overcomes the maternal-antibody problem. Inactivated oil-emulsion vaccines boost primed breeder hens to give chicks passive protection up to ~30 days. A live recombinant VP2 vaccine is also licensed in Europe. Recovered/vaccinated birds can shed for long periods, so eradication needs depopulation and rigorous disinfection.

Chapter recap
  • Gumboro disease — named after Gumboro, Delaware, discovered 1962.
  • Two-segment dsRNA (A, B); VP2 = antigenic, VP5 = cytolytic, VP1 = polymerase.
  • Serotype 1 pathogenic, serotype 2 not, in chickens.
  • Bursal size timeline — 5× swelling by day 4–6, 1/8 size by day 10.
  • Age-dependent immunosuppression — earlier infection = permanent damage.
  • Variant/classical/very-virulent pathotyping by mortality in SPF chick challenge.
  • In-ovo vaccination — solves the maternal-antibody interference problem.

Retrovirus — Equine Infectious Anaemia (Swamp Fever)

Equine Infectious Anaemia Virus

Genus Lentivirus, family Retroviridae — Group VI (reverse-transcribing RNA virus). Historically significant: the first horse disease proven caused by a filterable virus, the first insect-transmitted lentivirus disease, the first persistent virus with defined antigenic drift, and the first lentivirus with an approved diagnostic test. Synonym: swamp fever. OIE List B; no known human infection.

Morphology & genome EXAM HOT

Enveloped, spherical/pleomorphic, 80–100 nm, 8-nm glycoprotein surface spikes; rod-shaped core, concentric nucleoid. Genome is diploid — two identical linear (+)ssRNA monomers joined by hydrogen bonds, 9200 nt, capped 5'/poly-A 3'. Four genes: gag, pro, pol, env.

Retroviruses are unique among viruses in four ways: the only truly diploid viruses; the only RNA viruses whose genome is made by cellular transcriptional machinery; the only viruses needing a specific host tRNA for replication; and the only (+)RNA viruses whose genome does not serve directly as mRNA (hence the naked RNA is non-infectious).

Resistance & replication

Fragile — sensitive to heat, detergents, formaldehyde; irradiation does NOT affect infectivity. Survives months at room temperature in urine, faeces, dried blood. Replication (cytoplasmic, using host transcriptional machinery): SU/TM mediate fusion entry → partial uncoating → reverse transcriptase makes dsDNA provirus → migrates to nucleus, integrase inserts it into host DNA → expressed like a cellular gene → cytoplasmic assembly, budding acquires the envelope. Error-prone replication plus high mutation/recombination rates drive rapid antigenic drift — the classic mechanism by which EIAV evades the immune response.

HA property, serotypes, cultivation

No HA property; no distinct serotypes (though antigenic drift occurs within a horse over time). Cultivated in susceptible horses or leukocyte cultures, confirmed by antigen tests.

Pathogenesis

Infection is limited to Equidae; donkeys less severely affected than horses. Worldwide distribution (Australia, NZ have claimed freedom). Classic bloodborne infection — horseflies/deerflies interrupted mid-feed carry infective blood mechanically to the next host (virus survives <4 h on the insect); iatrogenic spread via unsterilised instruments; vertical transmission (in utero/milk) possible. Peak transmission summer/autumn (insect abundance). Very small blood volumes are highly infectious — 1/5 teaspoon of acute-case blood can infect a million horses; even in chronic disease, the same volume can infect 10,000 horses. All infected horses remain lifelong carriers.

Clinical course

  • Acute (first attack) — depression, profound weakness, weight loss, swinging intermittent fever, jaundice, mouth/nasal bleeding; hardest form to diagnose (signs appear fast, often only fever is noted)
  • Chronic (relapsing) — 2–3 weeks apparent recovery then relapse with milder signs: fever, mucosal petechiae, depression, weight loss, dependent limb/ventral oedema, anaemia, irregular heartbeat, jugular pulse
  • Inapparent carrier — most horses; no clinical signs, much lower blood virus titre (only 1 in 6 million horseflies transmits from such a horse), but a lifelong reservoir; stress/hard work/concurrent disease can reactivate chronic or acute disease

Lesions

In fatal cases: haemorrhages in liver, spleen, kidney, serosa, intestinal mucosa; enlarged yellowish-brown liver; enlarged spleen with soft pulp; pale, flabby, friable heart muscle.

Diagnosis

  • Field diagnosis rests on the Coggins test; since no vaccine exists, any EIA antibody = infection.
  • Isolation rarely attempted (no practical value); when done, blood is inoculated onto EIA-free leukocyte cultures, confirmed by ELISA/IF.
  • RT-PCR detects EIA proviral DNA in peripheral blood.
  • Coggins test (AGID) — OIE-prescribed for international trade; precipitin lines confirmed against a positive standard serum.
  • Competitive and non-competitive ELISA also used.
  • Differentials: purpura haemorrhagica, babesiosis, leptospirosis, severe strongyliasis/fascioliasis, phenothiazine toxicity, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia.

Treatment & control

No effective treatment; supportive care (± blood transfusion), but because recovered horses become lifelong carriers, destruction is usually preferred in a disease-free area. No vaccine exists. Eradication = test-and-segregate: identify and isolate seropositive horses at a safe distance from negatives; imports need a negative test within 30 days pre-shipment. Vector-avoidance measures: keep horses off low-lying/swampy land, drain swamps, use insecticides and insect-proof stabling. Biosecurity practice: one horse–one needle, sterilise instruments, clean stabling, insect control, no mixing of infected/healthy stock, no breeding EIAV-positive horses, isolate and test all new arrivals, and obtain required negative-test certification for shows/races/fairs.

Chapter recap
  • "Firsts" of virology — first filterable-virus horse disease, first insect-borne lentivirus, first defined antigenic drift, first approved lentivirus diagnostic test.
  • Diploid genome — two RNA monomers, hydrogen-bonded; unique retroviral feature.
  • Provirus integration — reverse transcriptase → dsDNA → integrase inserts into host genome.
  • Mechanical horsefly/deerfly transmission — interrupted feeding, virus survives <4 h on the insect.
  • Lifelong carriers — ALL infected horses, whether clinical or inapparent.
  • Coggins test (AGID) — OIE gold-standard, international-trade test.
  • No vaccine — control is entirely test-and-segregate plus vector avoidance.

Poxviruses — Sheep Pox and Goat Pox

Capripoxvirus (SGP)

Genus Capripoxvirus, family Poxviridae, subfamily Chordopoxvirinae, Group I dsDNA — fever, generalised papules/nodules, rare vesicles, internal (esp. pulmonary) lesions and death in sheep and goats.

Morphology & genome EXAM HOT

Enveloped, brick-shaped/ovoid, 140–260 × 220–450 nm. Extracellular enveloped virions (EEV) have 2 membranes; intracellular mature virions (IMV) have only the inner membrane. Biconcave dumbbell-shaped core with two "lateral bodies" (function unknown). Linear dsDNA, 130–300 kbp, ~250 genes — essential genes central, non-essential genes at the ends. Virion carries 10 enzymes needed for genome replication. No HA property (unlike most other poxviruses).

Replication

Entirely cytoplasmic — poxviruses carry their own replication enzymes and don't need the host nucleus. Endocytic entry via multiple receptors; two-stage uncoating (outer membrane lost on entry, inner membrane lost as the core enters cytoplasm); early transcription/translation makes replication enzymes, then genome replication, then late transcription/translation makes structural proteins; assembly occurs in the cytoskeleton (poorly understood).

Resistance & strains

Killed at 56°C/2h or 65°C/30min; sensitive to extreme pH, ether (20%), chloroform, formalin (1%), phenol (2%/15min) — but survives years in dried scabs, 2 months in wool, 6 months in premises. Malignant capripox syndromes include Kenyan sheep/goat pox, Indian goat dermatitis, north African stone pox. Common SPV strains: Ranipet, Jaipur, Kenyan.

Cultivation

Grows in bovine/ovine/caprine cell culture; primary/secondary lamb testis (LT) or lamb kidney (LK) cells are most susceptible. CPE: cell retraction/rounding, chromatin margination, and eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusions up to half the nucleus's size with a clear halo (no syncytia formation). Also grown in lambs.

Pathogenesis

Generally host-specific but strain-dependent cross-infection occurs; Merino/European sheep breeds more susceptible. Range: Africa (north of equator), Middle East, central Asia, Indian subcontinent to Myanmar. Direct contact, aerosol (from acute cases or contaminated dust/scabs), skin-abrasion fomite contact, and mechanical insect transmission. Incubation 4–8 days; morbidity 70–90% (endemic), mortality 5–10% (up to 100% in naive populations).

Clinical signs & lesions

Fever, depression, polypnoea, conjunctivitis, lacrimation, rhinitis, eyelid oedema, photophobia; erythematous eruptions on hair/wool-free skin (perineum, inguinal, scrotum, udder, muzzle, eyelids, axillae) → papules.

  • Papulovesicular form — papules turn white-grey, desiccate to easily-removed crusts; rarely become vesicles that rupture and crust
  • Nodular form — full-thickness dermal/subcutaneous nodules; necrosis/sloughing leaves hairless scars

Both forms cause pulmonary nodules → bronchopneumonia, cough, nasal discharge, anorexia, emaciation; recovery in 20–30 days if uncomplicated, but death is common with abortion, secondary infection, fly strike, septicaemia or GI localisation. Lesions: skin congestion/haemorrhage/oedema/vasculitis/necrosis through all layers; draining lymph nodes up to 8× enlarged; pox lesions on ocular/oral/nasal/pharyngeal/tracheal/rumenal/abomasal mucosae and genitalia; lung lesions focal and uniform — congestion, oedema, necrotic proliferation, lobular atelectasis.

Diagnosis

  • Vesicular fluid, scabs, skin scrapings, respiratory/GI lesion tissue → LT/LK cell isolation, confirmed by IF, inclusion staining, serum-neutralisation of CPE, ELISA, or PCR.
  • Histology (H&E): massive cellular infiltrate, vasculitis, oedema, perivascular cuffing (macrophage/neutrophil → lymphocyte/plasma-cell progression); pathognomonic "sheep pox cells" — large stellate cells with eosinophilic poorly-defined intracytoplasmic inclusions and vacuolated nuclei.
  • Immunological: AGID, ELISA, FAT; nucleic acid: PCR on biopsy/culture.
  • Differentials: bluetongue (muzzle oedema/coronitis, no true pox), PPR (necrotic oral lesions, high lamb/kid mortality, no skin pox), orf (proliferative not necrotic, zoonotic), photosensitisation, insect bites, parasitic pneumonia, caseous lymphadenitis, dermatophilosis, mange.

Control

No effective treatment. Live attenuated vaccine (SC/intradermal) gives up to 2 years' immunity; inactivated vaccine gives ~5 months. Eradication: isolate infected herds ≥45 days post-recovery, slaughter where feasible, proper cadaver/product disposal, stringent disinfection, pre-introduction quarantine, and animal/vehicle movement control.

Chapter recap
  • Capripoxvirus — genus covers both sheep pox and goat pox, host-specificity is strain-dependent.
  • EEV vs IMV — extracellular (2 membranes) vs intracellular (1 membrane) forms.
  • No HA property — unusual among poxviruses.
  • Sheep pox cells — pathognomonic large stellate dermal cells on histology.
  • LT/LK cells — the most susceptible isolation substrate.
  • Very hardy virus — years in dried scabs, months in wool/premises.
  • Live vaccine — 2 years' immunity vs killed's 5 months.

Poxviruses — Fowl Pox

Fowlpox Virus

Genus Avipoxvirus, family Poxviridae, Group I dsDNA — slow-spreading disease of chickens and turkeys with proliferative skin lesions/scabs and diphtheritic upper GI/respiratory lesions. OIE List B; no human risk.

Cultivation EXAM HOT

ECE via the CAM route: focal white pock lesions or generalised CAM thickening by 5–7 days; H&E-stained CAM sections show eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusions. Also grows in primary chicken embryo fibroblast/kidney/dermis cells or the permanent quail line QT-35, producing pock CPE.

Pathogenesis

Chickens and turkeys most affected, worldwide distribution. Direct contact and fomites are the main spread routes. Notably, integration of reticuloendotheliosis virus (REV) sequences into the fowlpox genome increases virulence — a documented virus-virus recombination event. Incubation 4–8 days.

Clinical signs

  • Cutaneous form (dry pox) — proliferative nodules to spherical wart-like masses on comb, wattle, other unfeathered skin
  • Diphtheritic form (wet pox) — slightly elevated white opaque nodules on oral/oesophageal/laryngeal/tracheal mucosa, rapidly enlarging into a yellowish diphtheritic membrane

Diagnosis

  • Bollinger bodies — large intracytoplasmic inclusions in infected epithelial cells, containing smaller Borrel bodies (elementary bodies); demonstrated by H&E, acridine orange, or Giemsa; Borrel bodies specifically shown by the Gimenez method on smears.
  • EM for typical poxvirus morphology (negative staining or ultrathin sections).
  • Isolation: pox lesions/scabs → ECE via CAM route, pocks by day 5–7, confirmed by FAT, AGID, or PCR (also restriction endonuclease analysis).
  • Serology: VN, AGID, passive HA, FAT, ELISA, Western blot.
  • Key differential: infectious laryngotracheitis (herpesvirus) — ILT produces intranuclear inclusions, distinguishing it from fowlpox's intracytoplasmic Bollinger bodies.

Control

No effective treatment. Live fowl/pigeon pox vaccines and fowlpox-vectored recombinant vaccines (chicken-embryo or avian-cell-culture derived) are used in endemic areas/premises with prior diagnosis, given around 6 weeks of age. Successful vaccination is confirmed 7–10 days later by checking for a "take" — a skin swelling/scab at the application site. Control combines good husbandry with targeted vaccination.

Chapter recap
  • Bollinger bodies (intracytoplasmic inclusions) contain Borrel bodies (elementary particles).
  • REV genome integration increases fowlpox virulence — a notable virus-virus interaction.
  • Dry (cutaneous) vs wet (diphtheritic) forms — skin nodules vs mucosal membranes.
  • ILT is the key differential — intranuclear (herpesvirus) vs intracytoplasmic (poxvirus) inclusions.
  • "Take" — the vaccination-success check at 7–10 days post-vaccination.
  • No human infection reported.

Poxviruses — Contagious Ecthyma (Orf)

Orf Virus

Genus Parapoxvirus, family Poxviridae — highly contagious disease of sheep and goats, occasionally zoonotic. Synonyms: contagious pustular dermatitis, scabby mouth, sore mouth.

Clinical picture EXAM HOT

Infects via skin abrasions, preferentially at cool, non-woolled sites — mouth, legs, feet, teats, poll. Sheep grazing stubble or coarse feed are more susceptible (more minor mouth/foot injuries). Lesion sequence: small reddened weeping areas → clear-fluid exudate hardens into a thick brown scab (firmly attached; forcible removal exposes a raw bleeding surface) → scabs dry and drop off after 2–3 weeks → rapid healing beneath. Most commonly at the mouth corners/muzzle; severe cases spread to lips, mouth, nostrils, occasionally around the eyes; leg lesions typically at fetlock/coronet; udder/teat lesions can cause ewes to refuse suckling (lamb weight loss) and predispose to mastitis.

Epidemiology & immunity

Spreads rapidly within a flock; lambs more susceptible than adults. Recovered animals gain lifelong immunity — hence on a property's first outbreak all ages can be affected, but in subsequent years usually only that season's lambs. Disease is normally self-limiting, clearing in 3–4 weeks without treatment.

Treatment & control

Manual scab removal delays healing and should be avoided; antibiotics are unnecessary unless secondary bacterial infection sets in. A live scabby-mouth vaccine is applied by scratching bare inner-thigh skin with a vaccine-dipped forked needle, producing a localised, non-spreading reaction; a line of pustules along the scratch a week later indicates a good response, checked in ~10 sheep/mob at 10–14 days post-vaccination for a "take".

Zoonotic risk

Humans occasionally become infected, presenting as red papules usually on the hands/arms; shearers can be affected in the axilla from holding infected feet under the arm. Accidental self-inoculation from the live vaccine is also a recognised risk — cuts/scratches should be covered, and care taken not to scratch skin with the applicator.

Chapter recap
  • Parapoxvirus — distinct genus from Capripoxvirus (sheep/goat pox).
  • Non-woolled, cool sites — mouth, legs, feet, teats, poll are the classic infection sites.
  • Self-limiting — clears in 3–4 weeks; manual scab removal delays healing.
  • Lifelong immunity after recovery — explains why only lambs are affected in subsequent years.
  • Live vaccine via scratch inoculation — forked needle, inner thigh; "take" checked at 10–14 days.
  • Zoonotic — orf in humans; shearers at particular risk; vaccine itself can cause accidental infection.

Poxviruses — Cowpox, Buffalopox, Pseudocowpox, Lumpy Skin Disease

Miscellaneous Pox Diseases

A cluster of distinct poxvirus diseases of livestock, several zoonotic, spanning Orthopoxvirus (cowpox, buffalopox), Parapoxvirus (pseudocowpox), and Capripoxvirus (lumpy skin disease).

Cowpox EXAM HOT

Genus Orthopoxvirus, restricted to Europe and adjacent former-USSR territory. Despite the name, rodents are the true reservoir; occasional spillover to cats, cattle, humans, and zoo animals (large cats, elephants). Human infection classically via infected cow teats during milking; now more often via domestic cats. Milkers/milking machines are the main spread route; insects act as mechanical vectors. Udder/teat infection is usually benign: papules → vesicles → rupture → scabs (drop off ~2 weeks); milk-yield loss follows teat soreness and secondary bacterial mastitis.

Buffalopox

Caused by buffalopox virus, an Orthopoxvirus identical/closely related to vaccinia virus. Disease of water buffalo, clinically analogous to cowpox; severe outbreaks reported from South-East Asia.

Pseudocowpox (Milker's Nodule)

Caused by pseudocowpox virus, a Parapoxvirus closely related to bovine papular stomatitis virus and to orf (contagious ecthyma) virus. Bright-red papules → vesicles → scabs → nodules on cow udder/teats over several weeks; spreads slowly through a herd but eventually reaches the whole group.

Lumpy Skin Disease

Capripoxvirus disease of cattle — fever, generalised skin/mucosal/internal-organ nodules, emaciation, lymphadenopathy, cutaneous oedema, occasional death; economically significant via lost production (especially dairy) and hide damage.

  • Fever 104–107°F (40–41.5°C) for up to 4 weeks; within 2 days, generalised 1–5 cm skin swellings/nodules appear.
  • Depression, anorexia, excess salivation, oculonasal discharge, agalactia, emaciation.
  • Nodules 1–7 cm, anywhere but especially muzzle, nares, back, legs, scrotum, perineum, eyelids, lower ear, nasal/oral mucosa, tail; hair stands erect over early lesions.
  • Painful full-thickness nodules (epidermis/dermis/subcutis, sometimes muscle); necrosis progresses to a deep scab called a "sitfast".
  • Teat lesions risk severe secondary mastitis with quarter loss.

LSD lesions

Skin nodules: congestion, haemorrhage, oedema, vasculitis, necrosis through all layers. Draining lymph nodes up to 10× enlarged with lymphoid proliferation/oedema/congestion/haemorrhage. Coalescing oral/nasal mucosal pox lesions; pharyngeal/epiglottic/tracheal lesions; synovitis/tendosynovitis with fibrinous synovial fluid; testicular and urinary-bladder pox lesions; pulmonary involvement seen as focal atelectasis/oedema rather than visible pocks; severe cases develop pleuritis with mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

Chapter recap
  • Cowpox reservoir = rodents, not cattle — a classic exam trap.
  • Buffalopox virus — essentially identical to vaccinia virus.
  • Pseudocowpox (milker's nodule) — a Parapoxvirus, related to orf and bovine papular stomatitis.
  • LSD — a Capripoxvirus disease, unlike the Ortho/Parapox others in this chapter.
  • Sitfast — the deep necrotic scab of LSD nodules.
  • 10× lymph-node enlargement in LSD.
  • Zoonotic potential — cowpox and buffalopox both can infect humans; pseudocowpox less so.

Asfarvirus — African Swine Fever

African Swine Fever Virus

Family Asfarviridae (formerly classified Iridoviridae, then unclassified) — tickborne, contagious, febrile systemic disease of swine, mortality up to 100%. First described Kenya 1921, then South Africa and Angola. OIE List A; no human infection.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Complex construction: envelope + capsid + core + nucleoprotein complex; unusually has an extracellular transcription phase in its life cycle. Enveloped but NOT ether-sensitive. Spherical, 175–215 nm; icosahedral capsid of 1892–2172 capsomers (13 nm each, hexagonal base with central cavity), no distinctive surface pattern. Genome: single linear dsDNA molecule, 170,000–190,000 nt (unsegmented).

Resistance & replication

Highly cold-resistant; inactivated at 56°C/70min or 60°C/20min, pH <3.9 or >11.5, and by NaOH (8/1000, 30min), hypochlorite (2.3% Cl, 30min), formalin (3/1000, 30min), ortho-phenylphenol (3%, 30min), iodine compounds. Remains viable long-term in blood, faeces, tissues. Replication: endocytic entry, cytoplasmic uncoating, DNA transported to nucleus for host RNA-pol-II-driven transcription, host DNA-replication machinery co-opted for genome copying, progeny DNA returns to cytoplasm for assembly, exit by budding or cell lysis. HA property: causes RBC adsorption (rosetting) in cell culture — NOT true haemagglutination.

Cultivation

Primary pig monocyte or bone-marrow cell cultures, identified by haemadsorption (RBCs cling to infected cell surfaces forming rosettes), IF, or PCR. Also cultivable in susceptible pigs.

Pathogenesis

Hosts: domestic pigs, warthogs, bush pigs, European wild boar, American wild pigs. Enzootic across most of Sub-Saharan Africa; reported in Iberian Peninsula/Sardinia; eradicated from some Central/South American countries; not reported in India (as of this text). Transmission: direct contact, garbage-feeding with infected pork, soft ticks of genus Ornithodoros (biological vector), and fomites — new herds are usually infected indirectly, then spread continues by both routes. Key risk factors: contact with tick-infested warthogs, new-pig introduction (purchase/loan/ceremonial use), infected meat from neighbouring villages, feeding raw/undercooked pork scraps, and vehicle/personnel movement during an outbreak. Incubation very short, 48–72 h. Morbidity 100% in a naive herd; mortality varies with strain virulence — highly virulent isolates 100%, less virulent 60–70%.

Clinical forms

Peracute, acute, subacute, chronic — signs appear 5–15 days post-infection.

  • Acute — high fever 41–42°C, depression, anorexia, shade-seeking, huddling, rapid breathing, skin flushing (white pigs) especially extremities/underparts, swaying gait/hind-limb weakness, whitish oculonasal discharge, dyspnoea with blood-tinged foam at nostrils, abdominal pain, vomiting, constipation or bloody diarrhoea, abortion at any gestational stage; skin flushing may deepen to bluish-purple with subcutaneous bleeding, red congested mucosae, terminal coma from haemorrhagic shock or pulmonary fluid overload; death 1–7 days post-signs; survivors of a few days may develop nervous signs
  • Subacute — fluctuating fever, depression, anorexia, painful gait, joint swelling, pneumonia signs; death can be from heart failure (preceded by throat swelling)
  • Chronic — emaciation, long dull hair, ulcerative sores over bony points, stiff/arthritic gait; survival from weeks to months

Lesions

  • Blood-stained fluid in chest/abdominal cavities.
  • Widespread haemorrhage over organ/body surfaces with congestion.
  • Enlarged blood-filled spleen and lymph nodes, resembling blood clots.
  • Heavy, shiny lungs with prominent interlobular divisions, oozing moisture/froth on cutting; trachea filled with blood-stained froth.
  • Pinpoint renal-surface haemorrhages; gastric haemorrhage/ulceration; congested intestines with bloody contents.

Diagnosis

  • Heparinised/clotted blood or serum; submandibular/inguinal lymph node, tonsil, spleen, gastrohepatic lymph node, lung, liver, kidney, bone marrow.
  • Isolation: pig monocyte/bone-marrow cultures or susceptible pigs; confirmed by haemadsorption rosetting, IF, or PCR.
  • Direct tissue detection: IF, immunoperoxidase staining, PCR.
  • Serology: ELISA is the most commonly used test; also indirect IF, immunoblotting, counter-immunoelectrophoresis. Antibody may be undetectable in acute fatal cases — serology is mainly for survivors and endemicity surveys.
  • Differentials: classical swine fever (clinically almost indistinguishable — lab confirmation essential), swine erysipelas, salmonellosis, pasteurellosis.

Control

No effective treatment; no vaccine exists. OIE strategy: free countries — strict import policy, proper disposal of aircraft/ship waste food from infected countries, efficient garbage sterilisation. Outbreaks — rapid slaughter and proper cadaver/litter disposal, thorough cleaning/disinfection, infected-zone designation with movement control, up-/down-stream epidemiological trace-back, and surveillance of the infected zone and surrounding area. Endemic countries — prevent pig–soft-tick vector contact (Africa), i.e. stop pigs wandering into tick-infested areas.

Chapter recap
  • Asfarviridae — unique large dsDNA virus with an extracellular transcription phase.
  • Ornithodoros soft ticks — the biological vector, central to the African sylvatic cycle.
  • Haemadsorption rosetting — NOT true HA, the key cell-culture identification method.
  • Clinically indistinguishable from CSF — laboratory diagnosis is essential.
  • No vaccine, no treatment — control is entirely biosecurity/eradication-based.
  • ELISA — the standard serological test, though useless in peracute/acute fatal cases.
  • No human infection reported.

Herpesviridae — General Characters & Pseudorabies (Aujeszky's Disease)

Herpesviridae

Group I dsDNA, ~100 members in three subfamilies: Alphaherpesvirinae, Betaherpesvirinae, Gammaherpesvirinae — "herpein" (Greek) = "to creep", reflecting the chronic/latent/recurrent infections these viruses cause. Animal diseases include pseudorabies, Marek's disease, IBR, ILT.

Family morphology & replication EXAM HOT

Enveloped (ether-sensitive), slightly pleomorphic, spherical, 120–200 nm; evenly dispersed surface spikes; icosahedral symmetry; tegument (protein-filled region between envelope and capsid); 162 capsomers/nucleocapsid. Core: DNA wrapped on a fibrillar spool anchored to the capsid shell; linear dsDNA, 120,000–220,000 nt, with unique long (UL), unique short (US), and terminal repeat regions. Replication is nuclear: glycoprotein-mediated fusion entry → capsid travels cytoskeleton to nuclear pore → DNA circularises in the nucleus → host RNA-pol-II transcription → cytoplasmic translation → capsid assembly/DNA packaging in the nucleus → primary envelopment by budding through the nuclear membrane → accumulation in the ER → release by exocytosis.

Pseudorabies (Aujeszky's Disease)

Pseudorabies Virus

Genus Varicellovirus (Alphaherpesvirinae) — acute, often-fatal multispecies disease; pigs are the natural host, with sporadic infection in cattle, sheep, goats, horses, dogs, cats, foxes, rodents. Named for its rabies-mimicking nervous signs plus intense itching/self-mutilation. Synonyms: Mad itch, infectious bulbar paralysis. OIE List B; no human infection.

Resistance & cultivation

Comparatively stable — survives long in pulmonary fluid and refrigerated; withstands 55–60°C/30–50min, 70°C/10min, 80°C/3min; sensitive to 5% phenol, 1% NaOH; destroyed in putrefied carcasses. No HA property. Grown via CAM route in eggs (pocks, embryo death 3–5 days), PK-15 cells (most sensitive; CPE 24–72h: refringent-cell accumulation, syncytia, cell-sheet detachment), or rabbits (SC/intracerebellar — characteristic CNS signs + intense pruritus, the preferred lab animal).

Pathogenesis & transmission

Spreads mainly by direct pig-pig contact (nasal discharge/saliva, ingestion/inhalation); also contaminated water/bedding/fomites, semen, vaginal secretions, colostrum, contaminated needles/instruments, and transplacental infection. Recovered pigs become carriers, infecting other pigs or cattle. Dogs/cats infect via contact with pigs or eating infected pork; wind-borne spread up to 2 km; rats and wild pigs are reservoirs; live-pig movement is the most likely route into a new area. Incubation 3–7 days; morbidity variable, mortality 20–100%.

Clinical signs by species

  • Piglets <2 weeks — up to 100% mortality; congenital cases die within 2 days with violent shaking/shivering; postnatal infections usually fatal by day 5
  • Pigs <3 weeks — sudden death with few signs; fever >105°F, dullness, anorexia, vomiting, weakness, incoordination, convulsions if diarrhoea occurs
  • Pigs >3 weeks — fever, anorexia, listlessness, laboured breathing, salivation, trembling, marked hind-limb incoordination ("sit like dogs"), sneezing/nasal discharge/nose-rubbing/coughing, terminal convulsions
  • Adult pigs — fever + respiratory signs; occasional nervous signs/itching/rare blindness; sows: early pregnancy → return to heat (foetal resorption), mid-pregnancy → mummified abortion, late pregnancy → abortion or weak/shaker/stillborn pigs
  • Cattle & sheep — ALWAYS FATAL: intense localised skin itching, licking, self-mutilation, incoordination, prostration, clonic convulsions, teeth grinding, rapid shallow breathing, cardiac irregularity; death ~2 days after signs
  • Dogs & cats — intense pruritus, self-mutilation, whimpering/howling, pharyngeal paralysis, salivation, convulsions; death in 1–2 days

Lesions

Often minimal/absent grossly. Congested pneumonic lungs; serous/fibrinous rhinitis; necrotic tonsillitis; yellow-white necrotic hepatic/splenic foci (2–3 mm); necrotic placentitis/endometritis; in aborted piglets, necrotic lung/liver/spleen/tonsil lesions.

Diagnosis

  • Live: oro-pharyngeal fluid, nasal swabs, tonsil biopsy. Dead: brain, tonsil. Trigeminal ganglion is the most consistent site for isolating latent virus (though latent virus is hard to culture — a negative isolation doesn't rule out PR).
  • Isolation: PK-15 cells, ECE (CAM), rabbits.
  • PCR identifies PRV genome directly from secretions/tissue.
  • VNT and ELISA (both OIE trade-approved) detect antibody from ~day 7, persisting years — but neither distinguishes natural infection from vaccination.
  • Differentials: Teschen disease, rabies, CSF, ASF, haemagglutinating encephalomyelitis, erysipelas, Nipah virus, streptococcal meningoencephalitis, hypoglycaemia, arsenic/mercury/salt poisoning, other abortion causes, swine influenza, congenital tremor.

Control

No effective treatment. Attenuated and inactivated vaccines reduce clinical disease and viral shedding but do NOT prevent latent infection — vaccinated animals can still transmit virus. Gene-deleted (marker) vaccines exist. Eradication: strict biosecurity, PR-free sourcing, movement control, excluding dogs/cats/rodents from pig housing, quarantine on outbreak, deep burial/incineration of carcasses, slaughter-only sale of recovered pigs, and "test and removal" or offspring segregation for herd cleanup.

Chapter recap
  • Herpes = "to creep" — reflects chronic/latent/recurrent infection pattern.
  • Nuclear replication, ER accumulation, exocytic release — distinctive herpesvirus life cycle.
  • Trigeminal ganglion — key latency site for pseudorabies.
  • Always fatal in cattle/sheep — unlike variable mortality in pigs.
  • Mad itch — intense pruritus/self-mutilation across non-porcine hosts.
  • Vaccines don't prevent latency — vaccinated pigs remain potential shedders.
  • No human infection from PRV.

Herpesvirus — Marek's Disease

Marek's Disease Virus

Genus Mardivirus (Alphaherpesvirinae), species Gallid herpesvirus 2 — lymphomatous/neuropathic disease of domestic fowl, one of poultry's most economically important viral diseases. Synonyms: neural lymphomatosis, fowl paralysis. OIE List B; no human infection.

Resistance & serotypes EXAM HOT

Strictly cell-associated virus, preserved at –60°C, infective across pH 5.5–8.4; no HA property.

Cultivation

Chicken kidney or duck embryo fibroblast cultures — plaque CPE at 3–5 days, enumerable by 7–10 days, with intranuclear inclusions. Also pock lesions in ECE via CAM route; SPF chickens used for cultivation.

Pathogenesis

Chickens, quails, turkeys highly susceptible; young birds most susceptible; most deaths occur 10–24 weeks of age. Worldwide. Highly infectious, spreading rapidly through unvaccinated flocks; healthy carrier birds spread it; virus survives up to 8 months in the environment. Replication and release occur in feather-follicle epithelium, shed copiously in dust/dander — infection is via respiratory inhalation of contaminated dust. Day 3–5 post-infection, B lymphocytes of bursa/spleen/thymus become infected, followed by CD4+ T-lymphocyte infection, latency, and cell-associated viraemia spreading the virus throughout the host; secondary cytolytic feather-follicle infection sheds cell-free virus in dander. Latently infected T cells are transformed, producing visceral lymphomatosis (mainly CD4+, possibly CD8+ T cells). No vertical (egg) transmission. Morbidity very high; mortality usually up to 15% over weeks-months, but outbreaks can reach 70%.

Clinical forms

  • Nervous (classical) form — sciatic and other peripheral nerve involvement; rapid-onset paralysis, unable to stand, wasting from inability to eat/drink; occasionally wings/neck involved; iris involvement can cause blindness
  • Visceral (acute) form — greyish-white tumours in ovary, liver, spleen, kidney, heart, other organs (or diffuse organ swelling without discrete tumours); depression, paralysis, anorexia, weight loss, anaemia (pale comb), dehydration (shrunken comb), diarrhoea; sudden death without prior signs is common
  • Ocular form — iridocyclitis ("grey eye"), pupil distortion, most common in 16–18-week birds

Lesions & nerve pathology

Classical form: enlarged peripheral nerves (brachial/sciatic plexus, coeliac plexus, abdominal vagus, intercostals), 2–3× normal thickness, loss of normal cross-striation/sheen, grey/yellow discolouration, sometimes oedema; small soft grey ovarian lymphomas (occasionally lung/kidney/heart/liver). Visceral form: grey-white organ tumours, feather-follicle and skeletal-muscle lymphomas, concurrent enlarged peripheral nerves; liver enlargement mild in young birds, marked in adults (mimicking lymphoid leucosis grossly).

Nerve lesion classification: Type A — proliferative infiltration by lymphoblasts/lymphocytes/macrophages, neoplastic; Type B — inflammatory, interneuritic oedema, small lymphocytes/plasma cells, Schwann-cell proliferation; Type C — regressive, light small-lymphocyte scattering, often subclinical. A/B-type lesions cause the demyelination responsible for clinical paralysis. MATSA (Marek's disease tumour-associated surface antigen) marks activated T-cell antigens on MD tumour cells, used to differentiate MD from lymphoid leucosis.

Diagnosis

  • Buffy-coat cells (heparinised blood), lymphoma/spleen cell suspensions — virus is cell-associated.
  • Isolation: chicken kidney or duck embryo fibroblast cultures (chicken embryo fibroblasts less sensitive for primary isolation); plaques at 3–5 days, counted 7–10 days; intranuclear inclusions. SPF chickens also used.
  • Cell-free virus isolable from feather follicles.
  • PCR identifies virus and differentiates serotype directly from clinical material.
  • MATSA demonstrated by indirect FAT.
  • Serology: AGID (most commonly used — 1% agar/PBS/8% NaCl slides, 37°C/24h humid incubation, antigen from disrupted MDV-infected cells or feather-tip/skin extract), indirect FAT, VNT, ELISA.
  • Differentials: lymphoid leucosis, reticuloendotheliosis.

Control

No effective treatment. Vaccination: cell-associated live virus, or cell-free herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT) — given in-ovo at day 17–18 of incubation or SC at hatch. HVT does NOT spread horizontally, so missed birds remain fully susceptible and can amplify/spread MDV. Recombinant vaccines exist but aren't commercially standard. Additional control: management (disinfection, all-in/all-out, age segregation, genetically resistant stock), isolating vaccinated chicks for their first 2 weeks (to let immunity develop before challenge, away from older birds' infected dust/fluff), and good nutrition/parasite-freedom to maximise flock resistance.

Chapter recap
  • Gallid herpesvirus 2 — the oncogenic serotype 1; serotypes 2/3 are non-oncogenic vaccine strains.
  • Feather-follicle epithelium — site of cell-free virus production/shedding in dust and dander.
  • No vertical transmission — not spread through the egg.
  • Type A/B/C nerve lesions — proliferative/neoplastic, inflammatory, regressive respectively.
  • MATSA — differentiates MD tumours from lymphoid leucosis.
  • AGID — the standard serological antibody test.
  • HVT vaccine doesn't spread horizontally — every bird needs individual vaccination.

Adenoviridae — Egg Drop Syndrome & Infectious Canine Hepatitis

Adenoviridae

Group I dsDNA, four genera: Ataadenovirus, Aviadenovirus, Mastadenovirus, Siadenovirus. Widespread across birds/mammals/humans; cause latent lymphoid infection, some oncogenic, frequent cause of "colds" (URT infection).

Family morphology & replication EXAM HOT

Non-enveloped (ether-resistant), 60–90 nm, icosahedral, single-layer capsid of 252 capsomers: 240 hexons + 12 pentons at the vertices. Distinct fibre filaments project from each vertex, mediating receptor binding. Pentons have toxin-like activity — purified pentons alone cause CPE, a unique property. Linear dsDNA, 35,800–36,200 nt. Replication: fibre-receptor interaction, then penton-mediated phagocytic entry; toxic penton activity ruptures the phagocytic vacuole, releasing virus to migrate to the nucleus; strand-displacement genome replication; assembly in nucleus; released by cell disintegration (not budding). Adenoviruses can act as helpers for dependent/satellite viruses.

Egg Drop Syndrome (EDS)

Duck Adenovirus 1

Genus Ataadenovirus — production of soft-shelled/shell-less eggs in otherwise healthy layers/breeders. Natural hosts ducks and geese; disease also seen in chickens (especially heavy broiler-breeders, brown-egg layers) and quail. Not OIE-listed; no human risk.

Agglutinates chicken RBCs, no elution. Three genotypes: classical EDS, UK-duck-associated, Australian. Grows in embryonated duck/goose eggs or duck/goose cell culture (NOT in ECE or mammalian cells); replicates well in chick kidney/embryo-liver cells, less well in CEF.

EDS transmission & pathogenesis

Worldwide except USA. Vertical (egg) transmission to a few flock members, who carry latent virus until lay, then shed and infect flockmates; also faecal shedding, contaminated water/fomites, wild-bird water contamination, infected litter, contaminated crates/trucks/needles. After infection: low-titre nasal replication → viraemia → lymphoid replication → ~8 days of massive oviduct replication, especially the pouch shell-gland region; eggs laid day 8–18 post-infection contain virus both inside and out. Incubation 8–18 days (experimental). Morbidity variable, mortality minimal.

Three forms: classical, endemic, sporadic. Failure to reach peak production or a production drop with inferior shell quality (brown eggs lose colour); birds often eat shell-less eggs (search for membranes to detect this); may show anaemia, transient diarrhoea, reduced intake — but NO increased mortality, and no effect on fertility/hatchability of settable eggs. Lesion: pouch shell-gland epithelial intranuclear inclusions, degeneration, replacement by squamous/cuboidal/undifferentiated cells, moderate-severe mucosal inflammation.

EDS diagnosis & control

  • Abnormal eggs, pouch shell glands → embryonated duck eggs, duck/chick-embryo liver cultures, or antibody-free chickens.
  • HI, ELISA, SNT, double immunodiffusion; PCR.
  • Differentials: Newcastle disease, avian influenza, infectious bronchitis.
  • Inactivated oil-adjuvant vaccine at 14–18 weeks (often combined with ND vaccine) — reduces but doesn't prevent shedding. Endemic form controlled by disinfecting reusable plastic Keyes egg trays; sporadic form by separating chickens from waterfowl and chlorinating suspect water.

Infectious Canine Hepatitis (Rubarth's Disease)

Canine Adenovirus 1 (CAV-1)

Genus Mastadenovirus — contagious disease of dogs (also foxes, wolves, coyotes, bears), ranging mild fever to severe depression/leukopenia/prolonged bleeding. Not OIE-listed; no human risk. Antigenically related to CAV-2 (causes infectious tracheobronchitis).

Resistant to lipid solvents, survives outside the host for months; sensitive to 1–3% sodium hypochlorite. No HA property. Grown in dog/pig/ferret kidney cell culture (intranuclear inclusion CPE), ECE, and dogs/ferrets as lab animals.

ICH pathogenesis

Worldwide. Recovered dogs shed virus in urine for up to 6 months; transmission via ingestion of urine/faeces/saliva. Initial replication in tonsillar crypts/Peyer's patches → viraemia → endothelial infection across many tissues, targeting liver, kidney, spleen, lung. Incubation 4–9 days. Mortality high in young dogs.

ICH clinical signs & lesions

Biphasic fever >104°F (1–6 days), persistent leukopenia (severity correlates with illness severity), apathy, anorexia, thirst, conjunctivitis, oculonasal discharge, occasional abdominal pain/vomiting, oral hyperaemia/petechiae, tonsillar enlargement, subcutaneous head/neck/trunk oedema. Coagulopathy: bleeding around deciduous teeth, spontaneous haematomas; severe cases show terminal convulsions with brain-stem haemorrhage. 7–10 days after recovery, bilateral corneal opacity ("blue eye") develops in many dogs, usually resolving spontaneously — sometimes the only sign in mild disease. Chronic hepatitis develops in dogs with low passive antibody at exposure; concurrent CAV-1/distemper infection is recognised. Lesions: hepatocellular necrosis (liver normal or swollen), oedematous thickened gallbladder wall, grey-white renal cortical foci, and characteristic "paintbrush" haemorrhages on gastric serosa, lymph nodes, thymus, pancreas, subcutaneous tissue (from intestinal endothelial damage).

ICH diagnosis & control

  • Field signs overlap with canine distemper; sudden onset with severe bleeding suggests ICH — correlate with blood picture.
  • Histopathology: intranuclear hepatic inclusions.
  • Isolation: dog/ferret kidney cell culture, confirmed by inclusion demonstration, IF, PCR.
  • Differentials: canine distemper, CAV-2 tracheobronchitis.
  • No specific antiviral; supportive blood transfusion and IV 5% dextrose saline in severe cases.
  • MLV vaccines (attenuated CAV-1 or CAV-2) given alongside distemper vaccination, annual boosters. Attenuated CAV-1 can itself cause corneal opacity and urine shedding; CAV-2 gives cross-protection against CAV-1 without causing corneal opacity/uveitis and without urine shedding — hence CAV-2 is the preferred modern vaccine strain.
Chapter recap
  • Toxic pentons — a unique adenoviral property, purified pentons alone cause CPE.
  • EDS = vertical transmission — latent carriers shed only once they reach lay.
  • EDS causes no mortality and no fertility/hatchability impact — purely an egg-quality/production issue.
  • CAV-1 causes ICH; CAV-2 causes tracheobronchitis — antigenically related, cross-protective.
  • "Blue eye" — corneal opacity 7–10 days post-recovery, ICH's signature sequela.
  • Paintbrush haemorrhages — classic ICH necropsy finding.
  • CAV-2 vaccine preferred — avoids the corneal-opacity/urine-shedding side effects of attenuated CAV-1.

Parvoviridae — Canine Parvovirus

Parvoviridae

Group II ssDNA, among the smallest/simplest eukaryotic viruses — two functional classes: defective (helper-virus-dependent) and autonomous (replication-competent). Two subfamilies (Parvovirinae, Densovirinae), subdivided into 5 and 4 genera respectively.

Family morphology & replication EXAM HOT

Non-enveloped (ether-resistant), icosahedral, 18–26 nm — among the smallest DNA virus particles; composition is 50% protein/50% DNA. 60-capsomer capsid, rough surface with small distinct spikes; three capsid proteins VP1–3, VP2 is essential for virulence. Genome: single linear ssDNA (negative-sense, or a mix of ± strands depending on species), only 5000 nt, with palindromic sequences at both termini. Replication is entirely nuclear and host-dependent (poorly understood strategy): endocytic entry → uncoating → nuclear DNA transcription/replication (host DNA polymerase required) → cytoplasmic translation and capsid assembly → nuclear DNA packaging → release by cell lysis.

Canine Parvovirus (CPV-2)

Canine Parvovirus 2

Genus Parvovirus — agglutinates porcine/Rhesus-monkey/human-O RBCs. Not OIE-listed; no human risk. Closely related to feline panleukopenia, mink enteritis, and raccoon enteritis viruses; CPV differs from FPLV by just two amino acids in capsid protein VP2, believed to have arisen by FPLV mutation in the late 1970s enabling canine replication. Vaccine strain: Cornell 780916. Current field variants: CPV-2a, 2b, 2c.

Resistance & cultivation

Very environmentally stable — wide pH/temperature tolerance, resistant to many disinfectants, survives months in contaminated environments. Grown in primary canine cells or established lines (Canine A72, Crandell feline kidney) — intranuclear inclusion CPE; does NOT grow in ECE. Has strong affinity for rapidly dividing cells.

Pathogenesis

Dogs are the natural host; Rottweiler, American Pit Bull Terrier, Doberman Pinscher, German Shepherd at increased risk; Toy Poodle/Cocker Spaniel at decreased risk. Worldwide. Direct contact and faecal-contaminated fomites; shed in faeces up to 3 weeks; recovered dogs can be periodic carriers. Oropharyngeal lymphoid replication → viraemia → attacks rapidly dividing cells: bone marrow (neutropenia), lymphopoietic tissue (lymphopenia), and jejunal/ileal crypt epithelium (villous collapse, epithelial necrosis, haemorrhagic diarrhoea) — denuded mucosa allows secondary bacterial invasion (Clostridium perfringens, E. coli). Incubation 3–8 days; morbidity variable, mortality 16–35%.

Clinical forms

  • Myocarditis — young pups, especially early neonatal period; myocardial necrosis causing acute cardiopulmonary failure (pulmonary oedema, cyanosis, collapse) or progressive myocardial scarring/cardiac insufficiency
  • Gastroenteritis — most common, pups 6–20 weeks (esp. young male dogs <1 year); acute lethargy, anorexia, fever, vomiting, leukopenia, loose diarrhoea ± mucus/blood

Often asymptomatic; stress and concurrent opportunistic enteric infection trigger/intensify disease; heavy/prolonged viral exposure raises risk. Most enteric-form dogs recover in days with supportive care; some die within hours of onset. Also associated with birth defects and infertility; subclinical infection common, especially in older dogs.

Lesions & diagnosis

Characteristic lesion: damage to microvilli and Crypts of Lieberkühn, allowing enteric bacterial bloodstream entry. Field diagnosis rests on clinical signs + leukopenia/lymphopenia, confirmed by testing.

  • Intestinal mucosal biopsy — microvillus damage gives conclusive evidence.
  • Faecal parvo ELISA — conclusive in unvaccinated dogs (less reliable post-vaccination).
  • Faecal virus detection via IEM and HA; PCR for nucleic acid.
  • Serology (HI, SNT) assesses protective status — HI titre ≥1:80 and SN titre ≥1:20 are considered protective.

Treatment & control

No specific antiviral therapy — supportive fluid-balance restoration is the mainstay. Live attenuated CPV vaccines induce more effective immunity than inactivated; modern high-titre vaccines can overcome the maternal-antibody "window of susceptibility" (antibody too low to protect but high enough to block active immunisation). Recommended schedule: three doses at 6, 9, and 12 weeks. Eradication: isolate affected dogs; the virus survives weeks in contaminated cages/kennels, requiring thorough disinfection (e.g. sodium hypochlorite) before admitting uninfected dogs; disinfect hands/clothing/bowls; keep pups isolated from adult dogs returning from shows/field trials.

Chapter recap
  • Smallest DNA virus — 18–26 nm, 5000-nt genome.
  • 2 amino-acid difference from FPLV — the origin of CPV in the late 1970s.
  • Rapidly dividing cells — bone marrow, lymphoid tissue, gut crypt epithelium are the targets.
  • Myocarditis (neonatal) vs gastroenteritis (6–20 wk) — the two clinical forms.
  • Microvilli/Crypts of Lieberkühn damage — the pathognomonic lesion.
  • Protective titres — HI ≥1:80, SN ≥1:20.
  • Maternal-antibody window — high-titre vaccines close the vulnerable gap; 3-dose schedule (6, 9, 12 wk).

Papillomaviridae & Polyomaviridae

Papovaviruses

An older umbrella term for two now-separate families of small DNA viruses causing benign epithelial growths: Papillomaviridae (papillomas/warts) and Polyomaviridae (polyomas). Widespread across birds and mammals.

Papillomaviridae morphology EXAM HOT

Small, non-enveloped, icosahedral, 52–55 nm, 72 capsomers. Circular dsDNA genome, ~8 kbp, complexed with cellular histones into a chromatin-like structure. Many genera (alpha through pi — 15 in total). Key animal-pathogenic species: bovine papillomavirus 3 & 5, equine papillomavirus 1, canine oral papillomavirus.

Replication

Infects basal dermal-layer cells, where early gene expression occurs; late gene expression (structural proteins, vegetative DNA synthesis) is restricted to terminally differentiated epidermal cells — linking viral gene expression tightly to host cell differentiation state.

Pathogenesis

Typical outcome is a benign cellular outgrowth — a wart/papilloma — anywhere on/in the body. Skin warts: flat (superficial) vs plantar (deep). Genital warts (condylomas) occur in the genital tract, sexually transmitted. Warts may persist for years or regress spontaneously via a CTL immune response; UV-exposed skin warts carry an enhanced risk of progression to invasive squamous cell carcinoma. Treatment: topical caustics or freezing; surgical removal is more reliable and required for internal warts (e.g. laryngeal).

Polyomaviridae

Infect a wide range of vertebrates. Non-enveloped virions; circular dsDNA genome, ~5 kbp (smaller than papillomavirus genomes). Key example: SV40 (Simian vacuolating virus 40).

Chapter recap
  • "Papova" = an obsolete combined term; now split into Papillomaviridae and Polyomaviridae.
  • Papillomavirus genome ~8 kbp vs polyomavirus ~5 kbp — key size distinction.
  • Differentiation-linked gene expression — late genes only in terminally differentiated epidermal cells.
  • Flat vs plantar warts — superficial vs deep skin lesions.
  • UV exposure raises squamous cell carcinoma risk in warts.
  • SV40 — the classic polyomavirus example.

Circoviridae — Chicken Infectious Anaemia

Chicken Anaemia Virus

Family Circoviridae, genus Gyrovirus — worldwide disease infecting chickens of all ages, but clinical signs only in young chicks. Not zoonotic.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Very small (19–24 nm), spherical, non-enveloped, icosahedral capsid, no tail; has an extracellular phase in its life cycle. Genome: single circular negative-sense ssDNA, only 2298–2319 nt, encoding both structural and non-structural proteins.

Resistance

Very resistant — withstands pH 3 and chloroform; NOT inactivated by 70°C/1h or 80°C/5min; resistant to lipid solvents and to 2h/37°C exposure to 5% quaternary ammonium compounds, amphoteric soap, or orthodichlorobenzene disinfectants.

Pathogenesis

Long field incubation period. All isolates cause anaemia and/or aplastic bone marrow — by day 8 post-infection, haematocrit, thrombocyte, and RBC/WBC counts drop and clotting time prolongs; hematological recovery occurs by day 28–36. Experimentally, virus is detectable within a day in brain, liver, spleen, bursa, bone marrow, rectal contents, serum. Latent infection in SPF flocks — virus persists in ovary/oviduct/testis/spleen without seroconversion until birds come into production. Transmission is both vertical and horizontal.

Clinical signs

Signs mainly in birds <2 weeks; most outbreaks in broilers, then replacement pullets. Outbreaks in older birds are linked to co-infection with other immunosuppressive agents (Marek's, IBD virus). Depression, huddling under heat, poor development for age, anaemia. CAV replicates in lymphopoietic organs, depleting lymphocytes — this enhances the effect of concurrent Marek's disease or IBD virus infection, and commonly reduces post-vaccination Newcastle disease antibody development.

Lesions

Characteristic "blue wing disease" — focal ecchymotic skin haemorrhages (mostly wings) that turn blue, may rupture releasing serosanguineous exudate prone to secondary bacterial infection and gangrenous dermatitis. Mortality peaks 5–6 days after signs appear, declining to normal by 5–6 days later. Also thymic and bone-marrow atrophy.

Diagnosis

Clinical signs and PM findings alone are inconclusive. Best serological approach: test serum at the time of signs and again 2–3 weeks later; retrospective breeder-serum testing helps investigate suspected vertical transmission.

Control

No effective treatment. Live vaccine, strain 26P4, given at 8 weeks of age or up to 4 weeks before onset of lay (IM, SC, or wing-web); control is best achieved through improved biosecurity plus breeder vaccination. Not zoonotic.

Chapter recap
  • Gyrovirus, Circoviridae — one of the smallest DNA viruses in veterinary virology (~2.3 kb).
  • Blue wing disease — the pathognomonic wing ecchymotic haemorrhage lesion.
  • Immunosuppressive synergy — worsens Marek's and IBD, blunts ND vaccine response.
  • Latent SPF-flock persistence — without seroconversion, until the birds come into lay.
  • Highly disinfectant-resistant — survives standard heat and chemical treatments.
  • 26P4 live vaccine — the standard control strain.
  • Not zoonotic.

Prion Diseases — BSE & Scrapie

Prions

Transmissible particles entirely lacking nucleic acid, composed of a misfolded protein, PrPSc. Normal cellular prion protein (PrPC) is converted to the pathogenic PrPSc conformation through a post-translational process (no genetic replication involved). The two major veterinary prion diseases are BSE and scrapie.

Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy EXAM HOT

"Mad cow disease" — a fatal neurodegenerative disease of cattle causing spongy degeneration of brain and spinal cord. Long incubation, ~4 years; peak clinical onset at 4–5 years of age; all breeds equally susceptible. Most scientists believe BSE can transmit to humans who consume the brain/spinal cord of infected carcasses — the resulting human disease is Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (variant CJD).

Scrapie

A fatal degenerative disease of the sheep/goat nervous system, named for affected animals' compulsive fleece-scraping against rocks/trees/fences. Presents as severe pruritus, lip-smacking, abnormal gait, convulsive collapse. Infectious and transmissible between animals; persists within flocks and can also arise spontaneously in flocks with no prior history of disease. The precise transmission mechanism and disease biology remain poorly understood — an active research area.

Chapter recap
  • PrPSc — the misfolded, infectious prion protein; no nucleic acid involved.
  • BSE — ~4-year incubation, peak onset 4–5 years, spongy brain/cord degeneration.
  • Variant CJD — the human disease linked to eating BSE-infected brain/spinal cord.
  • Scrapie — named for the compulsive fleece-scraping sign; pruritus, gait abnormality, collapse.
  • Scrapie can arise spontaneously — not solely dependent on prior flock exposure.
  • Transmission biology still poorly understood for both diseases.
