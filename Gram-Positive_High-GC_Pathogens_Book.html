Chapter 02

Streptococcus

Streptococcus

Gram-positive, catalase-negative cocci that divide in one plane and fail to separate fully, forming chains; family Streptococcaceae, order Lactobacillales.

History

  • 1873Rivolta described chain-forming organisms in pus from equine strangles.
  • 1878–79Pasteur recognised the organism as a pus-forming agent.
  • 1903Hugo Schottmüller used blood agar to differentiate haemolysis types.
  • 1919Brown classified alpha/beta/gamma haemolysis.
  • 1928/1933Rebecca Lancefield devised serological (C-antigen) grouping — 19 groups, A–U (no I, J).

Morphology & classification EXAM HOT

Spherical/ovoid cocci ~1 μm, facultative anaerobes, oxidase-negative, non-spore-forming, non-motile (except some enterococci). Capsule of hyaluronic acid occurs in S. pyogenes and some group-C/B/D strains.

  • Haemolysis (Brown, 1919)
    • α — partial, greenish (viridans, commensal, e.g. S. pneumoniae)
    • β — complete, clear zone (most pathogens)
    • γ — non-haemolytic (enterococci, S. faecalis)
  • Lancefield groups — key veterinary species
    • Grp B — S. agalactiae — chronic bovine mastitis
    • Grp C — S. dysgalactiae (acute mastitis, cattle), S. equi subsp. equi (strangles, horse), S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus (mastitis, metritis, multi-species septicaemia)
    • Grp C/D/E/P/V — S. uberis — mastitis
    • Ungrouped — S. suis — porcine meningitis, arthritis

Cultural & biochemical

37 °Coptimum growth temp
19Lancefield groups (A–U)
54 °C/30 minheat inactivation

Grows best on blood/serum-enriched media; Edward's medium (blood agar + crystal violet + aesculin) differentiates species by aesculin hydrolysis and colony colour. S. pneumoniae gives flat, draughtsman colonies at 48–72 h. Catalase/oxidase negative; ferments sugars with acid, no gas; gelatin not liquefied; indole negative; insoluble in 10% bile (unlike pneumococci).

Toxins & virulence factors

  • Streptolysin O — oxygen-labile, cardiotoxic; basis of the ASO titre.
  • Streptolysin S — oxygen-stable, causes surface β-haemolysis, nephrotoxic.
  • Erythrogenic (Dick) toxin — 4 types; intradermal test in rats = Dick test/Schultz-Charlton reaction.
  • Streptokinase (fibrinolysin), streptodornase (DNase, 4 types A–D), hyaluronidase, proteinase, neuraminidase, serum opacity factor.
  • M, T, R surface proteins — type-specific; M protein inhibits phagocytosis (main virulence factor).
Key hosts/disease
  • Cattle — mastitis (S. agalactiae, S. dysgalactiae, S. zooepidemicus, S. uberis)
  • Horse — strangles, purpura haemorrhagica, bastard strangles (S. equi)
  • Pig — S. suis meningitis/arthritis/endocarditis
  • Dog — S. canis neonatal septicaemia
  • Poultry — S. gallinarum septicaemia
Diagnosis
  • CAMP test — S. agalactiae enhances staphylococcal β-toxin lysis
  • Bile solubility — pneumococci autolyse in 10% Na deoxycholate
  • Optochin test — pneumococci inhibited, S. viridans not
Common mistake

Peptostreptococcus indolicus, an anaerobic streptococcus, is a co-pathogen of summer mastitis with Arcanobacterium pyogenes — it is not a Lancefield-groupable aerobic streptococcus.

Chapter recap
  • Lancefield 1933 — C-carbohydrate antigen grouping, 19 groups A–U.
  • Brown 1919 — α/β/γ haemolysis on horse-blood meat-infusion agar.
  • S. agalactiae (grp B) — chronic bovine mastitis; CAMP test positive.
  • S. equi subsp. equi — strangles; complication = purpura haemorrhagica.
  • Toxins — Streptolysin O (O2-labile), Streptolysin S (O2-stable, surface β-haemolysis).
  • M protein — chief anti-phagocytic virulence factor of S. pyogenes.
  • Control — penicillin remains first line; vaccines of limited value except equine strangles.
Chapter 03

Staphylococcus

Staphylococcus

Gram-positive cocci in grape-like clusters, family Staphylococcaceae; first described by Sir Alexander Ogston (1880) from infected tissue (Greek staphyle = bunch of grapes).

Morphology & culture EXAM HOT

Spherical cells 0.8–1 μm; non-motile, non-acid-fast, no flagella. Grows at 35–37 °C; tolerant of 7–10% NaCl (Mannitol Salt Agar is selective). Colonies round, smooth, golden/white; broth shows uniform turbidity with powdery deposit.

7–10%NaCl tolerance
60 °C/30 minheat kill point
1–2 minslide coagulase clumping time

Pigments & haemolysis

  • Old pigment-based naming (now obsolete)
    • S. aureus — golden/orange
    • S. albus — white
    • S. citreus — lemon yellow

On sheep/rabbit blood agar, hot-cold lysis occurs: incomplete haemolysis at 37 °C completes on chilling to 4 °C.

Antigens & toxins

  • Group antigen: ribitol teichoic acid (S. aureus) vs glycerol teichoic acid (S. intermedius); Protein A only in S. aureus.
  • Haemolysins α, β, δ, ε — α-toxin is the major toxin in gangrenous mastitis, necrotising and lethal.
  • Leukotoxin (a, d components) — leukocidal, aids spread.
  • Enterotoxin — heat-stable, survives 100 °C/80 min; causes human food poisoning; rarely from animal strains.
  • Exfoliative/dermonecrotoxin — skin exfoliation.
  • Toxic shock syndrome toxin (TSST) — excess lymphokine release.

Worked test

Coagulase tube test: culture + rabbit plasma, incubate 37 °C/24 h — clot formation = coagulase-positive staphylococci (CPS), the significant pathogens.

Which staphylococcal test differentiates bound clumping factor from free coagulase?

Slide test detects bound coagulase (clumping in 1–2 min); tube test detects free coagulase (clot at 37 °C/24 h).

Chapter recap
  • Ogston, 1880 — named Staphylococcus from infected tissue.
  • Hot-cold lysis — incomplete haemolysis at 37 °C, complete at 4 °C.
  • CPS vs CNS — coagulase-positive strains are the significant pathogens.
  • Alpha toxin — key toxin in gangrenous bovine mastitis.
  • S. hyicus — exudative epidermitis (greasy pig disease) in piglets.
  • MRSA — methicillin-resistant S. aureus, a public-health concern.
  • Treatment — penicillin (if susceptible), methicillin/oxacillin, tetracyclines.
Chapter 04

Bacillus anthracis

Bacillus anthracis

A large, Gram-positive, capsulated, aerobic/facultatively anaerobic, spore-forming rod (family Bacillaceae) that causes anthrax in animals and Woolsorter's disease, hide porter's disease and malignant pustule in humans.

History EXAM HOT

  • 1849Pollender — first pathogenic bacterium observed under a microscope.
  • 1850Davaine — first communicable disease shown transmissible by blood inoculation.
  • 1863–68Davaine & Rayer credited with discovery of the anthrax bacillus.
  • 1863Koch — first bacterium isolated in pure culture.
  • 1881Pasteur — attenuated vaccine (growth at 42–43 °C).

Morphology

Large rods, 4–8 × 1–1.5 μm, non-motile, arranged end to end giving a bamboo-stick / box-car appearance. Capsule = polymer of D-glutamic acid, formed only with serum/bicarbonate media and reduced pCO2 — demonstrated by the McFadyean reaction (polychrome methylene blue). Spores are central/oval, never formed inside the living body, only in soil or culture.

Culture & resistance

60 °C/30 minkills vegetative cells
60 yearsspore viability in soil
140 °C/2–3 hdry-heat resistance of spores
25–30 °Coptimum sporulation temp

Colonies: irregular, grey-white, frosted-glass; medusa head/curled-hair edge under LP microscope; inverted fir-tree growth in gelatin stab; selective medium = PLET (polymyxin, lysozyme, EDTA, thallous acetate). Spore destruction in wool/hair uses formaldehyde — duckering (2% HCHO, 39–40 °C, 20 min, or 0.25% at 60 °C, 6 h).

Toxin complex

3-factor exotoxin
EF + PA + LF → oedema + shock
  • EF Oedema Factor (Factor I)
  • PA Protective Antigen (Factor II)
  • LF Lethal Factor (Factor III)

No single factor is toxic alone; the complete complex produces local oedema and generalised shock.

Pathogenicity

SpeciesClinical course
CattlePeracute (death <2 h), acute (48–96 h, fever, bloody diarrhoea, abortion), chronic (rare); mortality 90% in bulls
SheepAcute, rapid death after convulsions
HorseAcute oedema of throat/neck ± colic
PigMore resistant; throat/neck oedema, enteritis, not always fatal
Necropsy caution

Never open a carcass suspected of anthrax — sporulation on air exposure contaminates the environment. Send an ear or muzzle piece for examination instead.

Diagnosis

  • Blood smearWright's/Giemsa stain; large blue rods with pink-purple capsules.
  • Ascoli's precipitation test (1911)Boiled tissue filtrate layered over anti-anthrax serum; white ring = positive.
  • String-of-pearls test (Charlton, 1980)0.05–0.5 IU penicillin/ml agar; swollen round cells in chains after 3–6 h.
  • Gamma phage testZone of lysis on strain-14 control confirms B. anthracis.
B. anthracis
  • Non-motile, capsulated
  • Long chains, no broth turbidity
  • Susceptible to gamma phage
Anthracoid organisms
  • Motile, non-capsulated
  • Short chains, turbid broth
  • Not susceptible to gamma phage
ImmunitySterne / Mazucchi vaccines

Sterne strain vaccine = non-capsulated avirulent spores, given 1 month pre-outbreak. Mazucchi vaccine = attenuated carbazoo spores in 2% saponin. Protective antibody to Factor II (PA) develops in 7–10 days, lasting ~1 year.

Chapter recap
  • Koch, 1863 — first bacterium isolated in pure culture.
  • Capsule — poly-D-glutamic acid; only in virulent strains.
  • 3-factor toxin — EF + PA + LF, none toxic alone.
  • Spore survival — up to 60 years in soil.
  • Duckering — formaldehyde disinfection of wool/hair.
  • Never open the carcass — prevents sporulation and environmental contamination.
  • String-of-pearls — penicillin-induced swollen cell chains, diagnostic.
Chapter 05

Clostridium — Family Characters & Disease Groups

Clostridium

Anaerobic, Gram-positive, pleomorphic, spore-forming rods (family Clostridiaceae) grouped by the type of disease their exotoxins cause.

Four disease-based groups EXAM HOT

  • Histotoxic — wound/trauma tissue infections
    • C. chauvoei — blackleg (cattle, sheep)
    • C. septicum — malignant oedema, braxy, necrotic dermatitis
    • C. novyi type A — gas gangrene, big head of rams
  • Hepatotoxic — toxin produced in the liver
    • C. novyi type B — black disease
    • C. haemolyticum (C. novyi type D) — bacillary haemoglobinuria
  • Enterotoxigenic — enterotoxaemia/food poisoning
    • C. perfringens types A–E — food poisoning, lamb dysentery, pulpy kidney, struck, necrotic enteritis
    • C. colinum — quail disease/ulcerative enteritis; C. piliforme — Tyzzer's disease; C. sordellii — gas gangrene
  • Neurotoxic — potent exotoxin
    • C. tetani — tetanus
    • C. botulinum — botulism

Shared family characters

  • Motile by peritrichous flagella except C. perfringens and C. tetani type VI.
  • C. perfringens is the only capsulated species.
  • Oxidase- and catalase-negative; C. novyi (oedematiens) strict anaerobe, dies on O2 exposure; C. histolyticum and C. perfringens are aerotolerant.
  • Robertson's cooked meat broth is the key isolation medium.
Saccharolytic (turn meat pink)
  • C. novyi, C. septicum, C. chauvoei, C. perfringens
Proteolytic (turn meat black, foul odour)
  • C. tetani, C. botulinum, C. haemolyticum
Chapter recap
  • 4 groups — histotoxic, hepatotoxic, enterotoxigenic, neurotoxic — grouped by disease mechanism, not phylogeny.
  • Only capsulated species — C. perfringens.
  • Non-motile exceptions — C. perfringens, C. tetani type VI.
  • Robertson's cooked meat broth — universal clostridial enrichment medium.
  • Saccharolytic vs proteolytic — meat turns pink (gas producers) vs black (foul odour producers).
Chapter 06

Clostridium tetani

Clostridium tetani

A strict anaerobic, Gram-positive rod producing a terminal spherical endospore that gives the classic drumstick appearance; cause of tetanus.

History

  • 1884Disease mechanism first understood (described earlier by Hippocrates).
  • 1886Rosenbach — demonstrated the slender bacillus with round terminal spores.
  • 1889Kitasato — isolated in pure culture, reproduced disease by inoculation.

Habitat & culture

Soil contaminated with animal faeces; also street/hospital dust, catgut, plaster of Paris. Strict anaerobe, optimum 37 °C, pH 7.4; swarms on moist agar — stiff agar (3%) confines it to rhizoid colonies. On horse blood agar, α-haemolysis develops later into β-haemolysis (tetanolysin). Gelatin stab shows a fir-tree pattern; feebly proteolytic, ferments no sugars, indole positive.

Antigens & toxins EXAM HOT

10flagellar serotypes (I–X)
65 °C/5 mintetanospasmin heat inactivation
3×10⁷min. mouse lethal doses/mg protein
Two toxins
Tetanospasmin (neurotoxin) + Tetanolysin (haemolysin)
  • Tetanospasmin MW 68,000 monomer (toxic), dimer (non-toxic, antigenic); oxygen-stable, heat-labile
  • Tetanolysin heat- and O2-labile, lyses rabbit/horse RBCs, similar to streptolysin O

Susceptibility to toxin (descending order): horse and human > guinea pig > mouse > goat > rabbit; birds and reptiles are highly resistant.

C. tetani toxin
  • Site: wounds
  • Acts centrally — blocks synaptic inhibition
  • Spastic paralysis
  • One antigenic type (tetanospasmin)
C. botulinum toxin
  • Site: carcases, decaying matter
  • Acts peripherally — blocks neuromuscular transmission
  • Flaccid paralysis
  • Eight antigenic types (A–G)

Why does tetanus present with spastic, not flaccid, paralysis?

Tetanospasmin blocks inhibitory synapses centrally in the CNS, releasing motor neurons from inhibition, unlike botulinum toxin's peripheral neuromuscular block.

Chapter recap
  • Kitasato, 1889 — first pure culture isolation.
  • Drumstick spore — terminal, spherical, bulges the cell.
  • Tetanospasmin — the neurotoxin, MW 68,000, blocks CNS synaptic inhibition.
  • Tetanolysin — the haemolysin, O2- and heat-labile.
  • Species susceptibility — horse/human most susceptible; birds/reptiles resistant.
  • 10 serotypes — flagellar (H) antigen types I–X; type VI non-flagellated.
  • Paralysis type — spastic (tetanus) vs flaccid (botulism).
Chapter 07

Clostridium botulinum

Clostridium botulinum

A group of Gram-positive, spore-forming anaerobes producing extremely potent neurotoxins that cause botulism — flaccid paralysis in birds, ruminants, horses and humans. Named from botulus, Latin for sausage.

History & habitat

Van Ermengem (1896) first isolated it from ham linked to an outbreak. Endospores are widespread in soil and water; disease follows ingestion of preformed toxin from contaminated cans, carcases, silage or rotting vegetation. Named regionally: Shaker foal syndrome (horse), Lamsiekte/loin disease (cattle), Limberneck/western duck sickness (waterfowl).

Toxin types & hosts EXAM HOT

TypeSusceptibleSourceDisease
AHumans, chickens, pigsVegetables, meat, fishFood-borne botulism
Cα (C1)WaterfowlInvertebrate carcases, refuseLimberneck
Cβ (C2)Cattle, horses, mink, dogsCarcases, baled silageForage poisoning
DCattle, sheepBone/carcase ingestion (P deficiency)Lamsiekte

8 types (A–G) total; toxin is a protoxin activated by trypsin, MW ~70,000, heat-labile (100 °C/20 min destroys toxin, though spores survive 100 °C for hours). Lethal human dose 1–2 μg; >120 million mouse lethal doses/mg.

Pathogenesis — three forms

  1. Food-bornePreformed toxin absorbed from gut, blocks acetylcholine release at the myoneural junction → flaccid paralysis, respiratory failure.
  2. WoundSpores germinate in a wound; toxin spreads systemically (mechanism in shaker foal syndrome).
  3. Infant/toxico-infectionSpores germinate in an immature gut flora — floppy baby syndrome in infants; rare type-C epidemics in broiler chicks/turkey poults.

Diagnosis

  • Mouse inoculation of serum/exudate — toxin produces the characteristic wasp-waist abdominal-breathing appearance.
  • Toxin identification — mouse neutralisation with polyvalent, then monovalent, antitoxin.
  • Foodstuff isolation — heat 65–80 °C/30 min, culture on blood agar under CO2 at 35 °C up to 5 days.
Control note

No routine immunisation exists; bivalent/trivalent antitoxin and IV therapeutic agents (tetraethylammonium, guanidine HCl) enhance neuromuscular transmitter release in emergencies.

Chapter recap
  • Van Ermengem, 1896 — first isolation, from contaminated ham.
  • 8 toxin types A–G; type D = Lamsiekte in cattle (phosphorus deficiency, Pica).
  • Mechanism — blocks acetylcholine release peripherally → flaccid paralysis.
  • 3 forms — food-borne, wound, infant (toxico-infectious).
  • Wasp-waist mice — diagnostic sign of toxin in mouse bioassay.
  • Toxin destroyed at 100 °C/20 min; spores survive far longer.
Chapter 08

Clostridium perfringens

Clostridium perfringens (C. welchii)

A capsulated, non-motile, Gram-positive anaerobe causing acute, highly fatal enterotoxaemias; first cultivated by Achalme (1891), described in detail by Welch and Nuttal (1892).

Culture EXAM HOT

Optimum 45 °C (generation time 10 min) though grows at 37 °C, pH 5.5–8.0. In litmus milk gives stormy fermentation (acid clot disrupted by gas). On blood agar, target hemolysis — narrow complete zone (theta toxin) inside a wide incomplete zone (alpha toxin). Alpha toxin (lecithinase C) is demonstrated by Nagler's reaction on egg-yolk agar, neutralised only on the antitoxin half of the plate.

Toxin types A–E

TypeMajor toxinsHostDisease
AAlphaHuman; lambs; broilersFood poisoning; yellow lamb disease; necrotic enteritis
BAlpha, BetaLambs <3 wksLamb dysentery
CAlpha, BetaPiglets 1–3 d; broilers; adult sheep/goatHaemorrhagic enteritis; necrotic enteritis; Struck
DAlpha, EpsilonSheep (not neonates)Pulpy kidney (over-eating disease)
EAlpha, IotaCalves, lambsEnterotoxaemia
  • Alpha — lecithinase C; lethal, dermonecrotic, haemolytic (hot-cold); produced by all types.
  • Beta — trypsin-sensitive; explains type B/C predilection for neonates (colostrum has antitrypsin activity).
  • Epsilon — a protoxin activated by trypsin; increases gut permeability, damages vascular endothelium — both enterotoxin and neurotoxin.
  • Iota — also a protoxin; shared with C. spiroforme and C. difficile.
  • Minor toxins — theta (haemolysin), kappa (collagenase), lambda (proteinase), mu (hyaluronidase), nu (DNase).

Pathogenesis & signs

Precipitated by abrupt dietary change/overeating → reduced peristalsis → toxin absorption. Pulpy kidney disease shows focal symmetrical encephalomalacia, glycosuria, sudden death in well-grown lambs.

Diagnostic sample

20–30 ml ileal contents from a fresh carcase, centrifuged; 0.4 ml supernatant IV into mice — death within 10 h from toxin (within 5 min = shock artefact).

Control

Pre-lambing ewe vaccination (formalised whole culture or alum-precipitated toxoid) gives lambs colostral protection for the first 3 weeks; direct lamb vaccination from day 3, boosted at 4 weeks; immunity lasts 6–12 months without a booster.

Chapter recap
  • Welch & Nuttal, 1892 — detailed original description.
  • 5 types A–E — distinguished by alpha/beta/epsilon/iota toxin combinations.
  • Type D — epsilon toxin, pulpy kidney/over-eating disease of sheep.
  • Nagler's reaction — lecithinase (alpha toxin) demonstration on egg-yolk agar.
  • Stormy fermentation — litmus milk acid-clot disruption by gas.
  • Target haemolysis — theta (inner, complete) + alpha (outer, partial) zones.
  • Colostral antitrypsin — protects neonates from beta/epsilon toxin activation.
Chapter 09

Clostridium chauvoei

Clostridium chauvoei (C. feseri, C. chauvoei type B)

A Gram-positive, worldwide soil organism causing blackleg (black quarter) in cattle and sheep.

Morphology & culture

Rod-shaped, rounded ends, 3–8 μm × 5 μm; sometimes pleomorphic cigar-shaped/citron forms; old cultures stain Gram-negative. Strict anaerobe, 37 °C optimum, growth enhanced by liver extract/glucose. Blood agar: whitish-grey colonies with a haemolytic zone. Cooked meat medium: slow growth, meat turns pink with sour odour. Ferments glucose, lactose, sucrose, maltose (acid + gas) but not salicin.

Toxins

  • Alpha — oxygen-stable haemolysin; necrotoxin causing dermonecrosis and fibrinolysis (lethal IV in mice/guinea pigs).
  • Beta — DNase.
  • Gamma — hyaluronidase.
  • Delta — oxygen-labile haemolysin.

Pathogenicity EXAM HOT

Affects cattle aged 6 months–2/3 years. Sign: crepitating swelling (gas) in the hind/fore quarter, lameness, trembling muscles; death within 24 h. In sheep, an acute febrile illness follows injury.

Lesion

Dark red, dry, necrotic muscle with small gas bubbles and a characteristic rancid odour; yellowish/blood-stained oedematous fluid surrounds it.

Diagnosis, control & prevention

  • SmearGram-positive rods from lesion/oedema fluid.
  • FATDistinguishes C. chauvoei from C. septicum.
  • Toxin neutralisationBroth culture tested in mice/guinea pigs against specific antitoxin.
  • VaccinationFormalised alum-precipitated whole culture; multi-component vaccine with C. chauvoei, C. septicum, C. welchii type D and C. tetani; two doses 2–3 weeks apart give strong immunity.
  • TreatmentHyperimmune serum + penicillin; oxytetracycline/chlortetracycline early.
Chapter recap
  • Age group — young cattle 6 months–3 years.
  • Crepitating swelling — gas under skin, hind/fore quarter, characteristic sign.
  • Alpha toxin — main lethal, dermonecrotic toxin.
  • Salicin-negative — key fermentation feature distinguishing it.
  • FAT — differentiates from C. septicum.
  • 4-in-1 vaccine — combines C. chauvoei, C. septicum, C. welchii type D, C. tetani.
Chapter 10

Clostridium septicum

Clostridium septicum

Closely related to C. chauvoei (also called C. chauvoei type A); first described by Pasteur and Joubert (1887) as Vibrion septique. Causes malignant oedema, braxy and necrotic dermatitis.

Morphology & culture

Highly pleomorphic, long filamentous forms; cigar-shaped/citron forms common. Strict anaerobe, 37 °C, glucose-promoted growth; colonies swarm/spread on ordinary media; rhizoid on stiff agar. Cooked meat medium: pink, rancid odour, abundant gas (saccharolytic). Litmus milk gives stormy fermentation like C. perfringens. Ferments glucose, lactose, maltose, salicin — not sucrose.

Antigens & toxins

  • Alpha — oxygen-stable haemolysin, dermonecrotic, lethal; direct cardiac muscle effect; needs iron for growth and toxin production.
  • Beta — leucotoxic, DNase.
  • Gamma — hyaluronidase.
  • Delta — oxygen-labile haemolysin.

6 antigenic groups based on somatic/flagellar antigens.

Two disease forms EXAM HOT

Braxy (sheep)
  • Winter; ingestion of frozen grass + soil spores
  • Abomasal mucosa damaged by cold
  • Abdominal pain, diarrhoea, rapid death
  • Haemorrhagic abomasal inflammation at necropsy
Malignant oedema (cattle, sheep, pig)
  • Exogenous — spores enter wounds
  • Gas-gangrenous, fever, soft wet swelling
  • Muscle dark red-black; much exudate and gas

Diagnosis

History, symptoms, lesions; FAT differentiates from C. chauvoei; culture-fluid + specific antitoxin neutralisation in guinea pigs. Treatment: penicillin ± hyperimmune serum; sheep vaccinated with multicomponent clostridial vaccine.

  • Braxy — winter frozen-grass disease of sheep, abomasal lesion.
  • Malignant oedema — exogenous wound gas gangrene, multi-species.

Clostridium novyi & Clostridium haemolyticum

Clostridium novyi (C. oedematiens)

Worldwide soil/gut organism classified into 4 toxin-based types (A–D); type D is renamed C. haemolyticum.

Culture & toxins EXAM HOT

Type B/D are demanding strict anaerobes — die within 15 min of O2 exposure; grown with cysteine media. C. novyi type A shows an iridescent pearly layer on egg-yolk agar (unique lecithinase + lipase combination). Toxins: alpha (types A, B — lethal, necrotising, capillary damage), beta (types B, D — lecithinase; dominant in bacillary haemoglobinuria), gamma (necrotising phospholipase D), delta (O2-labile haemolysin), epsilon (lipase), plus zeta, eta, theta.

Pathogenesis

Dormant spores in Kupffer cells activate when liver tissue is damaged — classically by migrating immature Fasciola hepatica. Anaerobic necrotic foci allow spore germination and toxin release into the circulation. Big head develops when subcutaneous ram-fighting wounds are invaded by type A.

  • 4 toxin types A–D; D renamed C. haemolyticum.
  • Type A — only clostridium producing both lecithinase and lipase (pearly egg-yolk sheen).
  • Black disease — type B, triggered by liver fluke migration.
  • Bacillary haemoglobinuria — type D/C. haemolyticum, beta toxin dominant, 90% mortality.

  • Control — snail (fluke vector) destruction + vaccination.

Listeria monocytogenes

Listeria monocytogenes

A small, Gram-positive, non-spore-forming rod (family Listeriaceae) causing septicaemia, abortion and CNS infection across many species. First isolated by Gill (1929) from sheep; named by Murray (1926), renamed by Pirie (1927/1940).

Habitat & morphology EXAM HOT

Widespread in soil, faeces, decaying vegetation and silage (pH 5.5) — poor-quality silage can carry up to 10⁷ cfu/kg. Grows 4–45 °C, pH 5.5–9.6, tolerant of 10% NaCl. Motile by 1–5 peritrichous flagella at room temperature but non-motile at 37 °C — the classic tumbling motility on hanging-drop at 25 °C. On TSA/BHI, colonies show a blue-green sheen under oblique light.

4–45 °Cgrowth range
10⁷ cfu/kgsilage load in outbreaks
16serovars; only 3 cause most disease

CAMP reaction & antigens

L. monocytogenes is CAMP-positive with S. aureus; L. ivanovii is CAMP-negative but gives a wider haemolysis zone. Of 16 serovars, disease is dominated by ½a, ½b and 4b.

Pathogenesis — four syndromes

  1. SubclinicalCommon after poor-quality, high-pH silage in cold weather.
  2. Septicaemic/neonatalGastroenteritis, bilateral meningitis; frequent neonatal death.
  3. AbortionSporadic, cattle/sheep, occurs 4–8 months gestation; retained placenta common.
  4. Neural (circling disease)14–40 day incubation; unilateral meningoencephalitis via trigeminal nerve or dental-pulp route; drooping ear, head tilt, circling, facial paralysis.

Route: gut epithelium → hepatic/splenic macrophages (via listeriolysin O) → septicaemia; or trigeminal/dental-pulp entry → CNS (micro-abscesses, most common in mid-brain, pons, medulla).

Diagnosis

  • Cold enrichment — homogenised CNS tissue held at 4 °C, subcultured weekly on blood agar for up to 12 weeks.
  • Anton's test — conjunctival inoculation in rabbit/guinea pig; purulent keratoconjunctivitis in 24–36 h is specific for L. monocytogenes.
  • Chick embryo CAM inoculation produces focal necrotic lesions.
Public health

Causes human meningoencephalitis, abortion, endocarditis; sources include soil, unpasteurised milk, soft cheese; risk concentrated in the immunocompromised.

Chapter recap
  • Gill, 1929 — first isolated from sheep.
  • Silage link — poor quality, pH 5.5, cold weather = classic risk factor.
  • Tumbling motility — 25 °C hanging-drop, end-over-end movement.
  • Circling disease — neural listeriosis, trigeminal/dental-pulp route, head tilt.
  • CAMP-positive — L. monocytogenes; L. ivanovii is CAMP-negative.
  • Anton's test — conjunctival keratoconjunctivitis, specific for L. monocytogenes.
  • Killed vaccines ineffective — intracellular pathogen needs cell-mediated immunity.

Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae

Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae

A Gram-positive, non-motile, non-spore-forming rod/filament (family Erysipelotrichaceae) causing swine erysipelas and disease in sheep, turkeys and other species.

Habitat & morphology EXAM HOT

Widespread — mammals, fish, birds, reptiles, amphibians, soil (survives ≥20 days in alkaline soil). 30–50% of pigs carry it in tonsils/lymphoid tissue; carrier animals are the main infection source. Smooth (S)-form colonies from acute disease = Gram-positive rods; rough (R)-form from chronic disease = Gram-positive filaments.

Culture & biochemistry

Grows 5–42 °C, pH 6.7–9.2, 8% NaCl; will not grow on MacConkey agar. TSI stab shows an H2S black line along the stab. R-forms give bottle-brush growth in gelatin stabs (21 °C/5 days). Coagulase-positive, catalase-negative, oxidase-negative; does not hydrolyse aesculin or produce urease.

23 serotypes; serotype 1 (1a/1b) and 2 dominate swine disease. Virulent strains produce high neuraminidase, causing vascular damage/thrombosis → diamond skin disease. Route: ingestion/skin abrasion → small intestine → epithelial adherence → bloodstream → localisation (joints, valves, skin).

Mycobacterium — Tuberculosis Complex

Mycobacterium

Slender, acid-fast rods (mycolic-acid-rich cell wall resists Gram staining) of family Mycobacteriaceae; genus name (Lehmann & Neumann, 1896) means "fungus bacterium" for its branching filamentous growth.

History

  • 1868Hansen — discovered the leprosy (Hansen) bacillus.
  • 1882Koch — isolated the mammalian tubercle bacillus, satisfied Koch's postulates; Ehrlich discovered the acid-fast property the same year.
  • 1895Johne — described Johne's bacillus (M. paratuberculosis).

Classification EXAM HOT

  • Slowly growing mycobacteria
    • M. tuberculosis — human TB, also dogs
    • M. bovis — bovine TB, wide host range incl. humans
    • M. africanum — human TB
    • M. avium — avian TB; also cattle, sheep, goat, pig
  • Atypical (Runyon, 1959) — by pigmentation/growth rate
    • Photochromogens — M. kansasii, M. marinum, M. simiae
    • Scotochromogens — M. gordonae
    • Non-chromogens — M. avium, M. intracellulare, M. paratuberculosis, M. lepraemurium (feline leprosy)
    • Rapid growers (<7 days) — M. phlei, M. smegmatis
  • Non-cultivable
    • M. leprae

Culture

Generation time 14–20 h; colonies at 2 weeks, sometimes 6–8 weeks. M. tuberculosis obligate aerobe; M. bovis microaerophilic. Lowenstein-Jensen medium (glycerol) — M. tuberculosis/M. avium grow well (eugonic); M. bovis grows sparsely (dysgonic) but thrives on pyruvate-based Stonebrink's medium. Niacin production and nitrate reduction are unique to M. tuberculosis.

60 °C/15–20 minheat kill point
10–14 dto CMI response after infection

Cell-wall virulence factors

  • Cord factor (trehalose-6,6′-dimycolate) + Wax D — inhibits chemotaxis, drives delayed hypersensitivity.
  • Sulfatides — block phagolysosome fusion, allowing intracellular survival.
  • No exotoxins produced; virulence resides entirely in cell-wall lipids (mycosides).

Pathogenesis & lesion

Inhaled/ingested 1–4 μm particles reach alveoli; macrophage aggregation forms the tubercle — central caseous necrosis, epithelioid-cell zone, peripheral lymphocytes/fibroblasts, often calcified. Haematogenous spread gives miliary TB (millet-seed lesions, notably in deer).

Diagnosis

Inoculated animalM. tuberculosisM. bovisM. avium

Guinea pig (SC)++++−
Chicken (IV)−−++

Ziehl-Neelsen acid-fast stain on smears; tuberculin skin test (intradermal, comparative, ophthalmic in cattle; wattle test in poultry); gamma-interferon assay, PCR, ELISA.

Control

Treatment and vaccination are not used in cattle control programmes; national eradication relies on tuberculin testing followed by isolation and slaughter of reactors (test-and-slaughter).

Chapter recap
  • Koch, 1882 — isolated the mammalian tubercle bacillus.
  • Acid-fastness — due to mycolic acid in the cell wall, discovered by Ehrlich (1882).
  • Eugonic vs dysgonic — M. tuberculosis/avium grow well on glycerol media; M. bovis needs pyruvate (Stonebrink's).
  • Cord factor — trehalose dimycolate, key virulence lipid.
  • Tubercle — caseous centre + epithelioid + lymphocyte zones, may calcify.
  • Control — test-and-slaughter, not vaccination, in cattle.

Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis

Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis (M. johnei)

An acid-fast, mycobactin-dependent rod causing chronic, fatal Johne's disease (paratuberculosis) in cattle, sheep, goats, camels and wild ruminants.

Morphology & culture EXAM HOT

Short rods 1–2 μm, rounded ends, motile, no spores; club-shaped on artificial media. Absolutely requires mycobactin (a killed extract of M. phlei) for growth. Herrold's egg-yolk medium with mycobactin, incubated at 37 °C for up to 16 weeks, is the standard isolation method; colonies are minute, grey-white, friable (<1 mm).

Pathogenesis

Shed in faeces, milk, semen; survives up to 1 year in the environment. Calves <1 month are most susceptible. Ingested bacilli are engulfed by macrophages in Peyer's patches, surviving and replicating intracellularly; immune-mediated granulomatous reaction → lamina propria/submucosa infiltration → protein loss and malabsorption.

16 weeksculture time on Herrold's medium
1 yearenvironmental survival
~100%mortality once clinical

Clinical course

Long subclinical phase; signs appear typically >2 years of age — persistent, profuse diarrhoea without loss of appetite, progressive weight loss to emaciation and death. In sheep/goats, diarrhoea is less marked or absent. Lesion: thickened, transversely-corrugated terminal ileum/large intestine mucosa; enlarged, oedematous mesenteric/ileocaecal lymph nodes.

Diagnosis

  • Acid-fast faecal smearDirect microscopy for a rapid presumptive result.
  • CultureDecontaminate with 0.3% benzalkonium chloride, culture on Herrold's egg-yolk medium, 37 °C up to 16 weeks.
  • SerologyCFT (laborious, insensitive); AGPT; ELISA (M. phlei-absorbed serum) detects subclinical cases.
  • Johnin testIntradermal (delayed hypersensitivity, peak at ~1 month post-infection) or intravenous (fever response).
  • DNA probesHigh sensitivity, direct faecal detection.
Zoonotic note

M. avium subsp. paratuberculosis infection in humans is implicated as Crohn's disease (chronic human enteritis) — association, not proven causation.

Control

Isolate clinical cases (heavy shedders); detect and cull subclinical carriers; inactivated adjuvanted vaccines exist; a live attenuated vaccine given subcutaneously to calves before 4 weeks of age reduces herd incidence.

Chapter recap
  • Johne, 1895 — first described the causative bacillus.
  • Mycobactin-dependent — obligatory growth requirement, unique among mycobacteria here.
  • Herrold's egg-yolk medium — standard culture, up to 16 weeks.
  • Susceptible age — calves <1 month most susceptible to infection.
  • Clinical onset — typically >2 years age, chronic diarrhoea without anorexia.
  • Crohn's disease link — proposed human association, unproven.
  • Johnin test — intradermal delayed hypersensitivity, peaks ~1 month post-infection.

Nocardia

Nocardia

Aerobic, Gram-positive, branching filamentous soil saprophytes (family Nocardiaceae), closely related to Corynebacterium, Mycobacterium and Rhodococcus. Nocard (1888) first described it from bovine farcy, giving the type species N. farcinica.

Morphology & culture EXAM HOT

Gram-positive branching filaments <1 μm diameter, fragmenting into coccobacillary forms; obligate aerobes, non-motile; some strains acid-fast (modified ZN, red-staining). Colonies on blood agar are vivid white, powdery (aerial filaments); on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA), dry, wrinkled, turning deep orange.

  • Three morphological groups
    • Group I — mycelia fragment within 2–14 h
    • Group II — mycelia fragment at ~18–20 h
    • Group III (pathogenic species) — leathery, pigmented; fragmentation delayed past 5 days

Actinomyces & Arcanobacterium pyogenes

Actinomyces / Arcanobacterium

Gram-positive, branching filamentous prokaryotes (order Actinomycetales) causing suppurative, granulomatous disease; the genus name was coined by Harz (1879), with A. bovis isolated by Boestrom (1891).

Morphology EXAM HOT

Pleomorphic, filamentous with V/Y/T configurations. Pus in actinomycosis contains pale-yellow sulfur granules — a mass of Gram-positive filaments in mineralised calcium phosphate, ringed by Gram-negative club-shaped bodies (the ray fungus appearance). Arcanobacterium pyogenes shows no granules — small pleomorphic rods, cocci, pear-shaped cells, sometimes Chinese-letter arrangement.

Culture

  • Neither genus grows on Sabouraud dextrose agar (unlike Nocardia).
  • A. bovis is capnophilic (needs 5–10% CO2); A. pyogenes/A. viscosus grow aerobically, enhanced by CO2.
  • A. bovis: 2–4 days to grow; A. pyogenes visible in 24 h.
  • A. pyogenes: hazy haemolysis at 24 h, complete narrow zone at 48 h; positive CAMP test with S. aureus; pits a Loeffler serum slope in 24–48 h.

Diseases

OrganismHostDisease
A. bovis (ray fungus)CattleLumpy jaw (bovine actinomycosis)

Pathogenesis

A. bovis is normal oral flora; dental trauma or coarse feed lets it invade bone (usually the mandible/maxilla), producing rarefying osteomyelitis — lumpy jaw — with sinus tracts discharging sulphur-granule pus. A. pyogenes is a mucosal commensal that becomes pathogenic after tissue injury or co-infection (Fusobacterium necrophorum, Peptostreptococcus indolicus), producing a dermonecrotic exotoxin, protease and neuraminidase.

Diagnosis

Crush a granule in 10% KOH to view clubs microscopically; Gram stain confirms the ray fungus. Anaerobic-preserving transport, culture on brain-heart-infusion/blood agar under anaerobic, microaerophilic and aerobic conditions in parallel. Failure to grow on SDA (unlike Nocardia) supports Actinomyces.

Treatment

Highly sensitive to tetracycline, chloramphenicol and penicillin (benzyl penicillin, ampicillin) — unlike the often-refractory Nocardia infections.

Chapter recap
  • Boestrom, 1891 — isolated Actinomyces bovis.
  • Sulfur granules — pathognomonic of actinomycosis, absent in A. pyogenes infection.
  • Lumpy jaw — mandibular/maxillary osteomyelitis from oral A. bovis.
  • Summer mastitis — A. pyogenes + Peptostreptococcus indolicus co-infection.
  • No SDA growth — key point distinguishing Actinomyces from Nocardia.
  • CAMP-positive — A. pyogenes enhances S. aureus haemolysis.
  • Highly penicillin-sensitive — unlike Nocardia.

Corynebacterium & Rhodococcus equi

Corynebacterium / Rhodococcus

Gram-positive, club-shaped, non-sporing rods with polar metachromatic (Babes-Ernst) granules; the animal-associated species are called diphtheroids, distinct from C. diphtheriae (human diphtheria).

Morphology & stains EXAM HOT

Palisade or Chinese-letter/cuneiform arrangement from incomplete daughter-cell separation. Polymetaphosphate granules stain reddish-purple with Loeffler's methylene blue (Albert's, Neisser's, Ponder's stains also used). Rhodococcus equi appears as a coccus, rod or club, capsulated, weakly acid-fast, in clusters.

Culture & CAMP behaviour

Scanty growth on plain media; needs blood/serum/egg enrichment, 37 °C, pH 7.2. R. equi colonies turn mucoid, salmon-pink by day 4. The renale group differs on milk agar: C. renale digests casein; C. pilosum and C. cystitidis do not.
