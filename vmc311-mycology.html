Chapter 01

Fungal Biology & Classification of Pathogenic Fungi

Fungi

Eukaryotic, chemoorganotrophic, spore-bearing, non-motile organisms lacking photosynthetic activity; the vegetative body is called a thallus, ranging from single-cell yeasts to multicellular molds, puffballs and mushrooms.

Growth conditions EXAM HOT

10–40 °Coverall growth range
22–30 °Coptimum, saprophytic fungi
30–37 °Coptimum, parasitic fungi
3.8–5.6optimum pH range

Mostly aerobic (especially molds); some yeasts are facultatively anaerobic; obligate anaerobic fungi occur in the bovine rumen. Fungi existing in mycelial form at room temperature but converting to yeast form at 37 °C in host tissue are dimorphic — the shift is termed the YM (Yeast-to-Mold) shift.

Classification by macroscopic morphology EXAM HOT

  • Monomorphic yeasts — budding blastospores, pasty/mucoid colonies
    • e.g. Candida albicans, Cryptococcus neoformans, Geotrichum candidum, Trichosporon cutaneum
  • Monomorphic molds — germ tubes → hyphae → mycelium, filamentous growth
    • e.g. Microsporum, Trichophyton, Aspergillus, Penicillium
  • Dimorphic fungi — mold at 25 °C, yeast at 37 °C
    • e.g. Histoplasma capsulatum, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Sporothrix schenckii

Microscopic morphology

FeatureTypes
HyphaeSeptate (cross-walled) · Aseptate/coenocytic (no cross-walls) · Pseudohypha (elongated budding cells that fail to detach)
Asexual sporesMacroconidium, microconidium, chlamydospore (thick-walled, resistant — e.g. C. albicans), arthrospore (mycelial disarticulation — e.g. Geotrichum), blastospore (budding — e.g. Saccharomyces), sporangiospore (in a sporangium — e.g. Rhizopus)
Sexual sporesAscospore (in an ascus — true yeasts, Ascomycetes), basidiospore (on a basidium — Basidiomycetes), zygospore (fused gametangia — Phycomycetes)
Spore-bearing structuresConidiophore (bears conidia, e.g. Penicillium) · Sporangiophore (bears sporangium, tipped by a columella)

Reproduction & taxonomy

Species with sexual (± asexual) reproduction = perfect fungi (e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae); asexual-only species = imperfect fungi (fungi imperfecti, class Deuteromycetes — home to the majority of veterinary pathogens).

  • Phycomycetes — most primitive; broad aseptate hyphae; asexual sporangiospores; sexual zygospores/oospores.
  • Ascomycetes — unicellular yeast type (Saccharomyces, ascospores in an ascus) and filamentous type (septate hyphae, conidia + ascospores within a perithecium).
  • Basidiomycetes — sexual basidiospores (usually 4) on a club-shaped basidium.
  • Deuteromycetes — fungi imperfecti; filamentous or imperfect-yeast forms; thallospores (arthro-, blasto-, chlamydospores) and conidia.

Classification by mycosis depth EXAM HOT

CategoryTissue invadedExamples
SuperficialOutermost skin/hair layersTrichosporon cutaneum
CutaneousKeratin — skin, hair, nailsCandida albicans, Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton
SubcutaneousMuscle tissueRhinosporidium seeberi, Sporothrix schenckii

Predisposing factors & general characteristics of fungal infection

  • Prolonged antibiotic therapy (alters normal flora — commonly intestinal candidiasis), immunosuppressive drugs, radiation, concurrent disease, pregnancy, genetic immune deficiency, environmental stress/trauma/moisture.
  • Most fungal disease is non-contagious/non-zoonotic — dermatophytosis is the notable exception.
  • Most fungi are opportunistic, low invasiveness/virulence in healthy hosts.
  • Infections often produce chronic granulomatous inflammation, similar to mycobacterial disease.
  • Cell-mediated immunity is usually more protective than humoral immunity, given the chronic course.
  • Hypersensitivity reactions can develop; most infections are asymptomatic or self-limited.
Chapter recap
  • YM shift — mold at room temp, yeast at 37 °C, defines a dimorphic fungus.
  • Deuteromycetes — fungi imperfecti, home to most veterinary pathogens.
  • 4 mycosis categories — superficial, cutaneous, subcutaneous, systemic (deepest, most serious).
  • Chlamydospore — thick-walled resistant spore, classic Candida albicans feature.
  • Dermatophytosis — the one contagious/zoonotic exception among fungal diseases.
  • Cell-mediated immunity — the dominant protective mechanism against chronic fungal disease.
Chapter 02

Dermatophytosis — Ringworm

Dermatophytosis (Ringworm, Tinea)

Contagious, zoonotic infection by keratinophilic molds (Ascomycetes) parasitizing skin, hair, feathers, horn, hooves, claws and nails — the only fungal disease group that is both contagious and a major zoonosis.

History EXAM HOT

  • AntiquityRomans termed the disease "tinea" (small insect), believing insects caused it.
  • 1910Sabouraud published the first detailed systematic study; 37 species now recognised.
  • 1958Gentles first reported oral griseofulvin curing experimental guinea-pig dermatophytosis.
  • 1959Sexual states of some dermatophytes identified — reclassified into Ascomycetes.

Key pathogens

PathogenEcologyAnimals affected
Trichophyton mentagrophytesZoophilicAll domestic animals
Trichophyton verrucosumZoophilicCattle, sheep
Trichophyton equinumZoophilicHorses
Microsporum canisZoophilicDogs, cats (also horses, primates)
Microsporum gypseumGeophilic (soil)Dogs, cats, horses, humans

Morphology & culture

Nonparasitic phase (culture): septate, branching hyphae with macro-/microconidia in the aerial mycelium — Microsporum predominantly macroconidia, Trichophyton predominantly microconidia. Parasitic phase (tissue): arthroconidia only, infectious for years. Grown on dermatophyte test medium (DTM) or Sabouraud dextrose agar (pH 5.6, mildly bacteriostatic), selectivity enhanced by cycloheximide/gentamicin-tetracycline/chloramphenicol; optimum 25–30 °C, days to weeks to grow. Wood's lamp fluorescence (green, from a tryptophan metabolite) is produced only by Microsporum canis among animal dermatophytes.

Pathogenesis EXAM HOT

Keratinase is the key virulence factor — species-specific keratinases limit host range (highly host-adapted species), while broad-acting keratinases (T. mentagrophytes) allow multi-species infection. Conidia enter through abrasions, germinate in the stratum corneum; hair invasion begins near the follicular orifice.

Endothrix
  • Arthroconidia develop within the hair shaft only
  • Cuticle remains intact
Ectothrix
  • Arthroconidia develop outside the hair shaft
  • Cuticle is destroyed

Clinical picture

First sign: hair loss, followed by inflammation. More common in young/old/sick and stabled animals, peaking in winter. Hair invasion weakens/breaks the shaft → circular scaly alopecia ± crusting. Mild in T. verrucosum (calves); severe in T. mentagrophytes (dogs) and M. gypseum (horses). Kerion = local plaque resembling a skin tumour, especially with M. canis. Common tineas: barbae (beard), capitis (scalp), corporis (body), cruris (groin), favosa, pedis (feet), unguium (nails). In fowl: fowl favus/white comb.

Diagnosis

  • Wood's lamp50–70% of M. canis/M. audouinii-infected hairs fluoresce bright greenish.
  • MicroscopySkin scrapings/hair in 10–20% KOH, gently heated; look for hyphae and arthroconidia; sample from the lesion margin, since the periphery is the active front while the centre heals.
  • CultureDTM (turns red as the dermatophyte grows) and SDA ± cycloheximide/chloramphenicol, 25–30 °C up to 4 weeks (T. verrucosum incubated at 37 °C).
Differential diagnosis

Distinguish from insect bites, urticaria, bacterial infections, seborrheic dermatitis, interdigital dermatitis.

Treatment & prevention

Often self-limiting over weeks to months. Systemic griseofulvin (incorporated into keratin, requires prolonged therapy — at least 1 month, longer for nails) is first-line for severe small-animal infections; ketoconazole/fluconazole are alternatives but costlier. Topical: salicylic/benzoic acid, iodine, natamycin, imidazoles (large animals, skin only — not nails). T. verrucosum vaccines give 3–5 years' protection in cattle.

Chapter recap
  • Sabouraud, 1910 — first systematic dermatophyte study.
  • Microsporum → macroconidia; Trichophyton → microconidia — key differential rule.
  • M. canis — the only animal dermatophyte fluorescing under Wood's lamp.
  • Keratinase — the defining virulence factor, host-range determinant.
  • Endothrix vs ectothrix — inside-only vs outside hair-shaft arthroconidia.
  • Sample from lesion margin — the active growing edge, not the healing centre.
  • Griseofulvin — incorporates into keratin; needs prolonged dosing.
Chapter 03

Candida albicans

Candida albicans

A commensal, opportunistic yeast of the oral, GI and genital tracts, causing superficial or systemic candidiasis (moniliasis, candidosis, thrush); the clinically important species among >150 in the genus.

Habitat & disease by host EXAM HOT

HostDisease
Chicken, turkey, pigeon, ducklingCrop mycosis / avian moniliasis
SwineStomach ulcers, cutaneous candidiasis
Puppies, kittens, calves, foalsMycotic stomatitis and enteritis
Mares, bitchesGenital tract infection

Other species: C. tropicalis, C. pseudotropicalis, C. parapsilosis, C. krusei, C. rugosa also cause bovine mastitis; C. parapsilosis → bovine abortion; C. rugosa → equine pyometra.

Pathogenesis

Mostly endogenous, precipitated by immunosuppression, prolonged antibiotics, malnutrition. Adhesins (fibrillar Mannans) bind fibronectin; yeast forms cause tissue damage, hyphal elements invade when yeast division is inhibited. Virulence factors: cell-wall glycoprotein (endotoxin-like activity), proteases, neuraminidase, chitin, mannoprotein, lipids.

Clinical picture

Tiny yellowish/greyish pustules resembling curdled milk; avian crop shows the classic "turkey-towelling" pseudomembrane. Pigs: vomiting, diarrhoea, emaciation. Cattle: pneumonic/enteric signs after antibiotic therapy; mastitis is usually mild, self-limiting (recovery within a week). Birds: stunted growth, ruffled feathers, high mortality in young acute cases; mouth/oesophageal ulcer-like patches.

Diagnosis EXAM HOT

  • MorphologyOval budding yeast, pseudohyphae; purple-blue on Gram stain, visible with 10% KOH or lactophenol cotton blue.
  • CultureBlood agar/SDA without inhibitors, 37 °C aerobically up to 5 days; white/cream, shiny, beery-smelling colonies. BiGGy agar — C. albicans reduces bismuth sulphite to sulphide, giving brownish colonies with no medium diffusion (vs C. tropicalis, which diffusely blackens the medium by 72 h).
  • Germ tube (serum tube) testInoculum in serum, 37 °C 2–3 h — thin-walled germ tubes = characteristic of C. albicans.
  • Chlamydospore demonstration (Dalmau technique)Corn meal-Tween 80 agar, 30 °C, 2–4 days; thick-walled chlamydospores on pseudohyphae tips confirm C. albicans.
  • BiochemistryC. albicans: glucose A+G, sucrose A only, maltose A+G, lactose −, chlamydospore + (distinguishes from C. tropicalis/pseudotropicalis/parapsilosis).
Treatment & control

Correct predisposing conditions first. Nystatin (feed additive or intramammary) treats most species; amphotericin B is most effective for systemic disease; ketoconazole/clotrimazole for mucocutaneous forms.

Chapter recap
  • >150 Candida species — only C. albicans is a common animal pathogen.
  • Turkey-towelling — pathognomonic crop pseudomembrane in avian moniliasis.
  • Germ tube test — rapid, specific for C. albicans.
  • BiGGy agar — bismuth sulphite reduction differentiates Candida species.
  • Chlamydospore on corn meal agar — the key confirmatory biochemical feature.
  • Amphotericin B — drug of choice for systemic candidiasis.
Chapter 04

Rhinosporidium seeberi

Rhinosporidium seeberi

Causative agent of rhinosporidiosis — large polyps, tumours or wart-like lesions on nasal/ocular mucous membranes of cattle, horses, mules, dogs and humans; the organism cannot be cultured and its taxonomic classification remains unresolved.

History & habitat EXAM HOT

First described in humans by Seeber (1910). Natural habitat is associated with stagnant water; worldwide but most common in India and Sri Lanka. 90% of infections involve the nose of male animals. Humid South Indian climates favour animal disease; dry areas see more human disease.

Pathogenesis

Mode of transmission is unconfirmed — no evidence of animal-to-animal or animal-to-human spread. Local trauma (e.g. swimming/water exposure) and possibly airborne soil particles are implicated. Life cycle: round endospore (6–10 μm) → thick-walled sporangium (100–450 μm) containing thousands of endospores → mature sporangiospores (7–9 μm) escape via a wall pore. Infection is limited to mucosal epithelium, producing local hyperplasia and a localised immune response — commonly nose/nasopharynx, also conjunctivae, skin, ear, genitals, rectum.

Clinical picture

Polyps are pink to deep red, sessile or pedunculated, strawberry-like, vascular and friable — bleed easily on manipulation; chronic but not painful; can obstruct the respiratory tract, causing asphyxia. In dogs: rare, chronic; sneezing, bleeding, wheezing, cauliflower-like nostril growths; zoonotic potential.

Diagnosis & treatment

  • Cannot be cultured and has no laboratory animal model — diagnosis relies entirely on demonstrating spores/sporangia in wet mounts of nasal discharge or polyp sections.
  • H&E-stained sections show young trophic forms (10–100 μm, single central basophilic karyosome) and mature forms (100–300 μm, containing 16,000–20,000 sporangiospores).
  • TreatmentSurgical excision of polyps — recurrence is common.
Chapter recap
  • Seeber, 1910 — first human description.
  • No culture possible — unique among the pathogens in this book; diagnosis is purely morphological.
  • 90% male-nose predilection — a distinctive epidemiological clue.
  • Strawberry-like polyps — vascular, friable, easily bleeding.
  • Sporangium — up to 20,000 endospores per structure.
  • Treatment — surgical excision only; recurrence common.
Chapter 05

Cryptococcus neoformans

Cryptococcus neoformans

An encapsulated yeast causing cryptococcosis (European blastomycosis, Torulosis) — subacute/chronic infection of the CNS, respiratory system and eye; only 1 of 19 Cryptococcus species is pathogenic.

Disease by host EXAM HOT

HostDisease
Dogs, catsSubcutaneous/nasal granulomas, meningitis, blindness
HorsesNasal granuloma
CattleCryptococcal mastitis
HumansCryptococcal meningitis

Morphology

Two varieties: C. neoformans var. neoformans (serotypes A, D; teleomorph Filobasidiella neoformans) and var. gattii (serotypes B, C; teleomorph F. bacillispora). Spherical/oval, thin-walled budding yeast surrounded by a thick mucoid polysaccharide capsule (in tissue, often wider than the cell itself) — visualised with India ink, nigrosin, or lactophenol cotton blue. Gram-positive.

Habitat & culture

Worldwide; found in dust, skin, mucosa, GI tract of normal animals/birds. Reservoir of serotypes A/D is pigeon faeces/soil — high creatinine content inhibits competitors but is utilised by C. neoformans, allowing survival >1 year. Grows on blood agar/SDA (no cycloheximide) aerobically at 37 °C up to 2 weeks; capsule enhanced on chocolate agar + 5% CO2. Unlike most Cryptococcus species, C. neoformans grows up to 40 °C. Birdseed (Guizotia) agar is selective — melanin-pigmented brown colonies after ≥1 week at 37 °C. Strongly urease-positive.

Pathogenesis

Exogenous, acquired by inhalation (no animal-to-animal spread); localises in nasal cavity/paranasal sinuses, extends to brain/meninges. Virulence from the antiphagocytic, immunosuppressive capsule plus a unique diphenol oxidase enzyme. Lesions resemble myxomatous neoplasm; meningeal infection can resemble tubercular meningitis; optic nerve involvement causes blindness.

Clinical picture

Mastitis: mild cases show only gland swelling; severe cases mimic bacterial mastitis (fever, firm swollen udder), milk becomes grey/white/viscid/mucoid. Dogs: CNS signs (incoordination, hyperaesthesia), nasal discharge, subcutaneous granulomas (ear, face, feet). Cats: chronic nasal/ocular discharge, granulomas, blindness.

Diagnosis

  • Direct microscopyIndia ink/nigrosin/LPCB — budding yeast with a large capsule; PAS-haematoxylin stains the cell but not the capsule (appears as a clear halo).
  • CultureBrown pigment on birdseed agar; strong urease production.
  • Animal inoculationIntracerebral/IP in mice — death in 4 days to 2 weeks; gelatinous abdominal/lung lesions.
  • SerologySlide LAT, tube agglutination, CFT, ELISA, IFAT.
Treatment

Amphotericin B is the drug of choice; ketoconazole/fluconazole are useful alternatives.

Chapter recap
  • 19 species, 1 pathogenic — only C. neoformans causes animal/human disease.
  • Pigeon droppings — the key environmental reservoir, thanks to creatinine tolerance.
  • Capsule — the dominant virulence factor, antiphagocytic and immunosuppressive.
  • Grows at 40 °C — distinguishes it from most non-pathogenic Cryptococcus species.
  • Birdseed agar — selective medium, brown pigment production.
  • Urease-positive — a key biochemical identifier.
Chapter 06

Histoplasma capsulatum

Histoplasma capsulatum var. capsulatum (Teleomorph: Ajellomyces capsulatus)

A dimorphic fungus causing chronic granulomatous histoplasmosis (Darling's disease, reticuloendothelial cytomycosis) — a facultative intracellular parasite of macrophages.

Habitat & morphology EXAM HOT

Concentrated in the droppings of blackbirds, gulls, starlings, pigeons; bats are important vectors. Isolated from soil in bat caves, bird roosts, chicken houses, pigeon-inhabited silos. Tissue-form cells: small (2–4 μm) oval yeast, single narrow-necked buds, with a clear halo around darker central material.

Culture

SDA + chloramphenicol (no cycloheximide) at 25 °C, or BHI + blood at 37 °C. At 25 °C, mycelial phase shows small round microconidia plus large (7–18 μm), thick-walled, knob-projected macroconidia; yeast phase grows at 37 °C on blood agar.

Epidemiology

Prevalent in the Americas, parts of Africa/Asia; rare in Australia/Europe; common through the US Midwest/eastern states. Inhalation of spores is the main route (ingestion also occurs); most infections subclinical. Dogs are especially susceptible; also cattle, cats, swine, horses, sheep, wild animals — not reported in birds; not contagious.

Pathogenesis

Ranges from small granulomas to acute disseminating fatal disease; pulmonary or intestinal presentation, inapparent to disseminated. Macrophages phagocytose the fungus, triggering inflammation — either the organism dies or granulomas form with calcification, depending on host immunity and spore load. Macrophages can also disseminate the organism — hence "disease of the reticuloendothelial system." Hepatosplenomegaly, tongue nodules, ocular involvement, and abortion also reported.

Clinical picture

Mostly asymptomatic/benign/self-limiting. Symptomatic: fever, night sweats, weight loss, haemoptysis; disseminated cases show hepato-/splenomegaly, anaemia, leucopenia. Chronic pulmonary: cough, dyspnoea, chest pain, apical lung cavities. Lesion: epithelioid + giant-cell pulmonary granulomas; RE-system invasion of adrenals, bone marrow, GI tract, liver, lymph nodes, spleen — often calcified.

Diagnosis

  • MicroscopyOrganisms rarely seen extracellularly; Gomori methenamine silver or PAS stains reveal them within macrophages (CSF, biopsy, bone marrow, lymph node, buffy coat).
  • Skin testRemains positive for life after exposure — not useful for active disease detection, and can confound serology.
  • CFTRapid titre rise post-infection, falls off by 9 months; cross-reacts with other systemic fungal antigens.
  • Immunodiffusion2 bands = active or past infection.
  • ScreeningLatex agglutination, FAT; CFT remains the confirmatory test.
Treatment

Amphotericin B is the drug of choice; ketoconazole, sulfonamides, ethyl vanillate may also be effective.

Chapter recap
  • Bird/bat droppings — the classic environmental source.
  • Not reported in birds — despite being the primary environmental reservoir species.
  • RE-system disease — macrophages both control and disseminate the organism.
  • Skin test limitation — lifelong positivity makes it useless for active-disease diagnosis.
  • CFT — the confirmatory serological test; titre falls off by 9 months.
  • Amphotericin B — first-line treatment.
Chapter 07

Histoplasma farciminosum — Epizootic Lymphangitis

Histoplasma farciminosum (syn. Cryptococcus/Zymonema farciminosum)

A dimorphic fungus (of debated true genus placement) causing epizootic lymphangitis — cord-like subcutaneous lymphatic thickening with pyogranulomas, mainly in equidae.

History & habitat EXAM HOT

First seen in pus by Rivolta (1873); first cultured by Tokishige (1896) in Japan. Worldwide, with endemic foci in North Africa and Asia. Yeast form in pus: double-contoured oval bodies, 2.5–3.5 × 3–4 μm.

Culture

Grows slowly (4–8 weeks, 25 °C) on SDA + 2.5% glycerol, BHI + 10% horse blood, or other enriched media, CO2-enhanced; mycelial colonies are yellowish-brown to deep brown, waxy, cauliflower-like. Yeast conversion in vitro achieved at 35–37 °C, needing 4–5 serial transfers every 8 days for full conversion. Catalase/urease positive; assimilates ammonium sulphate as sole N source; no CHO fermentation, no gelatin liquefaction, no nitrate reduction.

Resistance

1 monthsurvival in stable/kraal dust
25 monthsviable after lab desiccation
10 weekssurvival in non-sterile water, 26 °C

Epidemiology & pathogenesis

Contagious, zoonotic; mainly horses/mules/donkeys, horses <6 years most susceptible; camels/cattle occasionally. Transmission via direct contact with infective material through skin abrasion, contaminated fomites (grooming tools, harness), biting flies (Musca, Stomoxys — experimentally proven vectors), and venereally (stallion to mare). Incubation weeks to 6 months; skin invasion → lymphatic spread → regional nodes → (advanced cases) internal organs.

Clinical forms EXAM HOT

  • Cutaneous (most common) — indolent chancre-like papules along a lymphatic vessel → pyogranulomatous nodules → ulceration, alternating discharge/closure, scarring; forelimbs, chest, neck most affected; mortality 10–15%, main loss is lost working time.
  • Ophthalmic — conjunctivitis/naso-lachrymal infection; watery discharge, eyelid swelling, papules and ulcerating growths on conjunctiva/nictitating membrane.
  • Respiratory — usually secondary to cutaneous form; yellowish nasal papules/nodules → crater-like bleeding ulcers near the external nares; may extend to lungs.
  • Asymptomatic carrier — fibrocalcific skin scars at old infection sites; positive intradermal and serological tests.

Diagnosis

  • Direct smear/cultureAseptic pus shows double-contoured yeast-like cells; Gram-positive; culture takes 4–8 weeks.
  • SerologyFAT, AGID, ELISA, serum agglutination — none individually sensitive/specific enough to confirm alone.
  • Intradermal test0.1 ml soluble antigen; 8–20 mm skin thickening at 24 h = positive.
  • Animal inoculationImmunosuppressed mice are highly susceptible — useful diagnostically.
Differential diagnosis

Distinguish from glanders, strangles, ulcerative lymphangitis and sporotrichosis — all can present similarly in the same environments.

Treatment & control

IV sodium iodide (10%, weekly ×4) or amphotericin B (0.2 mg/kg, alternate days ×3); daily iodine cleaning; griseofulvin + iodides + surgical drainage/iodine tincture packing. Control: cull infected horses in endemic areas, hygiene; a killed formalised yeast-form vaccine (SC, 5 ml/year) and an attenuated heat-exposed vaccine both give good protection.

Chapter recap
  • Rivolta, 1873 / Tokishige, 1896 — first observed, then first cultured.
  • Cauliflower-like colonies — the classic mycelial-phase appearance.
  • Flies as vectors — Musca and Stomoxys, experimentally confirmed.
  • Horses <6 years — the most susceptible age group.
  • 4 clinical forms — cutaneous (most common), ophthalmic, respiratory, asymptomatic carrier.
  • Differential — glanders, strangles, ulcerative lymphangitis, sporotrichosis.
Chapter 08

Coccidioides immitis

Coccidioides immitis

The most virulent of the fungal pathogens, causing coccidioidomycosis — usually a benign, self-resolving upper respiratory infection, occasionally acute/chronic and fatal.

Epidemiology EXAM HOT

Endemic in soils of the southwestern USA and Central/South America; dust storms increase incidence; disease risk recognised among lab workers isolating the agent. Dogs most frequently affected among domestic animals; also horses, cats, swine, sheep, cattle, humans, non-human primates, and ~30 wild mammal species.

Morphology EXAM HOT

In soil: mold with slender septate hyphae forming chains of infectious arthroconidia, separated by empty disjunctor cells. In tissue: arthroconidia grow into spherical spherules with birefringent walls, producing hundreds of endospores by internal cleavage; wall rupture releases endospores, each capable of repeating the cycle. Only arthroconidia are naturally infectious (endospores can experimentally initiate disease); no sexual spores known. Coccidioidin (mycelial-culture supernatant, mainly polysaccharide) and spherulin (spherule lysate) are used in skin/serologic testing — both leukotactic.

Culture & resistance

Grows faster (1–2 weeks) than other dimorphic pathogens; moist white colony → fluffy mycelium on SDA/blood agar at 25 or 37 °C (bovine blood haemolysed). Arthroconidia in 5–7 days, seen on LPCB wet mount as thick-walled barrel-shaped cells alternating with empty disjunctor cells. Sporangial (spherule) phase reproduced at 40 °C in casein hydrolysate/glucose/biotin/glutathione medium. Arthroconidia resist drying and tolerate heat/salinity better than competing soil organisms — explaining rapid post-rain repopulation of surface soil.

Pathogenesis

Inhalation of arthrospores is the main route (cutaneous infection rare). A potent serine protease digests elastin/collagen/immunoglobulins, aiding spread. Phagocytes engulf but fail to kill the organism, carrying it to lymph nodes. Th-1-driven cell-mediated immunity normally arrests the infection; with inadequate immunity, dissemination reaches bones, skin, abdominal viscera, heart, genital tract, eye, and (rarely in animals) brain/meninges.

Clinical picture

Overt disease is the exception. Canine systemic disease is most prevalent in males 4–7 years; Boxers and Doberman Pinschers are highly susceptible; not reported in cats. Signs: cough, fever, bone-related lameness, discharging sinuses. Cattle/sheep/swine: usually asymptomatic, limited to lung/regional nodes, found only at slaughter. Lesions: white granulomas (miliary to irregular masses), pleural/pericardial/peritoneal effusions; chronic cases show focal suppurative granulomas without caseation/calcification; severe disseminated disease essentially confined to dogs and humans.

Diagnosis

  • Direct examination10% KOH wet mount — spherules 10–80 μm with endospores (2–5 μm); H&E or Gomori methenamine silver stains.
  • CultureBlood agar/SDA + antibiotics, tape-sealed (biohazard), 37 °C and 25 °C.
  • SerologyCoccidioidin skin test (prognostic — negative = poor prognosis); immunodiffusion (multiple bands = progressive disease, single band = stable chronic); CFT, latex agglutination, tube precipitin.
Treatment

Amphotericin B is the drug of choice; nystatin effective but more toxic; ketoconazole may help. No vaccine available.

Chapter recap
  • Most virulent fungal pathogen — a recognised lab-worker biohazard.
  • Spherule/endospore cycle — the unique tissue-phase reproductive pattern.
  • Only arthroconidia are naturally infectious — key distinction from endospores.
  • Boxers & Dobermans — the classic canine breed predisposition.
  • Dissemination limited to dogs/humans — livestock disease stays regional (lung/nodes).
  • Skin test — prognostic, not merely diagnostic — negative result is a poor sign.
Chapter 09

Sporothrix schenckii & Blastomyces dermatitidis

Sporothrix schenckii

A dimorphic, ubiquitous soil/wood/vegetation fungus causing sporotrichosis — nodular lesions that suppurate, ulcerate and drain along the cutaneous/subcutaneous lymphatics.

Sporotrichosis EXAM HOT

Entry via wound or traumatic implantation. Particularly common in horses and dogs; in dogs, more likely to disseminate to bone, muscle, CNS, lungs or genitourinary tract, and can be fatal. In horses, must be differentiated from epizootic lymphangitis (Histoplasma farciminosum). The lymphocutaneous form drains pus at several sites along the lymphatic chain, which itself becomes a transmission route within the animal. Infectious but not contagious; chronic course. Laboratory rodents are highly susceptible experimentally — indicating greater virulence than typical opportunistic fungi.

  • Cultured on BHI agar, blood agar, or SDA + cycloheximide/chloramphenicol; mold is white when young, turning brown-black.
  • Septate, fine hyphae; microconidia cluster at hyphal tips or sit sessile along the sides.
  • Differentiation from similar molds relies on mold-to-yeast conversion; single-celled, cigar-shaped yeasts may be seen in lesion pus.
  • FAT enhances visualisation; rising complement-fixing antibody titre supports serological diagnosis.
Treatment

Potassium iodide, amphotericin B, ketoconazole, miconazole are all effective.

Blastomyces dermatitidis

Blastomyces dermatitidis

Causes blastomycosis from soil enriched with decaying organic matter; primarily a North American disease with sporadic cases in India, Africa and the Middle East.

  • Clinical signs — cutaneous pustules, verrucous or ulcerated lesions, or subcutaneous abscesses; acute pulmonary disease (cough, pleuritic pain, chills, low-grade fever); chronic pulmonary disease (productive cough, fever, weight loss, dyspnoea, fatigue); dissemination to skin, bone, genitourinary tract.
  • Lesions — suppurative and granulomatous tissue response.
  • Treatment — amphotericin B or ketoconazole.
Chapter recap
  • Cigar-shaped yeasts — the classic Sporothrix tissue-phase morphology.
  • Sporotrichosis vs epizootic lymphangitis — key equine differential diagnosis.
  • Dogs — most prone to disseminated, potentially fatal sporotrichosis.
  • Blastomycosis — soil with decaying organic matter, mainly North America.
  • Both — treated with amphotericin B or an azole (ketoconazole).

Aspergillus

Aspergillus

A mold genus causing aspergillosis — inflammatory, granulomatous, necrotising respiratory disease with potential haematogenous spread to eye, skin, meninges; caused mainly by A. fumigatus, A. flavus and A. niger.

Disease by host EXAM HOT

An economically important disease due to high morbidity/mortality in brooder chicks.

Culture & species differentiation EXAM HOT

Grows well on SDA + chloramphenicol; cycloheximide must never be used (inhibits Aspergillus).

Pathogenicity

Acute aspergillosis (brooder pneumonia)
  • High morbidity/mortality in very young chicks
  • Loss of appetite, high temperature, listlessness, foetid diarrhoea, convulsions
  • Death within 24–48 h of onset
Chronic aspergillosis
  • Individual adult birds or few in a flock
  • Mild signs: anaemia, yellow faeces, respiratory rattle
  • Gradual decline, longer survival

Cattle: high humidity/temperature during hay/straw storage predisposes; A. fumigatus is the primary cause of mycotic abortion (also A. flavus, A. nidulans, A. niger, A. terreus, A. versicolor). Route: inhalation/ingestion → bloodstream → placenta → slow fungal placentitis (1–2 months) → foetal death/abortion (most commonly 6–7 months gestation) or chronic vaginitis/cervicitis/endometritis/infertility. Aborted foetus: raised ringworm-type skin lesions on head/neck; placenta thickened, haemorrhagic, oedematous, necrotic; cotyledons grey, intercotyledonary area leathery grey-tan. Necropsy: greyish/yellowish exudate with mycelia in lung/air sac; fungus ball in bronchi (more often A. niger than A. fumigatus).

Diagnosis

  • Confirmatory criteriaRepeated isolation, absence of other pathogens, recovery from unexposed tissue with hyphal demonstration — necessary because Aspergillus is a common lab/environmental contaminant.
  • Direct microscopyWet mount (sputum, nasal discharge, milk, uterine discharge, foetal stomach contents) with 10% KOH or LPCB.
  • ConfirmationIsolation from foetal stomach contents/placenta; slide culture demonstrates conidiospore/sterigmata pattern.
  • OtherAnimal pathogenicity test, allergic test.
Prevention & treatment

Reduce spore exposure, remove contamination sources, minimise stress; prophylactic 5-fluorocytosine inhibits inhaled spore germination. Vaccines (inactivated whole-cell filtrate, spores, mycelial fragments; some live-cell) are used in some settings. Treatment: amphotericin B, 5-fluorocytosine (oral, fungistatic); ketoconazole for cutaneous/GI aspergillosis.

Chapter recap
  • 3 key species — A. fumigatus, A. flavus, A. niger.
  • Never use cycloheximide — it inhibits Aspergillus growth on culture media.
  • A. fumigatus — primary cause of bovine mycotic abortion.
  • Fungus ball — bronchial colonisation, more often A. niger.
  • Confirmatory criteria — repeated isolation + no other pathogen + unexposed-tissue recovery, given its status as a common contaminant.
  • Brooder pneumonia — acute form, high mortality in young chicks within 24–48 h.
Chapter 11

Mycotoxins & Aflatoxicosis

Mycotoxins (Greek mykes = fungus + Latin toxicum = poison)

Low-molecular-weight, non-antigenic, heat-stable secondary fungal metabolites causing illness or death on ingestion — the resulting disease is termed mycotoxicosis.

History EXAM HOT

  • Middle AgesErgotism epidemics from Claviceps purpurea-infected grain first recorded; toxins identified in 1875.
  • 1942–47Alimentary toxic aleukia (ATA) outbreak from mouldy cereal grains.
  • 1960"Turkey X disease" — East Anglia, England; >100,000 turkey poults died; groundnut meal contaminated with A. flavus mycelia; toxic metabolites named aflatoxins after TLC identification.

Formation & fungal sources

>100 toxigenic fungal species; one fungus can make several toxins, and the same toxin can come from different fungi. Toxin production needs specific moisture/temperature/substrate/O2 conditions — e.g. Fusarium toxin at freezing temperature, A. flavus at 25 °C. Seed damage (insects, mechanical harvest, frost) predisposes to fungal attack.

Field fungi
  • Invade before harvest
  • Need higher water activity
  • e.g. Fusarium, Helminthosporium, Cladosporium
Storage fungi
  • Invade after harvest, during drying/storage
  • e.g. Aspergillus, Penicillium

Clinical features of mycotoxicosis

  • Not transmissible between in-contact animals — non-infectious, non-contagious.
  • Outbreaks often seasonal/sporadic, linked to specific feed batches or pasture types.
  • Early signs: decreased growth rate, immunosuppression.
  • Antibiotic treatment is typically ineffective.
  • Confirmatory diagnosis requires laboratory demonstration of the toxin in feed/tissue/secretions/excretions, supported by target-organ lesions.

Aflatoxicosis EXAM HOT

Produced by A. flavus/A. parasiticus on maize, cottonseed, groundnuts; favoured by moisture >15%, 25 °C, adequate aeration. 4 major aflatoxins — B1, B2 (blue fluorescence on TLC) and G1, G2 (green fluorescence); M1/M2 are hydroxylated B1/B2 metabolites excreted in milk.

Acute toxicity
  • Hepatic injury
  • Ataxia, convulsions
  • Sudden death possible
Chronic toxicity
  • Reduced feed conversion, weight gain, milk yield
  • Immunosuppression → intercurrent infection risk

Young pigs, calves, turkey poults, ducklings are highly susceptible; ducklings are the most sensitive avian species; sheep are highly resistant. Aflatoxin B1 is the most hepatogenic/carcinogenic/teratogenic/embryotoxic. Signs: calves — blindness, circling, teeth grinding, diarrhoea, tenesmus, convulsions; pigs — drowsiness, inappetance, jaundice, weight loss, yellow urine; ducklings — anorexia, poor growth, ataxia, opisthotonus, death; birds >3 weeks — subcutaneous leg/foot haemorrhages. Lesions: liver is the principal target — hepatomegaly, necrosis, bile-duct hyperplasia; acute cases show hepatic failure and haemorrhage from impaired clotting; chronic cases add renal degeneration and thymic cortical aplasia (impaired cell-mediated immunity).

Diagnosis

  • Chemical identification (TLC, HPLC — more sensitive) and biological toxicity assays.
  • >100 μg/kg aflatoxin B1 in feed is considered toxic for cattle.
  • Radioimmunoassay and ELISA also available.
  • Duckling bioassay (bile duct proliferation in 1-day-olds) and chick embryo bioassay are highly useful.
Control & prevention

Prevent contamination at production/storage/use stages. Decontamination: physical removal, chemical treatment (acids, alkalies, aldehydes, oxidising agents, ammonia gas). High-affinity preservatives (benzoic acid, propionic acid) are widely used for stored agricultural products.

Chapter recap
  • Turkey X disease, 1960 — the outbreak that led to the discovery of aflatoxins.
  • B/G naming — blue vs green fluorescence on TLC.
  • M1/M2 — the milk-excreted hydroxylated metabolites, a dairy public-health concern.
  • Ducklings — the most aflatoxin-sensitive species; sheep the most resistant.
  • Liver — the principal target organ of aflatoxin.
  • 100 μg/kg — the toxic threshold for aflatoxin B1 in cattle feed.
  • Not infectious/contagious — a key distinguishing feature of all mycotoxicoses.
