Chapter 01

History & Foundations of Immunology

Immunology

Branch of science dealing with the body's resistance to infection, or altered reactivity following exposure to a foreign substance. Immunity is the body's learned ability to resist infection; the immune system is the cells and molecules responsible; immune response is the reaction to a foreign antigen producing antibodies and lymphokines.

Ancient to early modern era EXAM HOT

  • 430 BCThucydides (Athens) first mentioned immunity to a plague-like infection.
  • 12th centuryChinese practised variolation — inhaling smallpox scab powder; deaths fell from 20% to 1%.
  • 18th centuryRinderpest control in Europe by inserting nasal-discharge-soaked string into a dewlap incision.
  • 1774Benjamin Jesty inoculated his wife with vaccinia virus against smallpox.
  • 1798Edward Jenner inoculated an 8-year-old boy with cowpox blister fluid, protecting him from smallpox — termed vaccination (vacca, Latin for cow). Jenner is the Father of Immunology.

Pasteur era

  • 1879Louis Pasteur discovered attenuated-culture immunity in chickens (Pasteurella multocida, fowl cholera) after an accidentally aged culture failed to kill.
  • 1881Pasteur publicly vaccinated sheep with heat-attenuated Bacillus anthracis, then challenged with virulent culture.
  • 1885Pasteur administered the first rabies vaccine to Joseph Meister (6 July 1885 — observed as Zoonosis Day); established the Pasteur Institute, Paris.
  • 1885Salmon (USA) showed heat-killed Bacillus suipestifer (now Salmonella choleraesuis) protects pigeons — first killed-vaccine demonstration.
  • 1888Roux & Yersin demonstrated bacterial toxins in diphtheria culture filtrate.
  • 1890Von Behring & Kitasato demonstrated toxin-antitoxin neutralisation and passive antibody transfer — Von Behring won the first Nobel Prize in Medicine, 1901.

Chapter 02

Theories of Immunity — Cellular vs Humoral

Two schools of thought explained Pasteur's successful vaccination results before the mechanism of immunity was understood, developing side by side in the late 19th century.

Cellular immunity theory EXAM HOT

  • 1882Elie Metchnikoff (Russian zoologist) observed motile cells surrounding a rose thorn in transparent starfish larvae.
  • 1883Observed fungal spores attacked by blood cells in Daphnia; extended to mammalian leukocytes engulfing microbes, terming this phagocytosis.

Metchnikoff proposed phagocytosis as the main infection defence, mediated by two circulating phagocyte types: microphages (polymorphonuclear leukocytes) and macrophages (larger cells). Some pathologists criticised the theory, citing immunity observed without cells; it regained study focus ~50 years later.

Humoral theory

  • 1886Fodor observed direct anthrax-bacillus killing action of immune serum.
  • 1888George Nuttall observed bactericidal blood action in certain animals.
  • 1889Hans Buchner showed cell-free serum is bactericidal, but loses effect after 55 °C/1 h heating; the heat-labile substance was termed alexin (later cytolysin, then complement).
  • 1890Von Behring & Kitasato demonstrated neutralising antitoxic serum activity against diphtheria/tetanus toxin — the first proof of humoral immunity.
Cellular theory (Metchnikoff)
  • Phagocytosis is the primary defence
  • Microphages (PMNs) and macrophages engulf microbes
  • Criticised: immunity seen without cells present
Humoral theory
  • Soluble blood/serum factors (antibody, alexin) mediate immunity
  • Serum bactericidal action lost on heating (55 °C/1 h)
  • Proven by antitoxin neutralisation and passive transfer
Key point

Both theories are correct and complementary — cellular (phagocytic) and humoral (antibody/complement-mediated) mechanisms operate together in innate and adaptive immunity; this dual framework underlies the modern classification of immune components.

Chapter recap
  • Metchnikoff, 1882-83 — starfish larva/Daphnia observations; coined "phagocytosis".
  • Two phagocyte types — microphages (PMNs) and macrophages.
  • Buchner, 1889 — named the heat-labile bactericidal serum factor "alexin" (= complement).
  • Heat lability test — 55 °C for 1 hour destroys complement activity.
  • Von Behring & Kitasato, 1890 — first proof of humoral immunity via antitoxin transfer.
  • Both theories developed simultaneously and are now understood as complementary.
Chapter 03

Thymus

Thymus

The first lymphoid organ to develop in mammals; a bilobed lympho-epithelial organ in the anterior mediastinum, developing from ectodermal invaginations (3rd bronchial cleft in humans).

Location & growth pattern EXAM HOT

In horse, cattle, sheep, pig and chicken it extends up the neck to the thyroid gland. Size increases through fetal and neonatal life, peaks at puberty, then atrophies (involutes) though remnants persist into old age.

Structure

Each lobe = lobules of loosely packed epithelial cells within a connective-tissue capsule, divided into outer cortex and inner medulla.

  • Cortex
    • Thymocytes (developing T cells) at various stages, reticular epithelial cells (stellate, desmosome-linked), few macrophages
    • Capillaries have thick basement membrane + continuous epithelial layer — the blood-thymus barrier excluding antigen
    • Immature T-lineage cells (yolk sac/bone marrow/fetal liver derived) enter via blood vessels and mature while migrating toward medulla
  • Medulla
    • Few epithelial (reticular) cells, occasional Hassall's corpuscles (whorled epithelial-cell remnants)
    • Mostly mature T cells bearing CD4+ or CD8+ markers
    • Only mature medullary T cells exit via efferent vessels to blood/peripheral lymphoid tissue

Selection & hormones EXAM HOT

During cortex-to-medulla migration, thymocytes contact epithelial cells, macrophages and dendritic cells, undergoing positive selection (imposes self-MHC restriction) and negative selection (central tolerance, removes self-reactive clones).

90-95%thymocytes die by apoptosis in the thymus
5-10%mature to CD4+ or CD8+ T cells

Thymic epithelial cells secrete hormones — thymopoietins, thymosins, thymulin, thymostimulins — driving maturation.

Functions

  • Differentiation of immature thymocytes into immunocompetent T lymphocytes.
  • Colonisation of secondary lymphoid organs (spleen, lymph nodes) by thymus-derived lymphocytes.
Chapter recap
  • First lymphoid organ to develop; bilobed, ectodermal origin.
  • Blood-thymus barrier — thick basement membrane + epithelial layer excludes antigen from cortex.
  • Positive selection = self-MHC restriction; negative selection = central tolerance.
  • 90-95% apoptosis rate — only 5-10% of thymocytes mature and exit.
  • Hassall's corpuscles — whorled epithelial remnants unique to medulla.
  • Involutes after puberty but remnants persist lifelong.
Chapter 04

Other Primary Lymphoid Organs

Primary lymphoid organs

Also called generative/central lymphoid organs — where lymphocytes differentiate and mature into immunocompetent cells without antigenic stimulation. Includes bone marrow, thymus, bursa of Fabricius, some Peyer's patches and fetal liver.

Bursa of Fabricius EXAM HOT

Found only in birds (mammalian GALT equivalents: Peyer's patches, colon/appendix lymphoid tissue, tonsil). A round (elongated in ducks) sac from the dorsal cloacal epithelial diverticulum, above the cloaca. Reaches maximum size by 3 weeks post-hatch, then involutes.

  • Structure: epithelial-lined sac connected to cloaca by a duct; folds (plicae) contain bursal follicles with a cortex (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages) and medulla.
  • Bursal microenvironment hormone: bursepoietin/bursin drives stem-cell differentiation into B cells.
  • B cells appear in bursa 12-15 days into embryonic development.
  • Not a pure primary organ — traps antigen and does some antibody synthesis; contains a small T-cell focus near the duct opening.

Peyer's patches EXAM HOT

Some ruminant intestinal PP function as primary lymphoid organs. Sheep have two types:

Bone marrow

Primary lymphoid organ for B-cell development except in ruminants/pigs (ileo-caecal PP). Site of all circulating blood cell generation in adults, including B-cell maturation. Embryonic haematopoiesis: yolk-sac blood islands + para-aortic mesenchyme → liver/spleen → bone marrow after birth. At puberty, occurs mainly in flat bones (sternum, vertebrae, iliac bone, ribs).

  • Red marrow: sponge-like reticular framework lined with endothelium between long trabeculae.
  • Spaces hold fat cells, stem cells, haematopoietic cells; precursors exit via vascular sinuses.
  • All blood cells except T lymphocytes are produced here.

Lymphoepithelial glands

Found throughout the large intestine/caecum of horses, dogs, pigs and other mammals — lymphoid aggregates surrounding elongated intestinal/caecal glands forming submucosal diverticula, resembling avian bursa. Rich in plasma cells, suggesting a major antibody-production site.

Chapter recap
  • Bursa of Fabricius — bird-only; site of B-cell maturation; bursin hormone drives differentiation.
  • Ileo-cecal PP — resembles avian bursa; disappears by 15 months in sheep.
  • Jejunal PP — persists lifelong, up to 30% T cells.
  • Bone marrow — B-cell primary organ except in ruminants/pigs (use ileo-cecal PP instead).
  • All blood cells except T cells arise from bone marrow.
  • Lymphoepithelial glands — large-intestine structures resembling bursa in horses, dogs, pigs.
Chapter 05

Lymph Nodes & Lymphocyte Circulation

Lymph nodes

Bean-shaped structures along lymphatic vessels, trapping antigen carried by lymph; covered by a fibrous capsule with outer cortex, para-cortical zone, and inner medulla, penetrated by lymphatic sinuses.

Structure EXAM HOT

  • Cortex — B-cell zone
    • Primary follicles (naïve mature B cells)
    • Secondary follicles with germinal centres — sites of B-cell somatic maturation, Ig class switching, memory-cell formation
    • Follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) trap antigen, present to B cells
    • ~70% of cortical T cells are CD4+ (helper)
  • Paracortex
    • T-cell homing zone; interdigitating dendritic cells capture/process/present antigen to T cells
  • Medulla
    • B cells, macrophages, reticular cells, plasma cells in cellular cords between sinuses
    • Activated T cells exit to circulation; activated B cells migrate to germinal centre/medulla and secrete antibody
Species variation

Pigs, elephants, hippopotamuses, rhinoceroses and dolphins have lymph nodes made of aggregated nodules with cortex centrally and medulla peripherally — the reverse of the usual arrangement.

Hemolymph nodes & dendritic cells

Hemolymph nodes resemble lymph nodes in ruminants and other mammals; function unclear. Dendritic cells occur in skin, respiratory tract, GI tract and most parenchymal organs epithelia; lobulated nuclei, clear cytoplasm with Birbeck granules; capture and transport antigen to peripheral lymphoid organs; all derive from bone marrow (lymphoid or myeloid precursor lineage), related to mononuclear phagocytes.

Lymphocyte circulation EXAM HOT

Blood lymphocytes are predominantly T cells, leaving blood by two routes:

  • Naïve T cells bind high endothelial venules (HEV) in the paracortex — tall rounded endothelial cells with discontinuous junctions (absent in sheep lymph nodes).
  • Memory T cells leave via conventional blood vessels in tissues, reaching nodes via afferent lymph.
90%of node-leaving lymphocytes entered via HEV
10%entered via afferent lymph

Lymph leaves via efferent lymphatics → large vessels → thoracic duct (largest lymph vessel, collects from lower body/intestine) → anterior vena cava. Thoracic duct cannulation drops blood/paracortical T cells sharply, confirming recirculation. In pigs, circulating lymphocytes enter nodes via HEV but leave the same way (not via lymphatics), so pig lymph has very few lymphocytes.

Chapter recap
  • Germinal centres — B-cell somatic maturation, class switching, memory formation.
  • Paracortex — T-cell zone, interdigitating dendritic cells.
  • Reversed cortex/medulla — seen in pigs, elephants, hippos, rhinos, dolphins.
  • Birbeck granules — characteristic dendritic-cell cytoplasmic granules.
  • HEV — route for 90% of node-leaving lymphocytes; absent in sheep nodes.
  • Thoracic duct — largest lymph vessel; cannulation confirms T-cell recirculation.
  • Pig lymph — nearly lymphocyte-free (both entry and exit via HEV).
Chapter 06

Spleen & Other Secondary Lymphoid Tissues

Spleen

Major site of immune response to blood-borne antigens (lymph nodes filter lymph; spleen filters blood). Removes antigen particles and aged blood cells; stores red cells/platelets; produces blood cells in the fetus.

Structure EXAM HOT

Covered by capsule with trabeculae extending into splenic pulp, divided into red and white pulp, supported by a reticular-cell/fibre meshwork. Single splenic artery pierces the capsule at the hilum, dividing into trabecular arteries.

  • White pulp
    • Periarterial lymphoid sheath (PALS) — T-cell cuff around trabecular (central) arterioles
    • B-cell follicles within PALS, some with germinal centres (Malpighian corpuscles)
    • Mantle zone — T-cell layer surrounding each follicle
  • Marginal zone
    • Separates white and red pulp; fine reticular meshwork; contains both T and B cells
  • Red pulp
    • Cords of reticular meshwork + sinuses; contains RBCs, macrophages, platelets, plasma cells
    • Filters RBCs/WBCs; site of extramedullary haematopoiesis
    • Destroys platelets/RBCs — termed hemocatharesis

Functions

  • Site for development of immune response (occurs in white pulp).
  • Site for haematopoiesis (lymphopoiesis and erythropoiesis).
  • Organ for filtration of RBCs and WBCs.
  • Traps antigen and mounts an immune response.

Cutaneous immune system

Epidermis: keratinocytes, melanocytes (both innate-immunity effectors), epidermal Langerhans cells, intraepithelial T cells (~2% of epidermal cells, majority CD8+). Langerhans cells capture antigen, migrate (chemokine-driven) to dermis then to lymph nodes via lymphatics. Dermis: CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, mainly perivascular, with scattered macrophages.

Mucosal immune system (MALT)

Defends GI, respiratory, genital tract and oral cavity mucosal surfaces. In the GI tract, lymphocytes occur in three regions: intraepithelial (mainly CD8+ T cells), lamina propria (scattered), and Peyer's patches of lamina propria. Follicles hold B cells (IgM at periphery, IgA in germinal centre), macrophages, dendritic cells; T cells predominate in inter-/para-follicular regions.

Bone marrow as a secondary organ

Probably the largest secondary lymphoid tissue. IV antigen is partly trapped here. During primary response, antibodies form largely in spleen/lymph node; late in the response, memory cells colonise bone marrow. On second antigen dose, bone marrow produces very large antibody quantities — the major source of IgG in adult rodents.

Chapter recap
  • PALS — T-cell sheath around the central (trabecular) artery in white pulp.
  • Marginal zone — separates red/white pulp, mixed T+B cells.
  • Hemocatharesis — red-pulp destruction of aged RBCs/platelets.
  • Langerhans cells — epidermal antigen-capturing DCs; migrate to lymph nodes.
  • GI lymphocyte sites — intraepithelial, lamina propria, Peyer's patches.
  • Bone marrow (secondary role) — major IgG source after memory-cell colonisation post-primary response.

Cells of the Non-Specific Immune System

Innate immune cells (myeloid lineage)

Non-specific immune response is the first line of defence; phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages) trap and destroy invading microorganisms.

Neutrophils EXAM HOT

First line of defence — "martyrs of the immune system"; recruited by chemotactic signals; macrophages follow 5-6 hours later. Major myeloid cell type; originate from bone marrow, ~2 weeks development; acquire cytoplasmic granules during myelocyte stage (polymorphonuclear, PMN). Circulate 10-12 h then move into tissues; total lifespan a few days; do not multiply. ~12 μm diameter, segmented nucleus.

Macrophages

Immature forms produced in bone marrow, released as monocytes (~5% blood leukocytes); mature into macrophages after tissue migration. Rarely divide; radiation-resistant; lifespan 3-4 months. 15-20 μm, bean-shaped/round nucleus, rich in mitochondria/lysosomes/RER/Golgi. Differentiate into: microglia (CNS), Kupffer cells (liver), alveolar macrophages (lung), osteoclasts (bone), splenic/peritoneal macrophages, dendritic cells (lymphoid organs), Langerhans cells (skin), mesangial cells (kidney).

  • Phagocytosis of foreign particles.
  • Antigen processing/presentation for the immune response.
  • Secretion of >100 bioreactive molecules: enzymes (lysozyme, collagenase, proteases, elastases), immune mediators (IL-1, interferon-α, complement components), inflammatory mediators (prostaglandins, thromboplastins, plasminogen activators).
  • Immune regulation — correct antigen presentation, dose regulation, monokine secretion.
  • Antitumour activity — TNF-α, hydrolytic enzymes, H2O2, superoxide, IL-1.
  • Wound healing — proteases clear damaged tissue; growth factors stimulate fibroblast collagen secretion and angiogenesis.

Eosinophils

Second major granulocyte, named for intense eosin staining. Bone marrow origin, mature in spleen; short lifespan — blood half-life ~30 min, tissue half-life ~12 days; tissue eosinophils ~500× blood count. Slightly larger than neutrophils, bilobed nucleus.

  • Primary granules — arylsulfatase, peroxidase, acid phosphatase.
  • Crystalloid granules — core of major basic protein + matrix (eosinophil cationic protein, eosinophil peroxidase, eosinophil-derived neurotoxin).
  • Function: extracellular parasite killing via antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) using Fc receptors specific for IgE; helminths resist neutrophil/macrophage lysosomal enzymes but not eosinophil major basic/cationic protein.
  • Participate in Type I hypersensitivity (IgE-mediated) tissue damage.

Basophils & mast cells

Basophils — bone marrow derived, smallest granulocyte, IgE Fc receptors, histamine-rich granules; not normally in extravascular tissue but may infiltrate under lymphocyte influence. Mast cells — bone marrow derived, resident cells essential for IgE-mediated inflammation; granules rich in histamine, heparin, TNF-α and other mediators, plus superoxide dismutase/peroxidase/acid hydrolases; scattered in connective tissue beneath skin, lung alveoli, GI/nasal mucosa.

Chapter recap
  • Neutrophil development — ~2 weeks in bone marrow; circulate only 10-12 h.
  • Primary vs secondary granules — azurophilic (defensins, MPO) vs specific (lysozyme, lactoferrin, collagenase).
  • Neutrophils cannot present antigen — unlike macrophages.
  • Macrophage differentiation — microglia, Kupffer cells, alveolar macrophages, osteoclasts etc.
  • Eosinophils — ADCC via IgE-Fc receptor, kill helminths with major basic protein.
  • Mast cells vs basophils — both IgE-bearing, histamine-rich; mast cells are tissue-resident, basophils circulate.
Chapter 08

Lymphocytes — B Cells & T Cells

Lymphocytes

Cells recognising foreign antigen and mounting immune response; small round cells (7-15 μm) with a large hematoxylin-staining nucleus and thin cytoplasmic rim. Originate from bone marrow stem cells, mature/differentiate into B and T cells in lymphoid organs; highly mobile, circulating blood ⇌ tissue ⇌ lymph.

B lymphocytes EXAM HOT

Origin: bone marrow multipotent stem cells; maturation in fetal liver (before birth), then bone marrow or Peyer's patches (mammals) or bursa (birds). Principal maturation event: Ig gene rearrangement/expression. Mature B cells are not antibody-secreting until antigenic stimulation differentiates them into plasma cells — large (2× lymphocyte size), eccentric nucleus, abundant RER, non-dividing, 2-3 day lifespan, secreting ~300 Ig molecules/second. Non-plasma-cell B cells become memory cells, some surviving >20 years without antigen.

Cluster of differentiation (CD) system

Surface markers identified by monoclonal antibodies, uniformly named by "cluster" designation (CD1, CD2...); >200 CD molecules defined; incompletely characterised ones are "workshop" candidates (CDw).

  • B-cell surface receptors: BCR (Ig + Igα + Igβ), immunoglobulin receptors, complement receptors, cytokine receptors, MHC Class II, CD19, CD20, CD21, CD40.

T lymphocytes

Progenitors originate in bone marrow, are attracted to thymus at any embryonic/post-embryonic time. Immature thymocytes populate the cortex, undergo TCR gene rearrangement, express CD3/TCR/CD4/CD8, migrate to medulla for maturation.

  • T-cell receptors (α/β and γ/δ) with CD3 complex.
  • CD2 — receptor for CD58; CD4 — helper T-cell marker; CD8 — cytotoxic T-cell marker.
  • CD71 — transferrin receptor; CD35 — complement receptor; CD25 — IL-2 receptor.
  • Adherence molecules — integrin family, selectin family.

Chapter 09

Innate Immunity — Barriers, Phagocytosis & NK Cells

Innate (non-specific/natural/native) immunity

First line of defence, independent of prior exposure. Components: physical/chemical barriers, biological barriers (phagocytes, NK cells), humoral factors (complement, inflammatory mediators), cytokines, genetic factors.

Physical & chemical barriers EXAM HOT

  • Skin — keratinised layer impermeable to microbes; dry environment limits growth; sweat lactic acid/salts inhibit bacteria/fungi; normal flora inhibits superinfection; sebaceous sebum protects; epithelial peptides act as natural antibiotics.
  • Mucous membrane — stratified squamous epithelium + mucus traps microbes; gastric HCl/enzymes kill microbes; intestinal cryptocidins (antimicrobial peptides); acidic vaginal pH kills microbes; tears contain lysozyme.

Biological barriers — phagocyte functions EXAM HOT

  1. RecruitmentChemotactic gradients (C5a, fibrinopeptide B, platelet factor 4) draw neutrophils/monocytes; endothelial adhesion molecules (triggered by LPS/cytokines) mediate rolling → diapedesis into tissue.
  2. RecognitionMannose-binding receptors (bind terminal mannose/fructose on bacterial glycoproteins), opsonin receptors (opsonisation = coating for phagocytosis; opsonic index = ratio of opsonin in diseased vs healthy serum), Toll-like receptors (TLRs, pattern recognition of microbial molecules).
  3. PhagocytosisPseudopod extension (actin/myosin), opsonin-receptor binding, engulfment forming a phagosome.
  4. Destruction — oxygen-dependentRespiratory burst (reactive oxygen intermediates, ROIs) via NADPH oxidase → superoxide → H2O2 → myeloperoxidase converts to microbicidal hypohalides; nitric oxide (via inducible NOS) forms further reactive radicals.
  5. Destruction — oxygen-independentPrimary granules fuse with phagosome (phagolysosome); proteases (elastase), lipases, nucleic-acid enzymes digest the microbe.
  6. Cytokine productionMonokines (macrophage-derived) or lymphokines (lymphocyte-derived); interleukins numbered chronologically IL-1 to IL-35; mediate/regulate innate and adaptive immunity, stimulate haematopoiesis, recruit/activate leukocytes.
Key point

Neutrophils circulate only ~6 h; undergo programmed cell death if not recruited within hours, cleared by liver/spleen resident macrophages. All mononuclear phagocytes (bone-marrow derived monocytes → tissue macrophages: histiocytes, Kupffer cells, microglia, alveolar/mesangial macrophages) form the reticuloendothelial (RE) system.

Natural killer (NK) cells EXAM HOT

5-20% of mononuclear cells in blood/spleen, rare elsewhere. Kill target cells without prior activation; neither T nor B lineage — no Ig or TCR expression. Large granular lymphocytes from bone marrow precursors.

  • Bear activating and inhibitory receptors — inhibitory dominates when both engaged, protecting normal host cells.
  • Infected cells often lose MHC Class I expression (viral immune evasion), removing inhibitory-receptor ligands → NK activation.
  • Granules contain perforin (forms membrane pores) and granzymes (enter pores, trigger apoptosis).
  • Kill virus-infected and intracellular bacteria-infected cells; IFN-γ from NK cells aids macrophage phagocytosis; participate in ADCC.

General barriers (genetic & physiological)

  • Species specificity — rinderpest affects cattle/sheep/goats, not humans; FMD virus does not infect horses or dogs.
  • Genetically resistant groups — e.g. African-descent individuals more TB-susceptible in America; white-line (B1) broiler chickens more susceptible to hydropericardium syndrome.
  • Cold-blooded animals resist tetanus toxin.
  • Avian body temperature (42 °C) restricts many pathogens; fever is beneficial.
  • Age — very young and old are most susceptible to infection.
Chapter recap
  • Opsonic index = ratio of opsonin, diseased vs healthy serum.
  • Respiratory burst — NADPH oxidase → ROIs, myeloperoxidase converts H2O2 to hypohalides.
  • RE system — all mononuclear phagocytes collectively (histiocytes, Kupffer, microglia etc).
  • NK cells — neither T nor B; kill via perforin + granzyme; inhibited by self-MHC I.
  • Loss of MHC I — the trigger that activates NK killing of infected cells.
  • Neutrophil circulation — only ~6 hours before recruitment or apoptosis.
  • Species/genetic barriers — rinderpest, FMD host restriction are classic examples.

Specific for microbial/non-microbial antigens, increased by repeated exposure. B-lymphocyte mediated = humoral immunity; T-lymphocyte mediated = cell-mediated immunity (CMI). Develops as a response and adapts to infection, unlike innate immunity.

Complement System

Complement system

A major humoral/innate effector mechanism — at least 19 heat-labile (56 °C/30 min) serum proteins, ~10% of the globulin fraction of serum, molecular weight 24 kDa (factor D) to 460 kDa (C1q).

Nomenclature & activation EXAM HOT

Numeric prefix C (C1-C9) or alphabetic (B, D, P); peptide fragments = lowercase suffix (C3a, C3b); enzymatically active complexes get a bar over the symbol. Proteins requiring proteolytic activation are zymogens; sequential activation = cascade reaction. Guinea pig serum (fresh, pooled) is the best complement source; haemolysed or tissue-damaged blood releases anticomplement factors and must be avoided for CFT. Mouse and horse complement is incomplete/poorly lytic.

Three activation pathways

  • Classical pathway — activated by antibody (IgM/IgG) bound to antigen
  • Alternative pathway — activated by microbial cell surface, no antibody needed
  • Lectin pathway — activated by plasma lectin bound to mannose residues

Classical pathway EXAM HOT

  1. C1 bindingC1 (C1q+C1r+C1s, Ca²⁺-linked) binds the CH3 domain of IgM or CH2 of IgG; ≥2 IgG Fc regions needed (IgM pentamer activates with one molecule — making IgM the more efficient complement fixer).
  2. C1 activationC1q binding to ≥2 Fc regions activates C1r, which cleaves/activates C1s (serine esterases); normally blocked by C1-INH, overcome once Ig binds antigen.
  3. C4 cleavageC1s cleaves C4 → C4a (released) + C4b (binds target surface).
  4. C3 convertase formationC2 binds C4b (Mg²⁺-dependent) → C4b2; C1s splits C2 into C2a+C2b (substrate modulation) → C4b2a = classical C3 convertase.
  5. C3 cleavageC4b2a cleaves C3 → C3a (released) + C3b (deposits on surface); can also feed the alternative pathway.
  6. C5 convertaseC4b2a3b = classical C5 convertase, cleaves C5, initiating the terminal pathway.

Alternative pathway

C3 spontaneously breaks to C3a+C3b; C3b binds covalently to cell surfaces. On mammalian (sialylated) cells, H factor binds C3b, activates I factor (protease), destroying C3b — no activation. On microbial surfaces (bacterial walls, LPS, viruses, aggregated IgA, cobra venom), C3b instead binds factor B; factor D cleaves bound B → Bb (+soluble Ba); C3bBb = alternative C3 convertase (half-life 5 min). Properdin (factor P) stabilises it to C3bBbP (half-life 30 min). C3bBb3b = alternative C5 convertase.

Lectin pathway

Macrophage ingestion stimulates IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α → hepatocytes secrete acute-phase mannose-binding protein (MBP/MBL), structurally similar to C1q, activating C1r-C1s (classical pathway) via MBP-associated serine esterase. Cattle/buffalo have additional mannose-binding proteins including conglutinin, which clumps C3b-coated particles.

Terminal pathway EXAM HOT

Any C5 convertase cleaves C5 → C5a (released) + C5b (binds C3b) → binds C6+C7 → C5b67 (detaches, inserts into nearby lipid bilayer) → binds C8 + 12-18 molecules of C9 → membrane attack complex (MAC), C5b678(9)n — a transmembrane doughnut-shaped pore causing osmotic lysis.

Antigen — Properties & Antigenicity

Antigen

A substance that, introduced into a susceptible animal's tissues, stimulates formation of specific antibody and/or lymphokines with which it reacts specifically and observably.

Immunogenicity vs antigenicity EXAM HOT

  • Immunogenicity — ability to induce a humoral and/or cell-mediated immune response; such material = immunogen.
  • Antigenicity — ability of a molecule to be recognised by antibody or lymphocyte.
  • All immunogens are antigenic, but not all antigens are immunogenic.

Factors determining antigenicity EXAM HOT

  • Molecular size
    • Larger molecular weight → better antigen (e.g. ovalbumin, 40,000 Da)
    • Exception: glucagon (2,600 Da) is a good antigen
    • Penicillin/aspirin (<1,000 Da) are poor antigens
  • Complexity
    • Complex proteins > repeating polymers (lipids, carbohydrates, nucleic acids)
    • Bacterial exotoxins, egg albumin — powerful; haemoglobin — weak; protamines/histones/gelatin — almost devoid
    • Complex carbohydrate bound to protein is more immunogenic (e.g. Gram-negative cell-wall antigens)
  • Shape
    • Fixed shape → higher antibody response; highly flexible molecules are poor antigens
  • Stability
    • Antigen must be stable/rigid — gelatin is poor unless cross-linked by tyrosine/tryptophan residues
    • Flagellin is weak; stability/antigenicity enhanced by polymerisation
  • Degradability
    • Must be degradable to trigger response — stainless steel, plastic (inert polymers) are not antigenic
  • Foreignness
    • Antigenicity ∝ degree of foreignness — same-species < related-species < unrelated-species antigens
    • Self-antigens normally don't provoke antibody; exceptions = autoantibodies
  • Genotype of recipient
    • Immune-response genetic control confined to MHC genes — MHC products present processed antigen to T cells
  • Dosage & route
    • Insufficient dose fails to activate enough lymphocytes; excessive dose can induce non-responsive state; boosters needed for strong response; parenteral routes preferred
  • Adjuvants
    • Enhance immunogenicity — prolong antigenic persistence, enhance co-stimulation, induce granuloma, non-specifically stimulate lymphocyte proliferation

Adjuvant examples

Alum, aluminium hydroxide, bacterial LPS, saponin, IL-12, Montanide, Freund's adjuvant. Freund's incomplete adjuvant = mineral oil + emulsifier (mannide monooleate); Freund's complete adjuvant = incomplete + heat-killed Mycobacteria — more potent.

Hapten EXAM HOT

Hapten

Usually non-protein, low-MW substance with little/no antigenicity alone, but antigenic when coupled to a carrier protein — a partial antigen (Greek haptein, "to fasten"). Cannot induce antibody formation alone but reacts specifically with existing antibody.

Test chemical (e.g. aniline, p-aminobenzoic acid) is diazotized and coupled to a protein forming a conjugated antigen/azoprotein. Complex hapten = high MW, polyvalent — forms visible precipitation with antibody against the complete antigenic complex. Simple hapten = low MW, univalent — no visible precipitation. Examples: penicillin, dinitrophenyl (DNP).

Epitopes (antigenic determinants) EXAM HOT

The smallest unit of antigenicity — specific chemical groupings/spatial configurations on the antigen surface, sensitising immunocytes and reacting with the complementary antibody/TCR site. The antibody-side combining region = paratope/idiotope; the T-cell recognition site = agretope. Most epitopes span 6-10 amino acids (T-cell epitopes) or 10-15 amino acids (B-cell epitopes).

Chapter recap
  • All immunogens are antigens; not all antigens are immunogens.
  • MW <1000 Da — penicillin, aspirin — usually poor antigens; glucagon (2600 Da) is an exception.
  • 9 factors govern antigenicity — size, complexity, shape, stability, degradability, foreignness, genotype, dose/route, adjuvants.
  • Freund's complete = incomplete + heat-killed Mycobacteria.
  • Hapten — antigenic only when carrier-conjugated; complex (visible precipitate) vs simple (no precipitate).
  • Paratope — antibody combining site; Agretope — T-cell recognition site.
  • Epitope size — 6-10 aa (T cell) vs 10-15 aa (B cell).

Types of Antigen, Blood Groups & MHC

Classification by antigenic specificity EXAM HOT

  • Species-specific — specific to one host species
  • Organ-specific — specific to one organ within a species (e.g. kidney protein differs from lung)
  • Heterophile antigens — identical antigens shared across bacterial species and different animal tissues; antibody to one cross-reacts with the other
    • Forssman antigens — glycoprotein in cell walls of Streptococcus, Shigella, Salmonella, Clostridium and on RBCs of horses, sheep, cats, mice; hosts bearing them don't make antibody (self-recognition)
    • Antigen in Proteus strains OX-19, OX-2, OX-K and Rickettsial organisms — basis of the Weil-Felix reaction
  • Alloantigens (isoantigens) — antigenic to other individuals of the same species; corresponding antibodies = alloantibodies (e.g. blood group, transplantation antigens)
  • Syngenic antigen — present in individuals of identical genetic makeup (e.g. monozygotic twins)
  • Autoantigen — sequestered self-tissue that becomes antigenic if released into circulation (e.g. sperm, lens protein)
  • Superantigen — activates T cells without APC processing (e.g. streptococcal A antigen)

By T-cell dependency

T-dependent antigen
  • Complex (RBC, serum proteins)
  • Rapidly metabolised
  • Requires T-cell help; produces memory + class switching
T-independent antigen
  • LPS, pneumococcal polysaccharide, flagellin, lectins (Con A, PHA, pokeweed)
  • Repeating-unit composition, slowly metabolised
  • No memory or class switching

Microbial antigens

  • Soluble — toxins, enzymes excreted into environment.
  • Cellular — bacterial: Somatic (O) antigen (LPS-protein, highly variable, common core has vaccine potential); Capsular (K) antigen (polysaccharide, e.g. E. coli K; or polypeptide, e.g. poly-D-glutamic acid in B. anthracis); Flagellar (H) antigen (flagellin protein); Fimbrial (F) antigen; Spore antigen (exosporidium); also porins, heat-shock proteins, exotoxins.
  • Viral — structural VP (viral proteins), capsid protein, envelope (lipoprotein+glycoprotein), e.g. HN glycoprotein of Newcastle disease virus.
  • Non-microbial — food substances, pollen grains, fungal spores, dust particles (allergic reactions).

Cell surface antigens

Important types: blood group antigen, MHC molecules, cluster of differentiation (CD). Blood transfusion became scientifically feasible after Landsteiner (1900) discovered blood groups. Most blood group antigens are glycoproteins/glycolipids, integral cell-membrane components; do not participate in antigen processing but influence graft rejection.

Antibody Structure

Antibody

A specific soluble glycoprotein shed by B cells into surrounding fluid after antigenic stimulation, binding specifically to antigen and working toward its destruction/elimination; most concentrated in serum.

Nature EXAM HOT

  • 1938Tiselius & Kabat electrophoresed immune serum into 4 fractions: albumin, α, β, γ globulins; most antibody is in the γ fraction (some in β).

Immunoglobulins constitute ~20% of plasma proteins. Ultracentrifuge sedimentation: most antibody at 7S (MW 150,000 Da), some at 19S (MW 900,000 Da) — S = Svedberg unit, a sedimentation constant of 1×10⁻¹³ seconds. Immunoglobulin is the structural/functional term; antibody is the biological/functional term. Five classes exist based on physicochemical/antigenic differences: IgG, IgM, IgA, IgD, IgE.

Basic structure EXAM HOT

An Ig monomer is ~160 kDa, composed of 2 pairs of peptide chains: heavy (H) chains (50-60 kDa) and light (L) chains (25 kDa) — both pairs identical. H-chain carboxy termini anchor into the B-cell membrane. L chains (half the H-chain length) link to H chains near the amino terminus via disulphide bonds, giving the classic Y shape; two H chains are also disulphide-linked to each other.

  • Fc region — the "tail" of the Y (crystallisable fragment); attaches to the B-cell membrane; binds complement/Fc receptors
  • Fab region — the "arms"; bind antigen at a groove formed between L and H chain N-termini

Heavy chains

5 types denoted by Greek letters — α, δ, ε, γ, μ — determining Ig class (IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, IgM respectively). α and γ chains ~450 amino acids; μ and ε chains ~550 amino acids. Each H chain has a constant region (identical within isotype) and a variable region (~110 aa, single Ig domain, differs by B-cell clone). γ, α, δ chains have a 3-domain constant region + hinge; μ and ε chains have a 4-domain constant region.

Light chains EXAM HOT

Either κ (kappa) or λ (lambda), never both in one Ig molecule (named after Koragold and Lapori). Each L chain = 2 domains of 110 aa: C-terminus constant (CL), N-terminus variable (VL) with hypervariable/complementarity-determining regions (CDRs or "hot spots", 6-10 aa each, at positions 24-34, 50-56, 89-97) separated by framework regions. MW of each L chain = 25 kDa.

Hinge region & digestion EXAM HOT

The Fab regions swing around the central molecule at the hinge region (~12 aa, between CH1 and CH2, unique per class/subclass, hydrophilic, proline-rich). μ and ε chains lack a hinge region; IgD's hinge lacks cysteine (no interchain links there).

Papain digestion
  • Cleaves at the hinge region
  • Produces 2 Fab fragments (3.5S) + 1 Fc fragment
Pepsin digestion
  • Cleaves toward the carboxy side of the disulphide bond
  • Produces F(ab)2 fragment (5S, both arms held together) + Fc fragment digested into multiple pieces

  • 7S (150 kDa) vs 19S (900 kDa) — sedimentation classes of antibody.
  • 5 heavy-chain types — α, δ, ε, γ, μ define IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, IgM.
  • κ or λ light chain — never both in one molecule; species ratio varies markedly.
  • CDRs — hypervariable "hot spots" at 3 positions in the VL domain.
  • Papain → 2 Fab + 1 Fc; Pepsin → F(ab)2 + digested Fc.
  • μ and ε chains — the two heavy chains lacking a hinge region.

The five immunoglobulin isotypes share the basic Y structure but differ in heavy chain, valency, and biological role.

Two γ heavy chains + κ or λ light chains; Fc has CH1/CH2/CH3 domains. Smallest Ig — escapes blood vessels into inflamed tissue; found in almost all tissue fluids except CSF. Only Ig crossing the placenta — provides passive newborn immunity.

IgM (macroglobulin) EXAM HOT

Secreted by plasma cells (spleen, bone marrow, lymph nodes); 19S (900 kDa), ~5-8% of Ig, 2nd-highest serum concentration. On the B-cell surface, IgM is a single monomer (180 kDa); secreted IgM is a pentamer (occasionally hexamer) — 5 monomers linked circularly by disulphide bonds and a cysteine/asparagine-rich J chain (15 kDa, 118-125 aa).

  • 80% intravascular; theoretical valence 10, practically ~5 (steric hindrance).
  • 2-mercaptoethanol (0.12 M) disrupts the pentamer into monomers — differentiates IgM from IgG.
  • First antibody in primary response; shorter half-life than IgG; high avidity.
  • 500-1000× more effective than IgG in opsonisation, 100× in bactericidal action, 20× in agglutination; a single IgM molecule causes haemolysis vs 1,000 IgG needed.
  • Complement-binding site: CH4 and CH3 domains.

IgA

Produced by plasma cells of intestinal/respiratory/urinary tract, mammary gland, skin. Major Ig in colostrum, saliva, tears; sedimentation 7S-11S, 160-360 kDa; 2 α heavy chains (3 constant domains) + κ or λ light chains.

  • Idiotypes — structural variation in the variable-domain amino acid sequence of heavy/light chains.
  • Bence Jones proteins — excess immunoglobulin light chains in myeloma-patient urine; precipitate on warming, redissolve at higher temperature.
Chapter recap
  • IgG — only placenta-crossing Ig; ~80% of total Ig.
  • IgM pentamer — J chain-linked; disrupted by 2-mercaptoethanol; first primary-response antibody.
  • SIgA — dimer + secretory component; protects against GI proteolysis.
  • IgE — reaginic antibody, Type I hypersensitivity, antiparasitic; does not fix complement.
  • IgD — function unclear, absent in some species, no interchain disulphide bonds.
  • IgY — the avian analogue of mammalian IgG.
  • Bence Jones protein — free light chains in myeloma urine.

Theories of Antibody Production

B lymphocytes recognise antigen and differentiate into memory cells and antibody-secreting plasma cells. Theories fall into two broad categories: selective theory (genetic information for antibody pre-exists before antigen exposure) and instructive theory (antigen instructs synthesis of complementary antibody).

Ehrlich's side chain theory (selective) EXAM HOT

Paul Ehrlich (1900): cells bear surface receptors ("side chains") used for nutrient absorption; foreign antigen binds and inactivates the complementary receptor; as compensation, the cell over-produces and releases the same receptor type as circulating antibody. Abandoned once Landsteiner showed antibodies form against synthetic chemicals never encountered in nature.

Template hypothesis (instructive)

  • Direct template hypothesis — Breinl & Haurowitz, 1930
    • Antigen enters the antibody-forming cell, causing configurational change
    • Antigen acts as a mould; antibody synthesised in direct contact, then dissociates as a mirror-image complement
  • Indirect template theory — Burnet & Fenner, 1949
    • Antigen enters the antibody-producing cell, induces a heritable genomic change (persistent "indirect template" in progeny)
    • Explained specificity/secondary response but abandoned once different-specificity antibodies were shown to have different amino acid sequences at combining sites

Natural selection theory

Jerne (1955): ~1 million globulins of all specificities pre-exist from embryonic life; antigen binds the complementary one, and the complex homes to the antibody-producing cell, stimulating synthesis of that antibody type. Failed to explain immunological memory.

Clonal selection theory EXAM HOT

Burnet (1957) — the currently accepted theory; Medawar & Burnet won the Nobel Prize in 1960.

  • Immunocompetent lymphocytes bear surface antibody receptors of differing specificity — each B cell recognises only one antigen, synthesising only one Ig type.
  • Antigen stimulates the matching lymphocyte clone to proliferate and differentiate into memory cells and antibody-secreting plasma cells.
  • Different determinants stimulate different clones, producing different memory/plasma cell populations.
  • Diversity arises through somatic mutation; self-reactive clones eliminated during embryonic development ("forbidden clones") — persistence causes autoimmune disease.

Network theory

Jerne postulated a mechanism for antibody-response regulation (Nobel Prize, 1984): variable-region amino-acid sequences at the antigen-binding site and adjacent path = idiotypes. Idiotypes can act as antigen, provoking anti-idiotype antibodies, forming a regulatory idiotype network.

Genetic basis of antibody diversity EXAM HOT

An individual can produce ≥10⁸ different antibody molecules — impossible via continuous codon arrays given human DNA's ~6.6×10⁹ nucleotides. Genetic information exists in discontinuous stretches: non-expressed introns separate peptide-coding exons. Susumu Tonegawa won the 1987 Nobel Prize explaining these gene rearrangements.

  • Ig molecule = V (variable), C (constant), J (joining) domains/segments.
  • L-chain V region: encoded by V and J segments. H-chain V region: encoded by V, D and J segments.
  • Limited C-segment number per species, transmitted in the germ line.
Germ line theory
  • Germ cells carry structural genes for all VL/VH chains
  • Diversity arises via gene duplication, mutation, selection
Somatic mutation theory
  • Germ cells contain few genes (e.g. human Vκ=3, Vλ=5)
  • Diversification via somatic mutation during embryonic development
  • Germ-line V genes suppress self-antigen antibody production; mutation drives affinity maturation against non-self

Isotype switching

On re-exposure, IgM-secreting cells switch to producing IgG/IgA of the same specificity via a switch (S) sequence enabling any constant segment to combine with VH-DH-JH. First exposure: VH-DH-JH-Cμ → IgM. Subsequent exposure: irreversible S-sequence rearrangement (deletion of μ/δ segments) → VH-DH-JH-Cγ → IgG, same variable segment, new constant segment.

Chapter recap
  • Ehrlich, 1900 — side chain theory; abandoned after Landsteiner's synthetic-antigen findings.
  • Burnet & Fenner, 1949 — indirect template theory (instructive).
  • Jerne, 1955 — natural selection theory; failed to explain memory.
  • Burnet, 1957 — clonal selection theory, the accepted model; Nobel Prize 1960.
  • Forbidden clones — self-reactive clones normally eliminated in embryonic life.
  • Jerne's network theory — idiotype/anti-idiotype regulation; Nobel Prize 1984.
  • Tonegawa, 1987 Nobel Prize — explained gene rearrangement/antibody diversity via V-D-J segments.
  • Isotype switching — same VH-DH-JH segment, new constant (C) segment.

Monoclonal Antibodies

Monoclonal antibody

Antibody produced by a single plasma-cell clone against one antigenic determinant — unlike natural infection, which produces polyclonal antibodies against the multiple epitopes of a natural antigen.

Hybridoma technology EXAM HOT

Continuous monoclonal antibody production is achieved by fusing antibody-producing plasma cells with immortal myeloma cells, forming hybridomas — somatic cell hybrids retaining both antibody production capacity and indefinite myeloma-cell multiplication. First described by Köhler & Milstein (1975).

Production steps

  1. ImmunisationMouse immunised repeatedly against the antigen of interest to ensure a good response; spleen removed 2-4 days after the last dose, disaggregated into single-cell suspension.
  2. FusionSpleen cells suspended with non-Ig-secreting mouse myeloma cells (e.g. MOPC 21, MPC 11, SP2/0, all BALB/c-derived); polyethylene glycol (PEG) induces fusion (~1 fusion per 200,000 spleen cells).
  3. Selection — HAT mediumFused-cell mixture cultured in hypoxanthine + aminopterin + thymidine (HAT). Aminopterin blocks the normal (de novo) nucleotide pathway; unfused myeloma cells lack HGPRT and thymidine kinase, so they die; hybridomas possess both enzymes (from the spleen-cell parent) and survive via the salvage pathway, using hypoxanthine/thymidine from the medium.
  4. Screening & cloningHybridomas double every 24-48 h; ~300-500 different hybrids/spleen, not all antibody-specific; supernatant checked after 2-4 weeks culture (~1 hybrid per 3 wells at 50,000 myeloma cells/well); positive clones sub-cloned to confirm single-clone antibody production, then mass-cultured.
  5. StorageHybridomas can lose antibody-producing ability with repeated culturing — stocks are aliquoted and cryopreserved.

Applications EXAM HOT

  • Immunodiagnostics — specific viral, bacterial, parasitic disease diagnosis, e.g. Mab-based C-ELISA kits for bluetongue disease antibody detection in sheep.
  • Tumour diagnosis & therapy — tumour-specific MAbs for imaging and immunotherapy.
  • HLA typing — anti-HLA MAbs, useful in forensic study and tissue typing.
  • Being homogeneous, MAb-based assays require shorter incubation periods than polyclonal assays.
Chapter recap
  • Köhler & Milstein, 1975 — first described hybridoma technology.
  • Hybridoma — spleen (antibody) cell + myeloma (immortal) cell fusion.
  • HAT medium selection — aminopterin blocks de novo synthesis; only fused hybrids survive via HGPRT/TK salvage pathway.
  • Non-Ig-secreting myeloma lines — required to avoid interfering with monoclonal output.
  • ~1 fusion per 200,000 spleen cells — typical fusion efficiency.
  • Sub-cloning — confirms single-clone origin before mass culture.
  • Applications — immunodiagnostics (e.g. bluetongue C-ELISA), tumour imaging/therapy, HLA typing.

Primary Binding Tests

Antigen-antibody interaction

Non-covalent binding — hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces — individually weaker than covalent bonds, so many are needed for strong binding.

Key concepts EXAM HOT

Association equilibrium
Ag + Ab ⇌ Ag·Ab (K1 forward, K-1 reverse)
  • Affinity (Ka) = K1/K-1 — combined strength of a single Ag-binding site with a single epitope
  • Avidity — strength when multiple epitopes interact with multiple antibody binding sites

Serology = the diagnostic measurement of Ag-Ab interactions. Serological techniques fall into 3 categories: primary binding tests (directly measure Ag-Ab binding), secondary binding tests (measure in-vitro results of Ag-Ab interaction — less sensitive, easier), tertiary binding tests (measure protective action in the animal — most complex).

Radioimmunoassay (RIA) EXAM HOT

Highly sensitive; first developed by Berson & Yalow (1960s) to measure insulin-anti-insulin complexes in diabetics.

  • Competitive RIA (antigen detection) — unlabelled antigen displaces radiolabelled antigen (tritium, C-14, I-125) from immune complexes; supernatant radioactivity rises as unlabelled antigen competes for binding sites; a standard curve from known unlabelled-antigen amounts quantifies the test sample.
  • RIA for antibody detection — antigen-coated cellulose discs immersed in test serum, then in radiolabelled antiglobulin; bound radioactivity indicates antibody level. Costly, health-hazard equipment limits small-lab use.
  • RAST (radioallergosorbent test) — measures specific IgE against a solid-phase allergen. RIST (radioimmunosorbent test) — measures total serum IgE via solid-phase anti-IgE.

Immunofluorescence assays EXAM HOT

Fluorescent dye labels: FITC (fluorescein isothiocyanate, yellow, re-emits green/yellow-green at 525 nm) is most common; rhodamine (deep red, 546 nm) and phycoerythrin (red) also used.

Similar principle to RIA but uses an enzyme label rather than radioisotope; enzyme + chromogenic substrate → coloured product. Common enzymes: alkaline phosphatase (substrate PNPP), horseradish peroxidase (substrates OPD, TMB, ABTS, 5-AS, DAB — DAB gives an insoluble product), β-galactosidase (substrate ONPG).

  • Direct ELISA — antigen on solid phase, enzyme-labelled antibody added directly, then substrate
  • Indirect ELISA — antigen on solid phase, test (primary) antibody, then enzyme-labelled secondary anti-species antibody
  • Sandwich ELISA — capture antibody immobilised, antigen sample added, then enzyme-linked detecting antibody (double sandwich = detecting antibody from a different species + anti-species conjugate)
  • Competitive ELISA — competing test antibody vs pretitrated specific antibody for solid-phase antigen; decreased colour = antibody present in test serum
  • Avidin-biotin ELISA — biotin binds antibody without affecting activity; avidin (enzyme-conjugated) binds biotin very strongly/specifically
  • Dot ELISA — nitrocellulose membrane solid phase, DAB substrate; brown dot = positive; field-usable, visually read, retrospectively storable

Immunoperoxidase test (IPT) & Western blotting

IPT — detects tissue-section antigen (similar to FAT/ELISA); enzyme-labelled Ig + DAB substrate; brown deposit = positive; visualised by light microscopy (unlike FAT), usable on paraffin/resin sections; disadvantage — endogenous peroxidase can cause false positives.

Western blotting (immunoblotting) — identifies a specific protein in a complex mixture, named for its similarity to Southern (DNA) and Northern (mRNA) blotting. Protein mixture separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nylon/nitrocellulose membrane, then flooded with radiolabelled or enzyme-linked antibody specific for the target protein; visualised by autoradiography (radiolabel) or chromogenic substrate (enzyme label). Can also identify a specific antibody in a mixture.

  • Berson & Yalow — developed RIA for insulin-antibody complexes.
  • RAST vs RIST — specific-allergen IgE vs total IgE.
  • FITC — 525 nm green/yellow-green fluorescence, the standard IF label.
  • Indirect FAT is brighter than direct — multiple antiglobulin binding per primary antibody.
  • DAB substrate — forms an insoluble brown product, used in Dot ELISA and IPT.
  • Western blotting — named for analogy to Southern (DNA)/Northern (RNA) blotting.

Precipitation & Agglutination Tests

Secondary binding tests

Antigen-antibody binding followed by a visible second reaction — precipitation (soluble antigen), agglutination (particulate antigen), or cell lysis (if complement is activated on a cell surface).

Precipitation test EXAM HOT

Antibodies aggregating soluble antigens = precipitins. Requirements: antibody must be bivalent; antigen must be bivalent/polyvalent (≥2 copies of an epitope, or multiple different epitopes for polyclonal antisera). Zone effects: antibody excess — each antigen molecule coated, no cross-linking, no precipitate; equivalence zone — extensive lattice formation, precipitate maximal; antigen excess — each antibody binds 2 antigen molecules, complexes stay small/soluble, no precipitate. (Horse antibodies show a distinct flocculation over a narrow antigen range, due to the IgG(T) subclass.)

Precipitation test types

  • Precipitin ring test
    • Antigen layered over antiserum without mixing; precipitation at the proper ratio interphase
    • Example: Ascoli's test for anthrax diagnosis
  • Gel diffusion (AGID/AGPT) — agar retards diffusion, allowing multiple distinct precipitin lines
    • Single diffusion, 1 dimension — Oudin test (antibody in agar column, antigen layered above)
    • Double diffusion, 1 dimension — Oakley-Fulthorpe test (antibody agar, then plain agar layer, then antigen)
    • Single diffusion, 2 dimensions — Mancini test (antibody in agar plate, antigen in wells, ring size quantifies antigen)
    • Double diffusion, 2 dimensions — Ouchterlony test (antigen/antibody in separate wells, meeting lines interpreted below); Coggins test for equine infectious anaemia uses this format

Immunoelectrophoresis variants

  • Immunoelectrophoresis — antigen mixture electrophoresed in agar, antiserum in a parallel trough diffuses laterally; curved precipitate arcs form, one per antigen constituent; resolves normal serum into 25-40 bands (identifies absent/excess proteins, e.g. myeloma).
  • Rocket electrophoresis — antigen electrophoretically driven into antiserum-containing agar (vs passive Mancini diffusion); rocket length ∝ antigen amount.
  • Counter immunoelectrophoresis (CIE) — gammaglobulins migrate cathodically while most proteins move anodically; applying voltage drives antigen and antibody together — faster, more sensitive than double diffusion; rapid multi-disease diagnosis.
  • Two-dimensional immunoelectrophoresis — electrophoretically resolved protein strip placed on one plate edge, antiserum-agarose poured over the rest, antigens forced into antibody layer by a second electrophoresis run.

Agglutination tests EXAM HOT

Particulate-antigen/antibody binding causes visible clumping (agglutination); antibodies causing it = agglutinins; IgM is more efficient than IgG. Requires correct Ag:Ab proportion (zone of equivalence); excess antibody inhibits it — the prozone effect (from over-coating of particles, or non-agglutinating "incomplete" antibodies with restricted hinge flexibility or deep-lying epitopes). Post-zone effect occurs with antigen excess. Direct antiglobulin test detects incomplete antibodies on particle surfaces.

Prerequisites & applications

  • Particles >200-250 nm, stable in suspension ≥48 h; antibody targeted to the surface antigen; optimal Ag:Ab proportion; electrolyte/buffer present.
  • Bacteria such as Brucella, Salmonella, E. coli, Proteus form smooth suspensions, agglutinated by antibody to flagellar/capsular/cell wall antigen — used for both antibody detection and strain identification (slide and tube agglutination tests).

Passive & latex agglutination

Passive haemagglutination — soluble antigen adsorbed onto tannic acid/chromium chloride-treated RBCs, agglutinated by specific antibody; used for Pasteurellosis-vaccination antibody titres. Latex agglutination — latex beads coated with antigen or antibody; clumping in positive samples, readable within a minute.

Viral haemagglutination & bifunctional antibodies

Some viruses agglutinate mammalian/avian RBCs (e.g. Newcastle disease, egg drop syndrome, canine parvovirus, rabies virus); specific antibody inhibits this — haemagglutination inhibition (HI), used for virus ID and antibody titre. Bifunctional antibodies — made by joining two different Ig halves (broken at the H-H bond); e.g. anti-dog-RBC half + anti-heartworm half cross-links Dirofilaria antigen to RBCs, giving visible haemagglutination in infected-dog blood within minutes.

Chapter recap
  • Ascoli's test — precipitin ring test for anthrax.
  • Ouchterlony test — identity/non-identity/partial-identity line patterns.
  • Coggins test — gel diffusion test for equine infectious anaemia.
  • Prozone effect — antibody excess prevents visible agglutination.
  • IgM > IgG in agglutination efficiency (as in complement fixation).
  • Rocket electrophoresis — rocket length is proportional to antigen amount.
  • Bifunctional antibody — links heartworm antigen to canine RBCs for diagnostic haemagglutination.

Complement Fixation & Living-System Assays

Complement Fixation Test (CFT)

Uses complement-mediated RBC lysis as a readout for antibody presence — a classic secondary binding test for many viral and bacterial diseases (e.g. FMD in animals, Japanese encephalitis in man).

CFT procedure EXAM HOT

  1. Heat-inactivate test serum56 °C/30 min destroys the serum's own complement.
  2. First incubationAntigen + heat-inactivated test serum + normal guinea pig serum (complement source) incubated together.
  3. Indicator systemAntibody-coated sheep RBCs added to measure remaining free complement.
  4. Read resultLysis (clear red solution) = negative — complement was not activated/fixed, so no antibody was present. No lysis (cloudy suspension/button) = positive — complement was consumed (fixed) by the Ag-Ab-complement reaction.

Assays in living systems

If an organism/antigen has measurable biological activity, antibody can be quantified by its ability to neutralise that activity (haemolysis, cell lysis, disease, or death) — subject to high variability.

  • Neutralization test — estimates antibody's ability to neutralise antigen's biological activity in vitro; used for bacterial toxins (e.g. Clostridium perfringens, Staphylococcus toxins) and viruses (antibody blocks critical cell-attachment sites); highly specific and extremely sensitive.
  • Protection test — an in-vivo form of neutralisation; specific antiserum given at increasing dilutions to test animals, then challenged with a standard pathogen/toxin dose; requires large animal numbers for meaningful results.
Key point

Guinea pig serum (fresh, preferably pooled) is the standard CFT complement source. Blood for serum separation must avoid haemolysis or tissue damage, which release anticomplementary factors and invalidate the test.

Chapter recap
  • CFT logic — lysis (negative) means complement was NOT consumed; no lysis (positive) means it WAS consumed by Ag-Ab binding.
  • 56 °C/30 min — heat-inactivation step for the test serum before CFT.
  • Guinea pig serum — the standard external complement source.
  • Neutralization test — measures antibody's ability to block biological/toxic activity in vitro.
  • Protection test — the in-vivo tertiary-binding equivalent of neutralisation.
  • CFT applications — FMD (animals), Japanese encephalitis (humans).

Type I Hypersensitivity

Hypersensitivity

Specific, acquired altered reactivity to a foreign substance producing deleterious tissue effects, rendering the host abnormally sensitive to ordinarily innocuous substances.

Gell & Coombs classification EXAM HOT

Peter Gell & Robert Coombs (1963) classified 4 hypersensitivity types: Type I (immediate/anaphylaxis), Type II (cytotoxic), Type III (immune complex/Arthus), Type IV (cell-mediated/delayed).

Type I — IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity

Acute inflammatory reaction mediated by IgE bound to mast cells/basophils, induced by allergens (non-parasitic antigens provoking Type I responses). Unlike normal humoral response, plasma cells secrete IgE. Beneficial roles: antigen elimination, resistance to parasitic worms.

Atopy — a condition where certain individuals make IgE continuously/excessively against common environmental antigens, with a hereditary correlation.

Mechanism EXAM HOT

  1. SensitisationAllergen induces humoral IgE response; IgE binds high-affinity Fc receptors on mast cells/basophils, "sensitising" them.
  2. Cross-linkingRe-exposure to the same allergen cross-links membrane-bound IgE, triggering degranulation.
  3. Mediator releasePreformed granule contents + newly-synthesised mediators generate the lesion.

Attracted to mast-cell degranulation sites; degranulate releasing their own mediators; contain enzymes that neutralise histamine/serotonin (soothing action) — eosinophilia is a favourable reaction, and even a slight rise is taken seriously as an allergy indicator.

Manifestations EXAM HOT

  • Cutaneous → local swelling/oedema, flare, urticaria

Clinical Type I conditions

  • Milk allergy — Jersey cattle allergic to their own α-casein; delayed milking forces protein into blood → anaphylaxis, urticaria, death.
  • Food allergy — incompletely absorbed protein (egg, fish, dairy, beef) reaches mast cells within minutes → pruritic papules/erythema, vomiting, diarrhoea (common in dogs/cats).
  • Atopic dermatitis — chronic multifactorial syndrome; environmental allergens (dust, pollen, mould, dander); dogs, cats, horses, goats; erythema → licking/scratching → hair loss, papules, scaling, crusting; some dogs develop otitis externa.
  • Vaccine/drug allergy — recorded with killed FMD, rabies, CBPP vaccines; penicillin allergy from therapeutic use or contaminated milk (penicillin degrades to penicilloyl group, binds protein, provokes response).
  • Parasite allergy — tapeworms, fly bites, mites (Demodex/flea saliva may instead trigger Type IV).

Avoid the allergen. Desensitisation therapy — small, gradually increasing dilute antigen doses over weeks (dogs/cats respond well, horses do not). Corticosteroids inhibit prostaglandin/leukotriene production (side effect: immunosuppression). β-agonists (epinephrine, isoprenaline, salbutamol) and α-antagonists (methoxamine, phenylephrine) treat acute reactions — epinephrine is the key anaphylaxis drug. Antihistamines used to a lesser extent.

Type II Hypersensitivity

Type II hypersensitivity

Antibody (IgG or IgM) mediated cytolytic/cytotoxic reaction. Antibody bridges antigen and effector cells (complement, phagocytes) via Fc regions, causing cell lysis, ADCC, or opsonisation.

Mechanism EXAM HOT

Antigen-antibody-complement complexes generate enzymes that damage the cell membrane, causing osmotic changes and lysis. Destruction also occurs by ADCC (Antibody Dependent Cell-mediated Cytotoxicity) and by antibody acting as an opsonin, facilitating phagocytosis.

Blood transfusion reactions EXAM HOT

Blood group antigens are largely cell-membrane components (some passively adsorbed soluble molecules). Animals can make antibodies against foreign blood-group antigens without prior direct exposure — natural antibodies arise from exposure to similar/identical heterophile epitopes shared with microorganisms/protozoa/helminths in nature. Agglutination of RBCs by another individual's antibodies = isohaemagglutination (antibodies = isohaemagglutinins, usually IgM).

  • If natural (IgM) antibodies to donor RBC antigens exist, immediate attack occurs — agglutination, haemolysis, opsonisation/phagocytosis.
  • Without natural antibody, first exposure stimulates a response; IgG forms over time and eliminates transfused cells; a second identical-donor transfusion causes immediate destruction.
  • Reaction severity depends on transfused volume — haemolysis, complement activation, anaphylatoxin release, mast-cell degranulation. Signs: sympathetic activity (sweating, salivation, lacrimation, diarrhoea, vomiting) then hypertension, arrhythmia, tachycardia/tachypnoea.
  • Cross-matching prevents reactions — donor RBCs + recipient serum, 37 °C/30 min; lysis/agglutination = incompatible.

Haemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN) EXAM HOT

Also called neonatal isoerythrolysis. Female sensitised to fetal RBCs (placental leakage), concentrates anti-RBC antibody in colostrum; newborn absorbs it via suckling, causing rapid RBC destruction. Four conditions required:

  1. Young inherits a sire RBC antigen the dam lacks.
  2. Dam is sensitised to this antigen.
  3. Response boosted by transplacental haemorrhage in late gestation.
  4. Newborn ingests colostrum with high antibody titre.

Type III & Type IV Hypersensitivity

Type III hypersensitivity

Antibody-mediated, characterised by deposition of antigen-antibody complexes (usually soluble antigen) in tissues (especially vascular endothelium), complement activation, polymorph infiltration, vasoactive-mediator release, inflammation and tissue destruction.

Local — Arthus reaction EXAM HOT

Subcutaneous antigen injection into an animal with existing precipitating antibody causes acute inflammation within hours — erythematous, oedematous swelling progressing to local haemorrhage/thrombosis and, if severe, tissue destruction. Antibodies involved must be both precipitating and complement-activating, usually IgG; immune complexes deposit between/beneath vascular endothelial cells. Eosinophil infiltration is not significant.

  • Reversed Arthus reaction — antibody given intradermally to an animal with high circulating antigen.
  • Passive Arthus reaction — antibody IV + antigen intradermally.
  • Reverse passive Arthus reaction — antigen IV + antibody intradermally.

Examples of Arthus-type reactions

  • Blue eye — seen in a small proportion of dogs infected/vaccinated with live canine adenovirus type I; anterior uveitis → corneal oedema/opacity, neutrophil infiltration, virus-antibody complexes detectable; develops 1-3 weeks post-infection, usually resolves spontaneously.
  • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis — sensitised animals inhale antigen; e.g. cattle fed mouldy hay sensitised to Saccharopolyspora rectivirgula spores develop interstitial pneumonia on re-exposure — "farmer's lung" in humans (equine equivalent: "hay sickness"); similar conditions from pigeon-faeces dust ("pigeon breeder's lung"), mushroom compost, library dust.

Serum sickness

Large antigen amounts entering blood, binding antibody, form circulating immune complexes; if antigen is in excess, small complexes escape clearance, deposit in medium-sized artery walls and outflow-rich sites (glomeruli, synovia, choroid plexus). Classic cause: large doses of foreign hyperimmune serum (e.g. antitetanus serum). Signs: generalised vasculitis (erythema, oedema, urticaria), neutropenia, lymph node enlargement, joint swelling, proteinuria — resolves within days.

Type IV hypersensitivity EXAM HOT

Delayed-Type Hypersensitivity (DTH)

Cell-mediated, from antigen–APC–T cell interaction; not induced by circulating antibody but by sensitised T cells releasing lymphokines. Cannot be passively transferred by serum, only by lymphocytes. Two forms: tuberculin (injection) type and contact dermatitis type.

Tuberculin reaction mechanism

Tuberculin = antigenic extract of M. tuberculosis/M. bovis/M. avium; most common form is purified protein derivative (PPD), made by growing organisms in synthetic medium, steam-killing, filtering, TCA-precipitating; the antigenic component is thought to be heat shock protein 65 (HSP65). Intradermal injection into a sensitised animal → red indurated swelling, onset 12-24 h, peak intensity 24-72 h, fades over weeks.

Mechanism: on first invasion, macrophages phagocytose organisms, present antigen to Th1 cells, generating memory T cells. On intradermal re-challenge, Langerhans cells carry antigen to draining nodes, activate memory T cells that recruit Th1 effectors; these accumulate, secrete IFN-γ, IL-2, serotonin, IL-8, lymphotactin, recruiting more CD4+/CD8+ T cells; macrophages accumulate and destroy the antigen.

Other Type IV conditions

  • Allergic contact dermatitis — chemical exposure (poison ivy oils, insecticides).
  • Infection allergy — associated with fungal, viral, chronic bacterial diseases.
  • Jones-Mote hypersensitivity — reactivity similar to DTH; inflammation + basophil infiltration immediately under the epidermis after antigen injection.

Tolerance

Central tolerance — deletes self-reactive T/B clones before maturity in primary lymphoid organs (bone marrow, thymus). Peripheral tolerance — inactivates surviving self-reactive T/B cells in secondary lymphoid organs, rendering them incapable of responding to self.

  • Blue eye — canine adenovirus-1 immune-complex uveitis.
  • Serum sickness — antigen-excess small complexes deposit in vessels/glomeruli.
  • DTH cannot transfer via serum — only via lymphocytes (defining Type IV feature).
  • PPD — HSP65-based tuberculin antigen; peak reaction 24-72 h.
  • Comparative test — distinguishes M. bovis/tuberculosis from M. avium/paratuberculosis reactivity.
  • Central vs peripheral tolerance — primary-organ deletion vs secondary-organ inactivation.
Chapter 24

Tolerance & Autoimmunity

Autoimmunity

A condition where the body produces antibodies and T-cell responses against its own tissue elements (autoantigens); the individual loses the ability to distinguish self from non-self. Self antigens are normally non-immunogenic.

History EXAM HOT

Ehrlich (1901) observed goats producing antibodies to erythrocytes received from other goats, and postulated horror autotoxicus — the principle that the body normally avoids attacking itself. Autoimmunity literally means "protection to self" but practically means injury to self.

Autoimmune reaction can arise from: (1) a normal immune response to an abnormal/unusual antigen, or (2) an abnormal immune response to a normal antigen.

Mechanisms of tolerance breakdown EXAM HOT

  • Sequestrated/hidden antigen
    • Proteins normally isolated from the immune system are not recognised as "self"
    • Trauma/infection releases them into circulation → antigenic, tissue damage
    • Example: lens protein — eye trauma releases it, causing sympathetic ophthalmia
    • Example: chronic hepatitis in dogs — autoantibodies against liver membrane proteins
  • Antigen formed by molecular changes
    • Physical/chemical/biological alteration of body tissues creates neoantigens eliciting immune response
    • Example: immunoconglutinins (IKs) — autoantibodies against complement components (C2, C3, C4); new epitopes exposed during complement activation; IK serum level reflects the amount of complement activation
  • Molecular mimicry (cross-reacting antigen)
    • Epitope sharing between an infectious agent/parasite and a body-tissue-specific antigen causes autoimmunity/tissue damage
    • Example: Trypanosoma cruzi antigen cross-reacts with mammalian neurons and heart muscle → nervous and cardiac disease
Chapter recap
  • Ehrlich, 1901 — coined "horror autotoxicus" from goat-erythrocyte experiments.
  • Autoimmunity — literally "protection to self", practically means injury to self.
  • 3 mechanisms — sequestrated antigen release, neoantigen formation (e.g. immunoconglutinins), molecular mimicry.
  • Sympathetic ophthalmia — classic sequestrated-antigen (lens protein) example.
  • Trypanosoma cruzi mimicry — cross-reacts with neurons/heart muscle.
  • Central vs peripheral tolerance — protects against most self-reactive clones; breakdown of either can trigger autoimmunity.
Chapter 25

Immunodeficiencies

Immunodeficiency

A defect in the immune system reducing resistance to infection; classified as primary (genetic) or secondary (acquired, from a known cause).

Primary immunodeficiencies EXAM HOT

Genetic mutations produce defects in the immune system.

  • Innate immunity defects — deficiencies in phagocytosis/intracellular killing → increased bacterial disease susceptibility. Example: bovine leukocyte adhesion deficiency (BLAD).
  • T-cell function defects — predispose to overwhelming viral infections.
  • B-cell function defects — predispose to overwhelming bacterial disease.
  • Combined immunodeficiencies — most severe; no resistance to any infectious agent.

Secondary immunodeficiencies EXAM HOT

Immunodeficiency from a known cause, not uncommon in domestic animals. The most important cause is viral infection — viruses may profoundly immunosuppress the host by infecting/killing lymphocytes, or by causing lymphocyte transformation (cancer), which favours viral survival within the host.

  • Other major causes: stress, malnutrition, mycotoxins, old age.
Chapter recap
  • Primary — genetic mutation-based; innate, T-cell, B-cell, or combined defects.
  • BLAD — bovine leukocyte adhesion deficiency, a classic innate-defect example.
  • Combined immunodeficiency — most severe, resistance to no pathogen class.
  • Secondary — acquired; viral infection is the most important cause.
  • Other secondary causes — stress, malnutrition, mycotoxins, old age.
  • T-cell defect → viral susceptibility; B-cell defect → bacterial susceptibility.
Chapter 26

Vaccines & Immunoprophylaxis

Vaccination

Introducing a safe antigen (vaccine) to induce immunity against infection; a vaccine is a suspension of living or inactivated organisms used as antigen. Named from vacca (Latin, cow) after Jenner's 1796 cowpox observations, which controlled and eventually eliminated smallpox.

Active vs passive immunity

Active immunity
  • Host reacts to antigen, produces its own antibody
  • Develops slowly, persists long
  • Natural (infection) or Artificial (vaccination)
Passive immunity
  • Pre-formed antibody introduced into the host
  • Rapid onset, short duration
  • Natural (transplacental/colostral transfer) or Artificial (immune serum/cell transfer)

Vaccine generations EXAM HOT

GenerationTypes

Live attenuated vaccines EXAM HOT

Attenuation reduces virulence while retaining immunogenicity/replication capacity — achieved by unnatural-host/cell-culture passage (70-80 repeats), altered physiological conditions, or cold adaptation (cold-adapted virus strains); thermostable strains grow at elevated temperature; temperature-sensitive mutants cannot grow at slightly raised temperature.

Advantages
  • Replication yields abundant immunogen; no adjuvant needed
  • Often single-dose, long-lasting immunity; both T and B epitopes present
  • Cost-effective; often no booster needed; effective against intracellular pathogens
Disadvantages
  • Reversion-to-virulence risk; possible shedding; transient immunosuppression
  • Requires cold chain; possible contamination with other animal viruses; unwanted-component side effects

Inactivated/killed vaccines

Physical/chemical inactivation (formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, beta-propiolactone, gamma/UV irradiation) removes replication ability while preserving epitope structure; usually prepared from virulent strains; generally requires an adjuvant.

Advantages
  • No reversion possible; no shedding/environmental contamination
  • Stable, less cold-chain dependent; more immunogenic per dose in some cases; whole organism has both T and B epitopes
Disadvantages
  • No replication — limited antigen; needs multiple doses/adjuvant/boosters
  • Incomplete inactivation risks outbreaks; allergic-reaction risk from large antigen loads; costly; less effective against intracellular organisms

Toxoid, anaculture & bacterins

  • Toxoid vaccine — exotoxin inactivated by formaldehyde/iodine/heat/other chemicals, retaining immunogenicity without toxicity (e.g. tetanus, anthrax); effective only if disease is solely toxin-driven.
  • Anaculture — combines toxoid + killed bacteria via formalinised whole culture (clostridial disease vaccines); trypsinisation increases immunogenicity.
  • Bacterins — killed-bacteria vaccines, usually formaldehyde-inactivated with an adjuvant (alum/aluminium hydroxide); autogenous vaccines use organisms from the infected animal/farm itself (e.g. fowl cholera vaccine); easy to prepare, no reversion risk, but immunity is short-lasting (usually <6 months).

Subunit, peptide & anti-idiotypic vaccines

  • Subunit/conjugate vaccines — purified peptide sites of major viral antigenic regions (e.g. HA vaccine for influenza); bacterial capsular polysaccharide is immunogenic but fails to evoke T-cell response alone — conjugating it to a T-epitope-carrying protein carrier ("conjugate vaccine") boosts efficacy.
  • Peptide vaccines — synthesised/purified immunogenic peptides; precisely defined, side-effect-free, stable, cheap; example — FMD vaccine using a linear 20-aa sequence (141-160) of VP1; require carrier-protein coupling to stimulate T cells.
  • Anti-idiotypic antibody vaccine — a secondary antibody (anti-idiotype) mimics the original antigen's structure at the antigen-combining site, itself usable as a vaccine (e.g. human hepatitis B vaccine); avoids pathogen exposure risk but is complex, costly, and not very effective.

Recombinant DNA & DIVA/marker vaccines EXAM HOT

Recombinant DNA vaccine — pathogen's immunodominant gene cloned into a vector, transcribed/translated within the vaccinated host's cells; expressed in bacteria, yeast, mammalian cells or viruses. Hybrid virus vaccines use poxviruses, adenoviruses, herpesviruses as vectors (e.g. ND virus in fowlpox virus; rabies virus in vaccinia virus); produce good immune responses, stable, stimulate both cellular and humoral immunity, relatively cheap.

DIVA/marker vaccines (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) — live or inactivated vaccines based on a deletion mutant or isolated antigenic proteins, letting serology distinguish vaccination from natural infection; used with a companion diagnostic test detecting antibody against the missing protein. Useful in controlling avian influenza (Italy).

Adjuvants

Provide a depot effect, extending antigen persistence/release, boosting immunogenicity of weak antigens, stimulating cell-mediated response, reducing antigen cost/dose. Examples: mineral oils (aluminium hydroxide, liquid paraffin), vegetable oils (groundnut oil, Montanide), mycobacterial products (Freund's adjuvant); delivery systems — ISCOM, nanoparticles.

Avoiding vaccination failure

  • Store live vaccines at recommended refrigeration temperature; maintain the cold chain to the field.
  • Vaccination interval should not be too short; use the correct dose; reconstitute with proper diluent.
  • Vaccinate only healthy (non-immunocompromised) animals.
  • Failure causes: incorrect administration route, dead live-vaccine, vaccinating a passively-protected animal, too-late vaccination (already infected), wrong strain, prior passive immunisation, animal immunosuppression, biological variation, inadequate vaccine.
