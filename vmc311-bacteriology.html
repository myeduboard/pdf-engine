Chapter 02

Streptococcus

Streptococcus

Gram-positive, catalase-negative cocci that divide in one plane and fail to separate fully, forming chains; family Streptococcaceae, order Lactobacillales.

History

  • 1873Rivolta described chain-forming organisms in pus from equine strangles.
  • 1878–79Pasteur recognised the organism as a pus-forming agent.
  • 1903Hugo Schottmüller used blood agar to differentiate haemolysis types.
  • 1919Brown classified alpha/beta/gamma haemolysis.
  • 1928/1933Rebecca Lancefield devised serological (C-antigen) grouping — 19 groups, A–U (no I, J).

Morphology & classification EXAM HOT

Spherical/ovoid cocci ~1 μm, facultative anaerobes, oxidase-negative, non-spore-forming, non-motile (except some enterococci). Capsule of hyaluronic acid occurs in S. pyogenes and some group-C/B/D strains.

  • Haemolysis (Brown, 1919)
    • α — partial, greenish (viridans, commensal, e.g. S. pneumoniae)
    • β — complete, clear zone (most pathogens)
    • γ — non-haemolytic (enterococci, S. faecalis)
  • Lancefield groups — key veterinary species
    • Grp B — S. agalactiae — chronic bovine mastitis
    • Grp C — S. dysgalactiae (acute mastitis, cattle), S. equi subsp. equi (strangles, horse), S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus (mastitis, metritis, multi-species septicaemia)
    • Grp C/D/E/P/V — S. uberis — mastitis
    • Ungrouped — S. suis — porcine meningitis, arthritis

Cultural & biochemical

37 °Coptimum growth temp
19Lancefield groups (A–U)
54 °C/30 minheat inactivation

Grows best on blood/serum-enriched media; Edward's medium (blood agar + crystal violet + aesculin) differentiates species by aesculin hydrolysis and colony colour. S. pneumoniae gives flat, draughtsman colonies at 48–72 h. Catalase/oxidase negative; ferments sugars with acid, no gas; gelatin not liquefied; indole negative; insoluble in 10% bile (unlike pneumococci).

Toxins & virulence factors

  • Streptolysin O — oxygen-labile, cardiotoxic; basis of the ASO titre.
  • Streptolysin S — oxygen-stable, causes surface β-haemolysis, nephrotoxic.
  • Erythrogenic (Dick) toxin — 4 types; intradermal test in rats = Dick test/Schultz-Charlton reaction.
  • Streptokinase (fibrinolysin), streptodornase (DNase, 4 types A–D), hyaluronidase, proteinase, neuraminidase, serum opacity factor.
  • M, T, R surface proteins — type-specific; M protein inhibits phagocytosis (main virulence factor).
Key hosts/disease
  • Cattle — mastitis (S. agalactiae, S. dysgalactiae, S. zooepidemicus, S. uberis)
  • Horse — strangles, purpura haemorrhagica, bastard strangles (S. equi)
  • Pig — S. suis meningitis/arthritis/endocarditis
  • Dog — S. canis neonatal septicaemia
  • Poultry — S. gallinarum septicaemia
Diagnosis
  • CAMP test — S. agalactiae enhances staphylococcal β-toxin lysis
  • Bile solubility — pneumococci autolyse in 10% Na deoxycholate
  • Optochin test — pneumococci inhibited, S. viridans not
Common mistake

Peptostreptococcus indolicus, an anaerobic streptococcus, is a co-pathogen of summer mastitis with Arcanobacterium pyogenes — it is not a Lancefield-groupable aerobic streptococcus.

Chapter recap
  • Lancefield 1933 — C-carbohydrate antigen grouping, 19 groups A–U.
  • Brown 1919 — α/β/γ haemolysis on horse-blood meat-infusion agar.
  • S. agalactiae (grp B) — chronic bovine mastitis; CAMP test positive.
  • S. equi subsp. equi — strangles; complication = purpura haemorrhagica.
  • Toxins — Streptolysin O (O2-labile), Streptolysin S (O2-stable, surface β-haemolysis).
  • M protein — chief anti-phagocytic virulence factor of S. pyogenes.
  • Control — penicillin remains first line; vaccines of limited value except equine strangles.
Chapter 03

Staphylococcus

Staphylococcus

Gram-positive cocci in grape-like clusters, family Staphylococcaceae; first described by Sir Alexander Ogston (1880) from infected tissue (Greek staphyle = bunch of grapes).

Morphology & culture EXAM HOT

Spherical cells 0.8–1 μm; non-motile, non-acid-fast, no flagella. Grows at 35–37 °C; tolerant of 7–10% NaCl (Mannitol Salt Agar is selective). Colonies round, smooth, golden/white; broth shows uniform turbidity with powdery deposit.

Pigments & haemolysis

  • Old pigment-based naming (now obsolete)
    • S. aureus — golden/orange
    • S. albus — white
    • S. citreus — lemon yellow

On sheep/rabbit blood agar, hot-cold lysis occurs: incomplete haemolysis at 37 °C completes on chilling to 4 °C.

Antigens & toxins

  • Group antigen: ribitol teichoic acid (S. aureus) vs glycerol teichoic acid (S. intermedius); Protein A only in S. aureus.
  • Haemolysins α, β, δ, ε — α-toxin is the major toxin in gangrenous mastitis, necrotising and lethal.
  • Leukotoxin (a, d components) — leukocidal, aids spread.
  • Enterotoxin — heat-stable, survives 100 °C/80 min; causes human food poisoning; rarely from animal strains.
  • Exfoliative/dermonecrotoxin — skin exfoliation.
  • Toxic shock syndrome toxin (TSST) — excess lymphokine release.

Worked test

Coagulase tube test: culture + rabbit plasma, incubate 37 °C/24 h — clot formation = coagulase-positive staphylococci (CPS), the significant pathogens.

Slide test detects bound coagulase (clumping in 1–2 min); tube test detects free coagulase (clot at 37 °C/24 h).

  • S. hyicus — exudative epidermitis (greasy pig disease) in piglets.
  • MRSA — methicillin-resistant S. aureus, a public-health concern.
  • Treatment — penicillin (if susceptible), methicillin/oxacillin, tetracyclines.
Chapter 04

Bacillus anthracis

Bacillus anthracis

A large, Gram-positive, capsulated, aerobic/facultatively anaerobic, spore-forming rod (family Bacillaceae) that causes anthrax in animals and Woolsorter's disease, hide porter's disease and malignant pustule in humans.

History EXAM HOT

  • 1849Pollender — first pathogenic bacterium observed under a microscope.
  • 1850Davaine — first communicable disease shown transmissible by blood inoculation.
  • 1863–68Davaine & Rayer credited with discovery of the anthrax bacillus.
  • 1863Koch — first bacterium isolated in pure culture.
  • 1881Pasteur — attenuated vaccine (growth at 42–43 °C).

Morphology

Large rods, 4–8 × 1–1.5 μm, non-motile, arranged end to end giving a bamboo-stick / box-car appearance. Capsule = polymer of D-glutamic acid, formed only with serum/bicarbonate media and reduced pCO2 — demonstrated by the McFadyean reaction (polychrome methylene blue). Spores are central/oval, never formed inside the living body, only in soil or culture.

Culture & resistance

Colonies: irregular, grey-white, frosted-glass; medusa head/curled-hair edge under LP microscope; inverted fir-tree growth in gelatin stab; selective medium = PLET (polymyxin, lysozyme, EDTA, thallous acetate). Spore destruction in wool/hair uses formaldehyde — duckering (2% HCHO, 39–40 °C, 20 min, or 0.25% at 60 °C, 6 h).

Toxin complex

3-factor exotoxin
EF + PA + LF → oedema + shock
  • EF Oedema Factor (Factor I)
  • PA Protective Antigen (Factor II)
  • LF Lethal Factor (Factor III)

No single factor is toxic alone; the complete complex produces local oedema and generalised shock.

Pathogenicity

SpeciesClinical course
CattlePeracute (death <2 h), acute (48–96 h, fever, bloody diarrhoea, abortion), chronic (rare); mortality 90% in bulls
SheepAcute, rapid death after convulsions
HorseAcute oedema of throat/neck ± colic
PigMore resistant; throat/neck oedema, enteritis, not always fatal
Necropsy caution

Never open a carcass suspected of anthrax — sporulation on air exposure contaminates the environment. Send an ear or muzzle piece for examination instead.

Diagnosis

  • Blood smearWright's/Giemsa stain; large blue rods with pink-purple capsules.
  • Ascoli's precipitation test (1911)Boiled tissue filtrate layered over anti-anthrax serum; white ring = positive.
  • String-of-pearls test (Charlton, 1980)0.05–0.5 IU penicillin/ml agar; swollen round cells in chains after 3–6 h.
  • Gamma phage testZone of lysis on strain-14 control confirms B. anthracis.
B. anthracis
  • Non-motile, capsulated
  • Long chains, no broth turbidity
  • Susceptible to gamma phage
Anthracoid organisms
  • Motile, non-capsulated
  • Short chains, turbid broth
  • Not susceptible to gamma phage
ImmunitySterne / Mazucchi vaccines

Sterne strain vaccine = non-capsulated avirulent spores, given 1 month pre-outbreak. Mazucchi vaccine = attenuated carbazoo spores in 2% saponin. Protective antibody to Factor II (PA) develops in 7–10 days, lasting ~1 year.

Chapter recap
  • Koch, 1863 — first bacterium isolated in pure culture.
  • Capsule — poly-D-glutamic acid; only in virulent strains.
  • 3-factor toxin — EF + PA + LF, none toxic alone.
  • Spore survival — up to 60 years in soil.
  • Duckering — formaldehyde disinfection of wool/hair.
  • Never open the carcass — prevents sporulation and environmental contamination.
  • String-of-pearls — penicillin-induced swollen cell chains, diagnostic.

Clostridium — Family Characters & Disease Groups

Clostridium

Anaerobic, Gram-positive, pleomorphic, spore-forming rods (family Clostridiaceae) grouped by the type of disease their exotoxins cause.

Four disease-based groups EXAM HOT

  • Histotoxic — wound/trauma tissue infections
    • C. chauvoei — blackleg (cattle, sheep)
    • C. septicum — malignant oedema, braxy, necrotic dermatitis
    • C. novyi type A — gas gangrene, big head of rams
  • Hepatotoxic — toxin produced in the liver
    • C. novyi type B — black disease
    • C. haemolyticum (C. novyi type D) — bacillary haemoglobinuria
  • Enterotoxigenic — enterotoxaemia/food poisoning
    • C. perfringens types A–E — food poisoning, lamb dysentery, pulpy kidney, struck, necrotic enteritis
    • C. colinum — quail disease/ulcerative enteritis; C. piliforme — Tyzzer's disease; C. sordellii — gas gangrene
  • Neurotoxic — potent exotoxin
    • C. tetani — tetanus
    • C. botulinum — botulism

Shared family characters

  • Motile by peritrichous flagella except C. perfringens and C. tetani type VI.
  • C. perfringens is the only capsulated species.
  • Oxidase- and catalase-negative; C. novyi (oedematiens) strict anaerobe, dies on O2 exposure; C. histolyticum and C. perfringens are aerotolerant.
  • Robertson's cooked meat broth is the key isolation medium.
Saccharolytic (turn meat pink)
  • C. novyi, C. septicum, C. chauvoei, C. perfringens
Proteolytic (turn meat black, foul odour)
  • C. tetani, C. botulinum, C. haemolyticum
Chapter recap
  • 4 groups — histotoxic, hepatotoxic, enterotoxigenic, neurotoxic — grouped by disease mechanism, not phylogeny.
  • Only capsulated species — C. perfringens.
  • Non-motile exceptions — C. perfringens, C. tetani type VI.
  • Robertson's cooked meat broth — universal clostridial enrichment medium.
  • Saccharolytic vs proteolytic — meat turns pink (gas producers) vs black (foul odour producers).

Clostridium tetani

Clostridium tetani

A strict anaerobic, Gram-positive rod producing a terminal spherical endospore that gives the classic drumstick appearance; cause of tetanus.

History

  • 1884Disease mechanism first understood (described earlier by Hippocrates).
  • 1886Rosenbach — demonstrated the slender bacillus with round terminal spores.
  • 1889Kitasato — isolated in pure culture, reproduced disease by inoculation.

Habitat & culture

Soil contaminated with animal faeces; also street/hospital dust, catgut, plaster of Paris. Strict anaerobe, optimum 37 °C, pH 7.4; swarms on moist agar — stiff agar (3%) confines it to rhizoid colonies. On horse blood agar, α-haemolysis develops later into β-haemolysis (tetanolysin). Gelatin stab shows a fir-tree pattern; feebly proteolytic, ferments no sugars, indole positive.

Antigens & toxins EXAM HOT

10flagellar serotypes (I–X)
65 °C/5 mintetanospasmin heat inactivation
3×10⁷min. mouse lethal doses/mg protein
Two toxins
Tetanospasmin (neurotoxin) + Tetanolysin (haemolysin)
  • Tetanospasmin MW 68,000 monomer (toxic), dimer (non-toxic, antigenic); oxygen-stable, heat-labile
  • Tetanolysin heat- and O2-labile, lyses rabbit/horse RBCs, similar to streptolysin O

Susceptibility to toxin (descending order): horse and human > guinea pig > mouse > goat > rabbit; birds and reptiles are highly resistant.

C. tetani toxin
  • Site: wounds
  • Acts centrally — blocks synaptic inhibition
  • Spastic paralysis
  • One antigenic type (tetanospasmin)
C. botulinum toxin
  • Site: carcases, decaying matter
  • Acts peripherally — blocks neuromuscular transmission
  • Flaccid paralysis
  • Eight antigenic types (A–G)

Why does tetanus present with spastic, not flaccid, paralysis?

Tetanospasmin blocks inhibitory synapses centrally in the CNS, releasing motor neurons from inhibition, unlike botulinum toxin's peripheral neuromuscular block.

Chapter recap
  • Kitasato, 1889 — first pure culture isolation.
  • Drumstick spore — terminal, spherical, bulges the cell.
  • Tetanospasmin — the neurotoxin, MW 68,000, blocks CNS synaptic inhibition.
  • Tetanolysin — the haemolysin, O2- and heat-labile.
  • Species susceptibility — horse/human most susceptible; birds/reptiles resistant.
  • 10 serotypes — flagellar (H) antigen types I–X; type VI non-flagellated.
  • Paralysis type — spastic (tetanus) vs flaccid (botulism).

Clostridium botulinum

Clostridium botulinum

A group of Gram-positive, spore-forming anaerobes producing extremely potent neurotoxins that cause botulism — flaccid paralysis in birds, ruminants, horses and humans. Named from botulus, Latin for sausage.

History & habitat

Van Ermengem (1896) first isolated it from ham linked to an outbreak. Endospores are widespread in soil and water; disease follows ingestion of preformed toxin from contaminated cans, carcases, silage or rotting vegetation. Named regionally: Shaker foal syndrome (horse), Lamsiekte/loin disease (cattle), Limberneck/western duck sickness (waterfowl).

Toxin types & hosts EXAM HOT

TypeSusceptibleSourceDisease
AHumans, chickens, pigsVegetables, meat, fishFood-borne botulism

Cβ (C2)Cattle, horses, mink, dogsCarcases, baled silageForage poisoning

8 types (A–G) total; toxin is a protoxin activated by trypsin, MW ~70,000, heat-labile (100 °C/20 min destroys toxin, though spores survive 100 °C for hours). Lethal human dose 1–2 μg; >120 million mouse lethal doses/mg.

Pathogenesis — three forms

  1. Food-bornePreformed toxin absorbed from gut, blocks acetylcholine release at the myoneural junction → flaccid paralysis, respiratory failure.
  2. WoundSpores germinate in a wound; toxin spreads systemically (mechanism in shaker foal syndrome).
  3. Infant/toxico-infectionSpores germinate in an immature gut flora — floppy baby syndrome in infants; rare type-C epidemics in broiler chicks/turkey poults.

Diagnosis

  • Mouse inoculation of serum/exudate — toxin produces the characteristic wasp-waist abdominal-breathing appearance.
  • Toxin identification — mouse neutralisation with polyvalent, then monovalent, antitoxin.
  • Foodstuff isolation — heat 65–80 °C/30 min, culture on blood agar under CO2 at 35 °C up to 5 days.
Control note

No routine immunisation exists; bivalent/trivalent antitoxin and IV therapeutic agents (tetraethylammonium, guanidine HCl) enhance neuromuscular transmitter release in emergencies.

Chapter recap
  • Van Ermengem, 1896 — first isolation, from contaminated ham.
  • 8 toxin types A–G; type D = Lamsiekte in cattle (phosphorus deficiency, Pica).
  • Mechanism — blocks acetylcholine release peripherally → flaccid paralysis.
  • 3 forms — food-borne, wound, infant (toxico-infectious).
  • Wasp-waist mice — diagnostic sign of toxin in mouse bioassay.
  • Toxin destroyed at 100 °C/20 min; spores survive far longer.

Clostridium perfringens

Clostridium perfringens (C. welchii)

A capsulated, non-motile, Gram-positive anaerobe causing acute, highly fatal enterotoxaemias; first cultivated by Achalme (1891), described in detail by Welch and Nuttal (1892).

Culture EXAM HOT

Optimum 45 °C (generation time 10 min) though grows at 37 °C, pH 5.5–8.0. In litmus milk gives stormy fermentation (acid clot disrupted by gas). On blood agar, target hemolysis — narrow complete zone (theta toxin) inside a wide incomplete zone (alpha toxin). Alpha toxin (lecithinase C) is demonstrated by Nagler's reaction on egg-yolk agar, neutralised only on the antitoxin half of the plate.

Toxin types A–E

AAlphaHuman; lambs; broilersFood poisoning; yellow lamb disease; necrotic enteritis

  • Alpha — lecithinase C; lethal, dermonecrotic, haemolytic (hot-cold); produced by all types.
  • Beta — trypsin-sensitive; explains type B/C predilection for neonates (colostrum has antitrypsin activity).
  • Epsilon — a protoxin activated by trypsin; increases gut permeability, damages vascular endothelium — both enterotoxin and neurotoxin.
  • Iota — also a protoxin; shared with C. spiroforme and C. difficile.
  • Minor toxins — theta (haemolysin), kappa (collagenase), lambda (proteinase), mu (hyaluronidase), nu (DNase).

Pathogenesis & signs

Precipitated by abrupt dietary change/overeating → reduced peristalsis → toxin absorption. Pulpy kidney disease shows focal symmetrical encephalomalacia, glycosuria, sudden death in well-grown lambs.

Diagnostic sample

20–30 ml ileal contents from a fresh carcase, centrifuged; 0.4 ml supernatant IV into mice — death within 10 h from toxin (within 5 min = shock artefact).

Control

Pre-lambing ewe vaccination (formalised whole culture or alum-precipitated toxoid) gives lambs colostral protection for the first 3 weeks; direct lamb vaccination from day 3, boosted at 4 weeks; immunity lasts 6–12 months without a booster.

Chapter recap
  • Welch & Nuttal, 1892 — detailed original description.
  • 5 types A–E — distinguished by alpha/beta/epsilon/iota toxin combinations.
  • Type D — epsilon toxin, pulpy kidney/over-eating disease of sheep.
  • Nagler's reaction — lecithinase (alpha toxin) demonstration on egg-yolk agar.
  • Stormy fermentation — litmus milk acid-clot disruption by gas.
  • Target haemolysis — theta (inner, complete) + alpha (outer, partial) zones.
  • Colostral antitrypsin — protects neonates from beta/epsilon toxin activation.
Chapter 09

Clostridium chauvoei

Clostridium chauvoei (C. feseri, C. chauvoei type B)

A Gram-positive, worldwide soil organism causing blackleg (black quarter) in cattle and sheep.

Morphology & culture

Rod-shaped, rounded ends, 3–8 μm × 5 μm; sometimes pleomorphic cigar-shaped/citron forms; old cultures stain Gram-negative. Strict anaerobe, 37 °C optimum, growth enhanced by liver extract/glucose. Blood agar: whitish-grey colonies with a haemolytic zone. Cooked meat medium: slow growth, meat turns pink with sour odour. Ferments glucose, lactose, sucrose, maltose (acid + gas) but not salicin.

Toxins

  • Alpha — oxygen-stable haemolysin; necrotoxin causing dermonecrosis and fibrinolysis (lethal IV in mice/guinea pigs).
  • Beta — DNase.
  • Gamma — hyaluronidase.
  • Delta — oxygen-labile haemolysin.

Pathogenicity EXAM HOT

Affects cattle aged 6 months–2/3 years. Sign: crepitating swelling (gas) in the hind/fore quarter, lameness, trembling muscles; death within 24 h. In sheep, an acute febrile illness follows injury.

Lesion

Dark red, dry, necrotic muscle with small gas bubbles and a characteristic rancid odour; yellowish/blood-stained oedematous fluid surrounds it.

Diagnosis, control & prevention

  • SmearGram-positive rods from lesion/oedema fluid.
  • FATDistinguishes C. chauvoei from C. septicum.
  • Toxin neutralisationBroth culture tested in mice/guinea pigs against specific antitoxin.
  • VaccinationFormalised alum-precipitated whole culture; multi-component vaccine with C. chauvoei, C. septicum, C. welchii type D and C. tetani; two doses 2–3 weeks apart give strong immunity.
  • TreatmentHyperimmune serum + penicillin; oxytetracycline/chlortetracycline early.
Chapter recap
  • Age group — young cattle 6 months–3 years.
  • Crepitating swelling — gas under skin, hind/fore quarter, characteristic sign.
  • Alpha toxin — main lethal, dermonecrotic toxin.
  • Salicin-negative — key fermentation feature distinguishing it.
  • FAT — differentiates from C. septicum.
  • 4-in-1 vaccine — combines C. chauvoei, C. septicum, C. welchii type D, C. tetani.
Chapter 10

Clostridium septicum

Clostridium septicum

Closely related to C. chauvoei (also called C. chauvoei type A); first described by Pasteur and Joubert (1887) as Vibrion septique. Causes malignant oedema, braxy and necrotic dermatitis.

Morphology & culture

Highly pleomorphic, long filamentous forms; cigar-shaped/citron forms common. Strict anaerobe, 37 °C, glucose-promoted growth; colonies swarm/spread on ordinary media; rhizoid on stiff agar. Listeria monocytogenes

Listeria monocytogenes

A small, Gram-positive, non-spore-forming rod (family Listeriaceae) causing septicaemia, abortion and CNS infection across many species. First isolated by Gill (1929) from sheep; named by Murray (1926), renamed by Pirie (1927/1940).

Habitat & morphology EXAM HOT

Widespread in soil, faeces, decaying vegetation and silage (pH 5.5) — poor-quality silage can carry up to 10⁷ cfu/kg. Grows 4–45 °C, pH 5.5–9.6, tolerant of 10% NaCl. Motile by 1–5 peritrichous flagella at room temperature but non-motile at 37 °C — the classic tumbling motility on hanging-drop at 25 °C. On TSA/BHI, colonies show a blue-green sheen under oblique light.

L. monocytogenes is CAMP-positive with S. aureus; L. ivanovii is CAMP-negative but gives a wider haemolysis zone. Of 16 serovars, disease is dominated by ½a, ½b and 4b.

  1. SubclinicalCommon after poor-quality, high-pH silage in cold weather.

  • Chick embryo CAM inoculation produces focal necrotic lesions.
Public health

Causes human meningoencephalitis, abortion, endocarditis; sources include soil, unpasteurised milk, soft cheese; risk concentrated in the immunocompromised.

  • Circling disease — neural listeriosis, trigeminal/dental-pulp route, head tilt.

Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae

Mycobacterium — Tuberculosis Complex

Mycobacterium

Slender, acid-fast rods (mycolic-acid-rich cell wall resists Gram staining) of family Mycobacteriaceae; genus name (Lehmann & Neumann, 1896) means "fungus bacterium" for its branching filamentous growth.

History

  • 1868Hansen — discovered the leprosy (Hansen) bacillus.
  • 1882Koch — isolated the mammalian tubercle bacillus, satisfied Koch's postulates; Ehrlich discovered the acid-fast property the same year.
  • 1895Johne — described Johne's bacillus (M. paratuberculosis).

Classification EXAM HOT

  • Slowly growing mycobacteria
    • M. tuberculosis — human TB, also dogs
    • M. bovis — bovine TB, wide host range incl. humans
    • M. africanum — human TB
    • M. avium — avian TB; also cattle, sheep, goat, pig
  • Atypical (Runyon, 1959) — by pigmentation/growth rate

    • Scotochromogens — M. gordonae
    • Non-chromogens — M. avium, M. intracellulare, M. paratuberculosis, M. lepraemurium (feline leprosy)
    • Rapid growers (<7 days) — M. phlei, M. smegmatis
  • Non-cultivable

Generation time 14–20 h; colonies at 2 weeks, sometimes 6–8 weeks. M. tuberculosis obligate aerobe; M. bovis microaerophilic. Lowenstein-Jensen medium (glycerol) — M. tuberculosis/M. avium grow well (eugonic); M. bovis grows sparsely (dysgonic) but thrives on pyruvate-based Stonebrink's medium. Niacin production and nitrate reduction are unique to M. tuberculosis.

Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis

Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis (M. johnei)

An acid-fast, mycobactin-dependent rod causing chronic, fatal Johne's disease (paratuberculosis) in cattle, sheep, goats, camels and wild ruminants.

Morphology & culture EXAM HOT

Short rods 1–2 μm, rounded ends, motile, no spores; club-shaped on artificial media. Absolutely requires mycobactin (a killed extract of M. phlei) for growth. Herrold's egg-yolk medium with mycobactin, incubated at 37 °C for up to 16 weeks, is the standard isolation method; colonies are minute, grey-white, friable (<1 mm).

Pathogenesis

Shed in faeces, milk, semen; survives up to 1 year in the environment. Calves <1 month are most susceptible. Ingested bacilli are engulfed by macrophages in Peyer's patches, surviving and replicating intracellularly; immune-mediated granulomatous reaction → lamina propria/submucosa infiltration → protein loss and malabsorption.

Clinical course

Long subclinical phase; signs appear typically >2 years of age — persistent, profuse diarrhoea without loss of appetite, progressive weight loss to emaciation and death. In sheep/goats, diarrhoea is less marked or absent. Lesion: thickened, transversely-corrugated terminal ileum/large intestine mucosa; enlarged, oedematous mesenteric/ileocaecal lymph nodes.

Diagnosis

  • Acid-fast faecal smearDirect microscopy for a rapid presumptive result.
  • CultureDecontaminate with 0.3% benzalkonium chloride, culture on Herrold's egg-yolk medium, 37 °C up to 16 weeks.
  • SerologyCFT (laborious, insensitive); AGPT; ELISA (M. phlei-absorbed serum) detects subclinical cases.
  • Johnin testIntradermal (delayed hypersensitivity, peak at ~1 month post-infection) or intravenous (fever response).
  • DNA probesHigh sensitivity, direct faecal detection.
Zoonotic note

M. avium subsp. paratuberculosis infection in humans is implicated as Crohn's disease (chronic human enteritis) — association, not proven causation.

Control

Isolate clinical cases (heavy shedders); detect and cull subclinical carriers; inactivated adjuvanted vaccines exist; a live attenuated vaccine given subcutaneously to calves before 4 weeks of age reduces herd incidence.

Chapter recap
  • Johne, 1895 — first described the causative bacillus.
  • Mycobactin-dependent — obligatory growth requirement, unique among mycobacteria here.
  • Herrold's egg-yolk medium — standard culture, up to 16 weeks.
  • Susceptible age — calves <1 month most susceptible to infection.
  • Clinical onset — typically >2 years age, chronic diarrhoea without anorexia.
  • Crohn's disease link — proposed human association, unproven.
  • Johnin test — intradermal delayed hypersensitivity, peaks ~1 month post-infection.

Nocardia

Actinomyces & Arcanobacterium pyogenes

Actinomyces / Arcanobacterium

Gram-positive, branching filamentous prokaryotes (order Actinomycetales) causing suppurative, granulomatous disease; the genus name was coined by Harz (1879), with A. bovis isolated by Boestrom (1891).

Morphology EXAM HOT

Pleomorphic, filamentous with V/Y/T configurations. Pus in actinomycosis contains pale-yellow sulfur granules — a mass of Gram-positive filaments in mineralised calcium phosphate, ringed by Gram-negative club-shaped bodies (the ray fungus appearance). Arcanobacterium pyogenes shows no granules — small pleomorphic rods, cocci, pear-shaped cells, sometimes Chinese-letter arrangement.

Culture

  • Neither genus grows on Sabouraud dextrose agar (unlike Nocardia).
  • A. bovis is capnophilic (needs 5–10% CO2); A. pyogenes/A. viscosus grow aerobically, enhanced by CO2.
  • A. bovis: 2–4 days to grow; A. pyogenes visible in 24 h.
  • A. pyogenes: hazy haemolysis at 24 h, complete narrow zone at 48 h; positive CAMP test with S. aureus; pits a Loeffler serum slope in 24–48 h.

Pathogenesis

A. bovis is normal oral flora; dental trauma or coarse feed lets it invade bone (usually the mandible/maxilla), producing rarefying osteomyelitis — lumpy jaw — with sinus tracts discharging sulphur-granule pus. A. pyogenes is a mucosal commensal that becomes pathogenic after tissue injury or co-infection (Fusobacterium necrophorum, Peptostreptococcus indolicus), producing a dermonecrotic exotoxin, protease and neuraminidase.

Diagnosis

Crush a granule in 10% KOH to view clubs microscopically; Gram stain confirms the ray fungus. Anaerobic-preserving transport, culture on brain-heart-infusion/blood agar under anaerobic, microaerophilic and aerobic conditions in parallel. Failure to grow on SDA (unlike Nocardia) supports Actinomyces.

Treatment

Highly sensitive to tetracycline, chloramphenicol and penicillin (benzyl penicillin, ampicillin) — unlike the often-refractory Nocardia infections.

  • No SDA growth — key point distinguishing Actinomyces from Nocardia.
  • CAMP-positive — A. pyogenes enhances S. aureus haemolysis.
  • Highly penicillin-sensitive — unlike Nocardia.

Corynebacterium / Rhodococcus

Escherichia coli

A Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic rod, normal inhabitant of the mammalian large intestine; named after Theobald Escherich, who first described it as Bacterium coli commune in 1885.

Habitat & culture EXAM HOT

Higher counts occur in carnivores/omnivores than herbivores; survives weeks-to-months in faecal particles, dust and water — its presence in drinking water is the standard indicator of faecal pollution. Motile by peritrichous flagella; some strains capsulated/fimbriated. Grows readily at 37 °C: β-haemolytic on blood agar (pathogenic strains), bright pink colonies on MacConkey (strong lactose fermenter), metallic green sheen on Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar — a hallmark diagnostic feature.

Biochemical identity

IMViC pattern
Indole (+) · Methyl Red (+) · Voges-Proskauer (−) · Citrate (−)

H2S negative, urease negative, oxidase negative, catalase positive. Heat resistance: survives 60 °C/15 min or 55 °C/60 min in some strains; more sensitive to phenol/cresol.

Antigens

Serotyped on O (somatic, 164 types), K (capsular, 103 types) and H (flagellar, 75 types) antigens, recorded e.g. O157:K85:H19. Three K-antigen classes (L, A, B) differ in heat lability.

Toxins & virulence factors EXAM HOT

  • Capsule — antiphagocytic, protects against complement.
  • Endotoxin (Lipid A) — causes colisepticaemia/coliform mastitis toxaemia; fever, leukopenia→leukocytosis, hyperglycaemia then hypoglycaemia, lethal shock.
  • Fimbriae/adhesins — Type I (mannose-sensitive), K88 (F4), K99 (F5), F6, F41 — attach to jejunal/ileal enterocytes.
  • Enterotoxins — LT (heat-labile, cholera-toxin-like, activates adenylate cyclase); ST (heat-stable, STa activates guanylate cyclase, STb).
  • Verotoxins (Shiga-like) — SLT-1/VT-1 (shiga-antitoxin neutralisable) and SLT-2/VT-2e (causes oedema disease in pigs, haemorrhagic colitis in man; NOT neutralised by shiga antitoxin); block the 60S ribosomal subunit.
  • Haemolysins — alpha (membrane damage, frees iron) and beta.
  • Siderophores — aerobactin, enterobactin (iron scavenging).
  • Colicins — plasmid-encoded antibacterial proteins (Gratia & Frederiq, 1946); ColV linked to virulence, serum resistance, altered motility.

Salmonella

Salmonella

A globally important zoonotic genus (family Enterobacteriaceae) comprising ~2,400 serotypes, of which about 50 are significant pathogens.

History EXAM HOT

  • 1880Eberth first observed S. typhi in typhoid-fever spleens/mesenteric nodes ("Eberth-Gaffky bacillus").
  • 1884Gaffky isolated the organism in pure culture.
  • 1885Salmon & Smith described S. cholerae-suis in swine — the first named Salmonella species.

Habitat, morphology & culture

Intestinal tract of warm- and cold-blooded animals; survives ≥9 months in moist soil, water, faeces, blood/bone/fish meal. Gram-negative, non-capsulated, non-spore-forming rods; motile by peritrichous flagella except S. gallinarum and S. pullorum (form non-adhesive type-2 fimbriae instead). Selective enrichment: tetrathionate broth, selenite broth, Rappaport-Vassiliadis (essential for the more fastidious poultry/pig-adapted serotypes). Colony reactions: pale/colourless on MacConkey (non-lactose fermenters, except some S. arizonae); red on brilliant green agar; black centre with red skirt on XLD; colourless with black centre on SS agar; classic TSI reaction — alkaline slant / acid butt / H2S+ (R/Y/H2S+).

Biochemistry

IMViC −,+,−,+. Ferments maltose, mannitol, mannose, glucose (acid+gas); does not ferment lactose, sucrose, salicin. Urease negative; H2S positive except S. cholerae-suis and S. paratyphi A. Lysine decarboxylase positive. Killed at 55 °C/1 h or 60 °C/15 min.

Antigens & variation EXAM HOT

  • H antigen — heat-labile flagellar protein, strongly immunogenic, shows diphasic variation.
  • O antigen — heat-stable somatic LPS complex (= endotoxin); extracted as the Boivin antigen; less immunogenic than H.
  • Vi antigen — envelope antigen of S. typhi/paratyphi/dublin masking O; virulence-linked, poorly immunogenic, lost on subculture.
  • Antigenic variations
    • H-O variation — phenol inhibits flagella (phenotypic, reversible); Craigie's/U-tube enriches for H-rich population
    • Phase variation — Phase 1 (specific, letters a–z) vs Phase 2 (shared, numbers); diphasic vs monophasic strains
    • V-W variation — fresh Vi-masked (V form) → repeated subculture loses Vi (W form); VW intermediate
    • S-R variation — smooth→rough loses O antigen and virulence

Classification

Reeves et al. (1989): genus splits into S. enterica (6 subspecies — enterica I, salamae II, arizonae IIIa, diarizonae IIIb, indica IV, houtenae V) and S. bongori. Serotyped by the Kauffmann-White scheme using O and H antigens (e.g. S. enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium); ~46 O serogroups from 67 described O antigens.

Pathogenesis

Fimbrial adherence → membrane ruffling → uptake in membrane-bound vesicles → intracellular replication → release. Long O-chain LPS resists phagocytosis and complement, aiding spread; LPS also drives endotoxin activity. Localises in ileal/caecal/colonic mucosa and mesenteric nodes; subclinical/latent (gall-bladder) carriage is common and reactivated by stress (transport, overcrowding, pregnancy, ration change).

Yersinia & Minor Enterobacteriaceae

Yersinia

Non-lactose-fermenting Enterobacteriaceae, motile (except Y. pestis), showing classic bipolar (safety-pin) staining in Giemsa smears; grows over a wide 5–42 °C range. Of >10 species, only three are pathogenic: Y. pestis, Y. pseudotuberculosis, Y. enterocolitica.

Yersinia pseudotuberculosis EXAM HOT

Synonym Pasteurella pseudotuberculosis; worldwide, mainly guinea pigs (pseudotuberculosis), also turkeys, pigeons, rats, rabbits, horses, occasionally sheep/goats/pigs/cattle/cats/man. Gram-negative ovoid rods, motile at 22 °C (not 37 °C), bipolar with Leishman stain. Grows on MacConkey agar — a key differentiator from Pasteurella multocida. Indole negative, nitrate reduced to nitrite. 10 serotypes (I, II, III cause most disease). Acute septicaemic form has high mortality; chronic form shows caseous/necrotic lesions in spleen, liver, mesenteric nodes resembling true tuberculosis. Cold-enrichment (4 °C, 3 weeks, weekly subculture) aids faecal isolation. Susceptible to chloramphenicol and streptomycin; in man causes mesenteric lymphadenitis mimicking appendicitis.

Yersinia pestis

Cause of bubonic/pneumonic plague; carried by rodents, transmitted by fleas (Xenopsylla cheopis). Gram-negative coccobacilli, bipolar Leishman staining; grows aerobically/anaerobically with serum/blood; grows on MacConkey; forms a capsule at 37 °C. Lesions: swollen haemorrhagic lymph glands and spleen, necrotic foci in spleen/liver. Feline plague has 3 forms: bubonic (enlarged lymph nodes/buboes), septicaemic (fatal, no lymphadenopathy), pneumonic (aerosol risk to humans). Treated with oxytetracycline; live/killed vaccines available.

Yersinia enterocolitica

Isolated from pigs, horses, cattle, sheep, goats, dogs, cats, rodents; 57 O-factors, 6 K-antigens, 19 H-antigens define 5 biotypes and >50 serotypes. Somatic antigens 2, 3, 5, 8, 9 dominate clinical isolates. Serotype O:9 cross-reacts with Brucella, causing false-positive brucella agglutination. Optimum growth 28 °C on blood agar; cold enrichment improves isolation. Causes gastroenteritis, terminal ileitis, mesenteric lymphadenitis, pseudo-appendicitis in humans; pigs (serotype O3, biotype 4) are the natural reservoir for the human-pathogenic strain.

Pasteurella multocida & Mannheimia haemolytica

Pasteurella / Mannheimia / Actinobacillus / Haemophilus / Lonepinella

Family Pasteurellaceae (order Pasteurellales), Gram-negative coccobacilli with classic bipolar staining on Leishman/Wright/Giemsa stain, worldwide in a wide host range.

History EXAM HOT

  • 1878Bollinger and Perroncito first reported P. multocida.
  • 1880Pasteur investigated it extensively as the cause of fowl cholera.
  • 1921Jones isolated "P. haemolytica" from diseased calves and sheep.
  • 1981Mannheim proposed the family name Pasteurellaceae; P. haemolytica later reclassified as Mannheimia haemolytica.

Culture

Non-motile, non-spore-forming, enhanced by blood/serum; 3 colonial forms — smooth (virulent, capsulated), rough (avirulent, no capsule/mucoid antigen), mucoid (intermediate, hyaluronic acid-rich). P. multocida: sweetish odour, non-haemolytic, no growth on MacConkey agar. Mannheimia haemolytica/P. trehalosi: beta-haemolytic, tolerate MacConkey bile salts (pin-point pink-red colonies); TSI = Y/Y/H2S-negative. P. anatipestifer: dewdrop colonies at 48 h, non-fermentative, non-haemolytic, no MacConkey growth.

Biochemistry, antigens & toxins

Oxidase +, catalase + (except P. trehalosi, R. anatipestifer); P. multocida indole +, M. haemolytica indole −. Ferments glucose, sucrose, galactose, sorbitol (acid, no gas).

  • P. multocida — Carter's capsular typing gives 5 serotypes A–F (C invalid); 16 somatic (LPS) types by AGPT; serotype = capsule:somatic, e.g. A:5, A:8, A:9 (fowl cholera), B:6 (haemorrhagic septicaemia, Asia), E:6 (haemorrhagic septicaemia, Africa). Toxin (type A/D strains) is cell-associated, polypeptide, heat-stable, dermonecrotic, lethal, immunogenic.
  • Mannheimia haemolytica/P. trehalosi — 17 serotypes on soluble surface antigens; 2 biotypes — A (arabinose-fermenting, = M. haemolytica, serotypes other than 3/4/10/15) and T (trehalose-fermenting, = P. trehalosi, serotypes 3, 4, 10, 15). Biotype A → ruminant pneumonia, lamb septicaemia, ovine mastitis; biotype T → septicaemia in older lambs/sheep. All strains produce a thermolabile leukotoxin resembling E. coli alpha-haemolysin.

Pathogenesis

Endogenous invasion of commensal organisms under stress (overcrowding, chilling, transport, intercurrent infection); exogenous spread by aerosol/contact. P. multocida is a primary cause of fowl cholera and haemorrhagic septicaemia, but often a secondary invader in pneumonia; septicaemia follows pharyngeal mucosal penetration. M. haemolytica pneumonia (shipping fever) follows transport stress by 1–2 weeks — serotype 1 predominates in cattle, serotype 2 in sheep; leukotoxin impairs phagocytosis, disrupts ion gradients, damages platelets → thrombosis and fibrin exudation.

  • CultureBlood agar/BHI, dewdrop colonies at 18 h; bone marrow useful when putrefied.

Control

Immunity is predominantly humoral. P. multocida strain P52 gives 6 months–1 year protection. Fowl cholera vaccines require locally prevalent serotypes. Notably, Pasteurella is one of the few Gram-negative genera sensitive to penicillin (also streptomycin, chloramphenicol, chlortetracycline, sulpha-trimethoprim, enrofloxacin).

X factor = heat-labile iron-porphyrin haematin, needed for catalase/aerobic-respiration enzymes. V factor = heat-labile, NAD/NADP+ co-enzyme, hydrogen acceptor. Growth also enhanced by 10% CO2. Chocolate agar is the medium of choice (releases intracellular V factor, retains heat-stable X factor); Levinthal's medium and Fildes' agar are alternatives.

Satellitism EXAM HOT

Classic test

Streaking S. aureus across a Haemophilus-inoculated blood agar plate produces larger colonies near the staphylococcal streak, tapering with distance — demonstrating dependence on staphylococcus-supplied V factor.

Biochemistry & resistance

Fermentation reactions unreliable for ID; nitrate reduced to nitrite. H. parasuis/H. paragallinarum oxidase negative; H. somnus oxidase positive, catalase negative. Fragile organisms — killed at 55 °C/30 min, sensitive to refrigeration, drying, disinfectants; autolyse within 2–3 days in culture; long-term storage needs lyophilisation.

Antigens & disease

Capsular polysaccharide antigens resemble pneumococcal capsule and E. coli K antigens; 15 serovars H. parasuis, 9 H. paragallinarum, 15 H. somnus (immunodiffusion).

Actinobacillus

Actinobacillus

Gram-negative, very small, non-motile, non-sporing, non-acid-fast bacilli of the family Pasteurellaceae, historically confused with fungi due to their tissue granules.

History & habitat EXAM HOT

Lignières and Spitz (1902) isolated A. lignieresii; Brumpt (1910) coined the genus name. Worldwide commensals of respiratory, alimentary or genital mucosa: A. lignieresii (bovine/ovine oral cavity and rumen), A. equuli (equine gut/mouth), A. suis (porcine tonsil/URT). Cannot survive free in the environment — carrier animals drive transmission.

Morphology EXAM HOT

Small granules scattered along the bacilli, often at one pole of a bacillary/filamentous form — the classic Morse-code appearance. In lesions, grey-white granules crush to reveal club colonies (calcium phosphate club-like processes around central Gram-negative rods); ZN-differentiated — clubs stain red, bacilli blue. Non-capsulated except A. pleuropneumoniae; extracellular slime present in A. lignieresii, A. equuli, A. suis.

Culture

Aerobic/microaerophilic/facultative anaerobe, 37 °C optimum. A. lignieresii — small, glistening, non-haemolytic on blood agar, slightly sticky on primary isolation (lost on subculture), late lactose fermenter on MacConkey (pale → pink). A. equuli and A. suis — haemolytic, persistently sticky colonies, lactose fermenters. CAMP test — A. pleuropneumoniae enhances S. aureus beta-haemolysis (positive).

Biochemistry, antigens & toxins

Catalase + (except A. pleuropneumoniae); oxidase + and urease +; ferment sugars with acid+gas; IMViC negative, H2S positive. Killed at 62 °C/10 min; cultures lose viability fast — subculture every 5–7 days. A. lignieresii: 6 antigenic types (1–6) + subtypes 1a/1c, no exotoxin. A. pleuropneumoniae: 12 serotypes, 2 biotypes (biotype 1 NAD-dependent, biotype 2 NAD-independent); capsule, LPS, OMPs and haemolytic/cytotoxic toxins drive pathogenicity.

Pathogenesis & signs

Wooden tongue spreads via lymphatics from oral commensal invasion, producing chronic pyogranulomatous swelling that impairs feeding, discharging pus with soft grey-white granules. A. suis enters via aerosol/contact/skin, spreading haematogenously — neonatal septicaemia is rapidly fatal (death <15 h) with cyanosis, petechiae, fever, respiratory distress, neurologic signs. A. pleuropneumoniae releases OMP, LPS and cytokines causing massive neutrophil-mediated lung destruction, extensive haemorrhage and fibrin deposition, severe respiratory distress. Sleepy foal disease: septicaemic newborn foals, febrile and recumbent, death in 1–2 days.

Diagnosis & treatment

Club colonies in tissue sections, growth on blood/MacConkey agar, biochemistry. Wooden tongue treated with parenteral sodium iodide or oral potassium iodide, sulphonamides + penicillin-streptomycin, oral isoniazid (30 days) for refractory lesions. A. suis responds to ampicillin, carbenicillin, potentiated sulphonamides, tetracyclines. Polyvalent bacterins give partial protection but do not prevent transmission or carrier states.

Pseudomonas — Glanders & Melioidosis

Pseudomonas

Gram-negative, aerobic, medium rods, motile by polar flagella except P. mallei; oxidase and catalase positive; most grow on MacConkey agar.

History & habitat EXAM HOT

  • 1882Gessary first isolated P. aeruginosa.
  • 1882Loeffler and Schütz discovered P. mallei.
  • 1912Whitmore and Krishnaswamy first described P. pseudomallei.

P. aeruginosa and P. pseudomallei are environmental saprophytes (water, soil, plants, skin, tropical soils; wild rodents reservoir P. pseudomallei); P. mallei is an obligate parasite of equidae.

Morphology & culture

Non-spore-forming, non-acid-fast rods; strict aerobes, growth 5–42 °C; non-fastidious (TSA, blood agar); cetrimide agar selects P. aeruginosa. Colonies grey-blue with a fruity, grape-like odour (amino-acetophenone). P. aeruginosa pigments: pyocyanin (blue-green, chloroform-soluble, unique to this species), pyoverdin/fluorescin (greenish-yellow, water-soluble), pyorubin (red), pyomelanin (dark brown-black). Non-lactose fermenter on MacConkey (pale, greenish-blue pigment overlay). P. pseudomallei: smooth→rough, wrinkled, honeycomb, earthy/musty (ammonia) odour, lactose fermenter on MacConkey but no growth on deoxycholate/SS agar. P. mallei: very slow, smooth, white-cream colonies, does NOT grow on MacConkey.

Biochemistry, antigens & toxins

Oxidase +, nitrate→nitrite, gelatin liquefied, IMViC −,−,−,+. 17 P. aeruginosa serotypes by somatic antigen; extracellular virulence factors include fibrinolysin, elastase, lecithinase, lipase, protease, haemolysins, leucocidin, alginate, phospholipase C, esterase and exotoxin A. P. pseudomallei produces a potent exotoxin (similar to exotoxin A), protease, lipase, lecithinase. No exotoxin is known in P. mallei.

Brucella

Brucella

Small, Gram-negative, non-motile, partially acid-fast coccobacilli (family Brucellaceae); all six recognised species are facultative intracellular parasites with a predilection for the reticuloendothelial system and reproductive tract.

History EXAM HOT

  • 1887Bruce identified B. melitensis in Malta.
  • 1897Bang first recognised B. abortus.
  • 1914Traum discovered B. suis.
  • 1918Alice Evans identified the first human-origin Brucella in the USA.
  • 1930Buck developed the live attenuated Strain 19 vaccine.

Culture

Strict aerobe (B. abortus is carboxyphilic, needs 5–10% CO2); optimum 37 °C; growth is slow — plates incubated up to 30 days; enhanced by serum/liver extract and amino acids/thiamine/biotin/isoerythritol/manganese. Selective media: Albimi, Columbia agar, or SDA + bacitracin/cycloheximide/nalidixic acid/nystatin/polymyxin B. Pinpoint, smooth, bluish, translucent colonies after 3–5 days. B. ovis and B. canis are permanently in the rough (R) form; other species start smooth (S) and roughen on repeated subculture. Non-haemolytic on blood agar.

Staining & biochemistry

Stains by Gram, Modified ZN and Koster's stain (chorion cells packed with organisms — most useful for bovine cotyledon smears). Catalase +, oxidase + (except B. ovis, B. neotomae), urease + (except B. ovis, B. melitensis), reduces nitrate, IMViC −,−,−,−.

Antigens & biotyping EXAM HOT

  • Somatic A and M antigens on LPS — B. abortus A:M ≈ 20:1; B. melitensis M:A ≈ 20:1; B. suis intermediate.
  • B. canis and B. ovis are antigenically rough, lack A/M, possess only surface antigen R.
  • S→R conversion (as in other Gram-negatives) = loss of virulence + auto-agglutination tendency. Strain 19 vaccine is a low-virulence intermediate mutant, still antigenic.
  • Heterophile cross-reactions with Yersinia enterocolitica O:9, Vibrio cholerae, some E. coli/Salmonella strains.
  • No exotoxin; surface carbohydrate binds B-lymphocytes, contributing to pathogenesis.
  • Biotyping by CO2 requirement, H2S production, urease, dye tolerance (thionin, basic fuchsin), monospecific-serum agglutination, phage typing — 3 biotypes in B. melitensis, 8 in B. abortus (biotype 3 dominant in India), 4 in B. suis.

Resistance

60 °C/10 minheat kill point
75 dayssurvival in an aborted foetus
4 monthssurvival in butter

Pathogenesis

Entry via digestive/genital mucosa or skin (venereal transmission is main route for B. ovis); phagocytosed, transported to regional nodes, then thoracic duct → bloodstream → parenchymatous organs; granulomas form in liver, spleen, bone marrow, joints. Erythritol (present in ruminant/pig placenta and male genital tract, absent in humans) favours brucellal growth and stimulates most species but not B. ovis, and inhibits Strain 19. Pyogranulomatous placentitis causes abortion from 6 months onward; females typically abort only once (partial immunity develops) but shed brucellae in fluids at subsequent parturitions; permanent infertility can occur in B. canis-infected male dogs.

Campylobacter

Campylobacter

Thin, curved, Gram-negative, microaerophilic, motile rods (family Campylobacteraceae) — formerly classified in genus Vibrio, hence diseases still sometimes called "vibriosis". Key pathogens: C. fetus subsp. fetus and subsp. venerealis, and C. jejuni.

Morphology & culture EXAM HOT

0.2–0.5 μm wide; joined daughter cells give S-shaped, seagull-shaped or long spiral forms; darting motility on phase-contrast/darkfield. DCF (dilute carbol fuchsin, 4 min) stains colonies well. Grows at 25 °C or 45 °C; C. fetus on Brucella/Columbia/BHI + 5–10% blood, selective with polymyxin B, novobiocin, cycloheximide; incubated 37 °C, 4–6 days under microaerophilic gas mix (6% O2, 10% CO2, 84% N2) — colonies are small, round, dewdrop-like. C. jejuni prefers charcoal-cefoperazone-deoxycholate agar or Blaser's Campy-BAP, 42 °C, 2–3 days — flat, grey, spreading/watery colonies. C. mucosalis/C. coli grow on Columbia blood agar ± brilliant green, anaerobically then microaerophilically; C. coli produces a pink-tan pigment.

Biochemistry, resistance & antigens

Non-fermentative, non-haemolytic, oxidase-positive, H2S-produced, nitrate-reduced, catalase-variable. C. jejuni is the only species hydrolysing sodium hippurate (hippurate test: positive = deep purple with ninhydrin after 10 min). Killed at 60 °C/5 min; survives 10–20 days in soil/hay/manure. C. fetus subsp. venerealis has "O" (stable) and "W" (Surface Array Protein, antigenically shifts once sIgA appears) antigens; subsp. fetus has O, A-2, B antigens.

Pathogenesis EXAM HOT

C. fetus subsp. venerealis
  • Obligate parasite of bovine genitalia
  • Transmitted venereally (coitus/AI)
  • Localises anterior vagina/cervix → endometritis, salpingitis after progesterone release
  • Causes infertility/early embryonic death; fertility regained within 5 months
C. fetus subsp. fetus
  • Enters via ingestion (no venereal spread)
  • Bacteraemia → placentitis
  • Ovine abortion near end of gestation (1st-half infection does not abort)
  • Occasional late-gestation abortion in cattle

C. jejuni produces an adhesin, a cytotoxin and an E. coli-like heat-labile toxin; faecal-oral transmission. C. mucosalis colonises the piglet mouth post-weaning, invades intestinal mucosa under stress, causing excessive proliferation of immature absorptive epithelium (swine proliferative enteritis).

  • Beta-lactamase production — explains penicillin/ampicillin resistance in many strains.
  • Virgin heifer test — classic (if costly) method to identify an infected bull.

Mycoplasma

Mycoplasma

Class Mollicutes ("soft skin") — the smallest and simplest self-replicating prokaryotes, lacking a cell wall entirely; genome ~5×10⁸ daltons vs the average bacterial 2.5×10⁹ daltons.

History & habitat EXAM HOT

Nocard and Roux (1898) first isolated the agent of bovine pleuropneumonia — the type species. Later-discovered organisms were called PPLO (pleuropneumonia-like organisms) for their resemblance. Free-living saprophytes or commensals on URT/intestinal/genital mucosa, joints, and the bovine mammary gland; >60 species cause animal disease.

Morphology

Pleomorphic — cocci, spirals, filaments, rings; no cell wall or wall precursors (muramic/diaminopimelic acid absent); stains poorly with Gram (appears Gram-negative), better with Giemsa/Romanowsky. Reproduce by binary fission or budding; minimal reproductive unit ~0.3 μm — small enough to pass a 0.22 μm filter. M. mycoides has a galactan capsule.

Culture EXAM HOT

Highly fastidious — require sterol/cholesterol (Mycoplasma, Ureaplasma) or not (Acholeplasma); complex media (bovine heart infusion, 20% horse serum, 10% yeast extract, adenine dinucleotide, penicillin + thallous acetate to inhibit contaminants), pH 7.5. Colonies (0.1–0.6 mm) show the classic fried-egg appearance under low power; best studied with Diene's stain (dense centre dark blue, periphery light blue). Most haemolytic on swine blood agar; can grow in embryonated eggs (yolk sac) and cell culture. Ureaplasma produces tiny "T-mycoplasma" colonies.

Biochemistry & resistance

Chemoorganotrophs, mainly fermentative, using glucose or arginine; urea hydrolysed only by ureaplasmas; not proteolytic. Fragile (no wall) — killed readily by drying, sunlight, disinfectants; but resistant to penicillin and sulphonamides (no cell-wall target).

Pathogenesis

Venereal, vertical or aerosol transmission; many avian mycoplasmas are egg-transmitted. Firm mucosal adherence via specific attachment organelles; extracellular haemolysins, proteases, nucleases, ammonia damage host cells or drive chronic infection. Ciliary destruction in the respiratory tract predisposes secondary bacterial invasion; fibrinous exudate shields organisms from antibody/drugs.

  • Sample handlingRefrigerate, deliver within 24–48 h — mycoplasmas are fragile.
  • FATMore reliable than simple staining for direct detection.
  • CultureFluids direct into broth; tissues homogenised/serially diluted first (inhibitors possible in semen/joint fluid).

  • Digitonin sensitivityMycoplasma/Ureaplasma sensitive (need cholesterol); Acholeplasma resistant.
  • SerologyRapid plate agglutination, HI, AGID (avian); CFT (CCPP, enzootic pneumonia); FAT, ELISA, LAT, DNA probes.
Treatment & control

Tetracycline, gentamicin, kanamycin, tylosin, erythromycin, quinolones are effective (never penicillin/sulphonamides). Hatching-egg antibiotic dips can produce M. gallisepticum-free chicks. Live attenuated M. mycoides subsp. mycoides vaccine controls CBPP; live/inactivated M. gallisepticum vaccines give partial egg-production protection.

Leptospira

Leptospira (Leptos = fine/thin)

Slender, helically coiled spirochaetes (family Leptospiraceae) with hooked ends, causing worldwide zoonotic leptospirosis — Weil's disease, seven-day fever, Stuttgart disease (dogs).

History & classification EXAM HOT

Weil (1886) described the first human case; the Weil's disease agent was isolated by Inada (1915) and named L. icterohaemorrhagiae. Split by pathogenicity/DNA/growth at 13 °C into L. interrogans (pathogenic) and L. biflexa (free-living, non-pathogenic). Key L. interrogans serovars: Pomona, Hardjo(-bovis), Icterohaemorrhagiae, Canicola, Grippotyphosa, Bratislava, Tarassovi.

Habitat

Colonises the renal proximal convoluted tubules of mammals, excreted in urine (often for months) with minimal clinical signs in the reservoir host; rats are especially important carriers, excreting up to 100 million leptospires/ml of urine. Warm, moist, neutral-to-alkaline environments favour survival outside the host.

Morphology & culture

0.2–0.3 μm × 6–20 μm; hooked ends; two periplasmic flagella (axial filaments) drive characteristic spinning motility; stain poorly with aniline/Romanowsky (Giemsa possible); best viewed by darkfield or Fontana silver impregnation. Obligate aerobes using long-chain fatty acids (not CHO/amino acids) as energy source; need vitamins B1, B12, purines; optimum 25–35 °C, highly fastidious. EMJH medium (bovine albumin, Tween 80, rabbit plasma) is standard; generation time 12–16 h; incubated up to 8 weeks at 30 °C; growth appears a few mm below the semisolid surface. 5-fluorouracil selectively inhibits contaminants but not Leptospira.

Resistance & antigens

Highly susceptible to acid, bile, most disinfectants; destroyed by gastric juice in 30 min. LPS antigen present in all members; serovars defined by surface protein-polysaccharide antigens. Produces haemolytic, lipolytic and cytotoxic substances.

Pathogenesis EXAM HOT

Entry via mucous membranes (nasal, genital, ocular, intestinal) or abraded/water-softened skin; venereal transmission important in pigs, vertical transmission in cattle. Rapid haematogenous spread → localisation/proliferation in liver, kidney, spleen, occasionally meninges (up to 16 days); damages small-vessel endothelium → haemorrhage and secondary ischaemic organ damage. Organisms persist in sites of poor antibody access — renal tubules, eyes, uterus.

Dichelobacter, Bacteroides & Fusobacterium — Footrot Group

Non-spore-forming anaerobes

Dichelobacter (Bacteroides) nodosus, other Bacteroides species, and Fusobacterium necrophorum are Gram-negative, strictly anaerobic, non-spore-forming rods responsible for necrotic/suppurative disease, typically as mixed infections.

Habitat & morphology EXAM HOT

D. nodosus is an obligate anaerobe of ovine digital epidermis; survives <1 week on pasture. Other Bacteroides are normal skin/mucosal/GI flora. F. necrophorum is a normal alimentary/mucosal commensal. Morphology: D. nodosus — large (1.7 × 3–6 μm), slightly curved, non-motile, often swollen at one/both ends. F. necrophorum — long (up to 100 μm), filamentous, non-branching, irregular staining.

Culture

Strict anaerobic conditions essential (10% H2, 5% CO2, 85% N2); enriched blood agar or Eugon/Columbia/trypticase soy/BHI + yeast extract, vitamin K, haemin, kanamycin, vancomycin. Bacteroides (except B. ureolyticus) resist kanamycin; Fusobacterium is kanamycin-sensitive. D. nodosus colony types: B-type (papillate/beaded — most pathogenic), M-type (mucoid — less pathogenic), C-type (circular — non-pathogenic, from repeated passage). B. melaninogenicus colonies darken to black centre with grey-brown periphery over 5–14 days, with haematin pigment and UV fluorescence.

Biochemistry, toxins & pathogenesis

Most Bacteroides catalase-negative (except B. fragilis); D. nodosus does not ferment carbohydrates. F. necrophorum is indole and H2S positive, ferments glucose/maltose (not lactose), does not liquefy gelatin. Killed at 50–60 °C/15 min; highly O2-sensitive.

  • D. nodosus produces keratinolytic enzymes — the mechanism of footrot invasion.
  • F. necrophorum produces an exotoxin (leukotoxin, protects Arcanobacterium/Corynebacterium pyogenes from phagocytosis) and a necrotising endotoxin.
  • Synergism: C. pyogenes secretes a diffusible factor that stimulates Fusobacterium proliferation in tissue — a classic polymicrobial partnership.
  • Volatile fatty acids (chiefly butyric acid) account for the characteristic putrid odour.

Differential diagnosis

Distinguish from strawberry foot rot (Dermatophilus), FMD, and Arcanobacterium/Corynebacterium pyogenes infection.

Treatment & control

D. nodosus: hoof trimming + 10% formalin, chloramphenicol or tetracycline; parenteral penicillin-streptomycin. F. necrophorum: 5–10% CuSO4 topically; sulphonamides, tetracycline, erythromycin for early lesions.

Chapter recap
  • D. nodosus colony types — B (pathogenic), M (less), C (non-pathogenic).
  • Barbell/club-shaped ends — D. nodosus morphological hallmark.
  • C. pyogenes-Fusobacterium synergism — classic mixed-infection mechanism.
  • Kanamycin — differentiates Bacteroides (resistant) from Fusobacterium (sensitive).
  • Butyric acid — source of the characteristic putrid necrobacillosis odour.
  • Strict anaerobes — require oxygen-free collection/transport for successful diagnosis.

Dermatophilus congolensis

Dermatophilus congolensis

Gram-positive, non-acid-fast, branching filamentous actinomycete (family Dermatophilaceae) causing exudative skin disease across many mammals; the only pathogenic species in the genus.

History & habitat EXAM HOT

First described in bovine disease by Van Saceghem (1915) in the Congo. Maintains itself in small carrier-animal foci or within scab particles in dust — survives in scab material for up to 3 years.

Morphology & life cycle EXAM HOT

Filamentous, branching; mature filaments comprise motile coccal zoospores arranged at least two abreast, giving the classic tram-track appearance. Zoospores are ~1 μm; rough handling of scab flakes disintegrates filaments, leaving only Gram-positive cocci visible. Life cycle: transverse/longitudinal septation of immature filaments → coccal zoospores → mature zoospores become motile (polar flagella) and infective.

Culture

Grows on sheep/ox blood agar, enhanced by 5–10% CO2, 37 °C, up to 5 days (visible 24–48 h). Colonies: small (~1 mm), grey-yellow, distinctly haemolytic at 24–48 h, firmly agar-embedded; by 3–4 days, 3 mm, rough, wrinkled, golden-yellow (older colonies mucoid). No growth on Sabouraud dextrose agar. Gram-stained colony smears show branching filaments (tram-track not visible from culture, only from direct lesion smears). Catalase +, urease +, gelatin +, acid from glucose/fructose/maltose; indole negative, non-nitrate-reducing.

Disease & pathogenesis

Most severe in tropical/subtropical regions; obligate parasite of animal skin; spread by contact, biting insects, fomites; moist/humid conditions promote disease. Thick crusts form that detach with a hair tuft, leaving a moist, bleeding, depressed area.

No vaccine exists. Recovery from natural infection appears to confer permanent immunity to reinfection.

  • Tram-track appearance — zoospores in parallel rows, diagnostic on direct lesion smear.
  • No SDA growth — a key differential point, echoing the Actinomyces/Nocardia distinction.
  • Strawberry foot rot — ovine lower-leg/coronet form; differential vs D. nodosus footrot.
  • Candle-jar technique — exploits chemotactic zoospore motility for isolation.

Rickettsiales — Q Fever, Ehrlichia, Heartwater & Salmon Poisoning

Rickettsiales

Minute, obligate intracellular bacteria — once thought virus-like, but now classed as true bacteria (Gram-negative-type cell wall with muramic acid, binary fission, own DNA+RNA, antibiotic-susceptible) specially adapted to intracellular parasitism.

History & classification EXAM HOT

Named for Howard Taylor Ricketts (1909), who first observed the organisms in Rocky Mountain spotted fever. Q fever traced by Derrick (1935) in Brisbane abattoir workers — "Query fever"; agent identified by Burnet (Australia) and Cox (USA), hence Coxiella burnetii.

NeorickettsiaReticular cells of lymphoid tissue

Habitat, morphology & cultivation

Parasites of arthropod alimentary tracts (fleas, lice, ticks, mites); transovarial (most genera) or transtadial (Cowdria, Ehrlichia) transmission in the vector. Do not survive free outside living cells — except Coxiella burnetii, which forms spore-like structures surviving 50+ days in dust. Small, non-motile, pleomorphic coccobacilli (0.3–0.6 × 0.8–2 μm), 3-layered wall; stain with Giemsa, Castaneda, Gimenez, Machiavello, Leishman — poorly with Gram. Cultivated in embryonated hen egg yolk sac (first shown by Cox) or cell lines (mouse fibroblast, HeLa, HEp2). Rochalimaea quintana is the only rickettsia that grows on blood agar.

Resistance & antigens

Generally fragile — lose viability via ATP/coenzyme depletion; preserved in skimmed milk or SPG medium. Coxiella burnetii is comparatively hardy — survives a year in dried tick faeces/wool at 4 °C, a month in meat; resists holding pasteurisation (flash method works). Susceptible to tetracycline/chloramphenicol; penicillin/sulphonamides ineffective (sulphonamides may even enhance growth). Antigens: group-specific soluble antigen, species-specific antigen, and an alkali-stable polysaccharide shared with Proteus strains (basis of the Weil-Felix reaction). C. burnetii phase variation — fresh isolates = Phase I; repeated yolk-sac passage → Phase II (autoagglutinable); reverts to Phase I via guinea pig passage; Phase I is the stronger immunogen.

Pathogenesis

Sequence: adherence (host surface receptors) → endocytosis → phagosome destruction (phospholipase) → cytoplasmic (or nuclear, in spotted fever) multiplication. Vascular endothelial proliferation and thrombosis → haemorrhagic rashes if capillaries affected. Q fever has predilection for mammary gland/placenta in cattle/sheep — causes abortion and ovine bronchopneumonia. Ehrlichiosis: congested mucosae, purulent ocular/nasal discharge, gastritis, hindlimb oedema, lymphadenopathy; up to 100% mortality in acute cases; PM shows pulmonary oedema/haemorrhage, hydrothorax, splenomegaly. Salmon poisoning: up to 90% mortality; weakness, vomiting, depression, diarrhoea; lymph node hyperplasia with necrosis. Metacercariae in fish muscle → mature flukes in dog intestine → eggs → snail intermediate host → cercariae infect fish.

Diagnosis

  • Blood/tissue smears (Giemsa, Gimenez, Machiavello, Leishman) and FAT.
  • Guinea pig/mouse IP inoculation, observed 3–4 weeks for temperature rise (Rochalimaea will not grow in these hosts).
  • Q fever: allergic (eyelid) test — antigen into lower eyelid, acute swelling + fever in positives at 3–4 days.
  • Canine ehrlichiosis: Giemsa blood smear (best at day 13 post-infection) shows characteristic monocyte/neutrophil inclusions; indirect FAT.
  • Salmon poisoning: fluke eggs in faeces; stained smears of lymph node aspirate.
Weil-Felix reaction (1916)

Agglutination test exploiting the shared alkali-stable antigen between certain rickettsiae and Proteus OX19/OX2/OXk. Not useful for trench fever or Q fever (both give negative reactions with all three Proteus strains).

  • Ricketts, 1909 — first observed these organisms, genus named in his honour.
  • Coxiella burnetii — the only rickettsia surviving free outside cells (spore-like forms, dust).
  • Rochalimaea quintana — the only rickettsia culturable on blood agar.
  • Phase variation — unique to Coxiella burnetii (Phase I ↔ Phase II).
  • Heartwater — Cowdria ruminantium, Amblyomma tick vector.
  • Salmon poisoning — Neorickettsia via an infected trematode in raw fish, not a tick.
  • Weil-Felix reaction — negative in both Q fever and trench fever, a key exam trap.

Chlamydia

Chlamydia / Chlamydophila

Obligate intracellular, filterable bacteria named for the mantle-like (chlamys) inclusion bodies seen beside infected-cell nuclei; occupy a position between rickettsiae and viruses, but classed as true bacteria based on their Gram-negative-type wall, DNA+RNA content, binary fission and antibiotic sensitivity.

History & habitat EXAM HOT

Sir Samuel Bedson conducted the preliminary psittacosis studies — hence "Bedsoniae" as an older name. GI tract is the natural habitat in birds and mammals, with prolonged faecal shedding; C. psittaci is commonly carried subclinically in the spleen and kidney of normal-appearing birds. Infection in psittacine birds (parrots) = psittacosis; in non-psittacine birds (pigeons, sparrows, turkeys, poultry) = ornithosis.

Cultivation

No cytochromes, essentially anaerobic metabolism, highly host-dependent for ATP ("energy parasites"). Mouse inoculation (intranasal/IP) — death within 10 days, elementary bodies (as Levinthal-Cole-Lillie (LCL) bodies) seen in lung/peritoneal exudate/spleen/brain smears. 6–7 day embryonated egg yolk-sac inoculation. Cell lines: McCoy, BHK-21, L929, Vero — cycloheximide and 5-iodo-2-deoxyuridine treatment enhances cultivation.

Resistance & antigens

Heat-labile — inactivated within minutes at 56 °C; susceptible to ethanol, ether, low-concentration phenol/formalin; infective for days at 4 °C, preserved at −70 °C or lyophilised; EBs are comparatively resistant, surviving days in the environment. Highly sensitive to oxytetracycline, erythromycin, tylosin. Antigens: group-specific LPS (complement-fixation antigen), species-specific, and >10 serovars (OMP-based) of C. psittaci. Produces a lethal (mouse IV), antitoxin-neutralisable, protein toxin.

Pathogenesis

Shed in faeces and semen; young acquire infection mainly through the dam's milk; inhalation of infectious dust/droplets is the main route, ingestion for enzootic abortion/enteritis of ewes. Severity depends on strain virulence (type 1 isolates → abortion/genital infection/enteritis; type II isolates → conjunctivitis/pneumonia/encephalomyelitis/polyarthritis), host age/sex/physiology, route/degree of exposure, and management. Epithelial cell predilection. Enzootic abortion of ewes: placental localisation, abortion in the last 2–3 weeks of gestation, diffuse necrotic placentitis. BUSS disease (sporadic bovine encephalomyelitis): vascular endothelial inflammation + nervous signs. Avian psittacosis: stress triggers splenic/renal multiplication and heavy faecal shedding, producing infectious dust.

Disease summary

  • Enzootic abortion of ewes (EAE), abortion, orchitis, epididymitis, seminal vesiculitis.
  • Feline pneumonitis (conjunctivitis-predominant).
  • Sporadic bovine encephalomyelitis (BUSS disease).
  • Conjunctivitis, rhinitis, hepatitis, polyserositis, polyarthritis.
  • Gastritis, diarrhoea, pneumonia across multiple species.

Diagnosis

  • SpecimensCotyledon/chorion smears, vaginal swabs, aborted-foetus surface swabs, uterine discharge, synovial fluid (arthritis), conjunctival swabs, lung/liver/spleen, paired sera.
  • Direct stainingChemical stains or immunofluorescence for EBs; note Brucella can resemble C. psittaci microscopically — differentiate by serology, isolation, immunostaining.
  • IsolationMouse, egg yolk sac, cell culture — demonstrate LCL bodies.
  • SerologyELISA, CFT, IFAT, LAT — cross-reactivity with other Gram-negatives can complicate interpretation.
  • AdvancedPAGE, restriction endonuclease analysis, monoclonal antibody typing.
Treatment & control

Immunity is both cell-mediated and humoral; serotype-specific antigen drives protective antibody. Killed-organism vaccines exist for feline pneumonitis and enzootic abortion of ewes. Tetracycline and chloramphenicol are effective; penicillin and sulphonamides are not useful.

  • EB vs RB — infectious extracellular EB (small) vs replicating intracellular RB (large); ~40 h full cycle.
  • LCL bodies — Levinthal-Cole-Lillie inclusion bodies, seen on mouse/yolk-sac isolation.
  • Type I vs Type II strains — reproductive/enteric vs respiratory/neuro/arthritic disease patterns.
  • EAE — enzootic abortion of ewes, fresh (not autolysed) aborted foetus — contrast with Campylobacter abortion.
  • Energy parasites — wholly dependent on host ATP.
