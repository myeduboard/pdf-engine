Chapter 01

History of Microbiology

Microbiology

The study of microscopic organisms (bacteria, fungi, algae, protozoa, viruses) invisible to the unaided eye; the field began once glass lenses could be combined to give useful magnification.

Founding figures EXAM HOT

Antony van Leeuwenhoek (1632-1723), a Dutch merchant, is called the father of Microbiology. Grinding lenses as a hobby, he built microscopes giving ~300-400X magnification and observed hay/pepper infusions, describing bacteria, protozoa and yeasts as "animalcules". He communicated his findings to the Royal Society of London (elected member 1680).

Theory of spontaneous generation vs biogenesis

  • Spontaneous generation (abiogenesis) — belief that life arises from non-living matter.
  • Francesco Redi (1688) — sealed-jar meat experiment disproved maggots arising spontaneously.
  • Spallanzani (1769) — boiled, sealed broths stayed sterile; criticised by Needham for excluding air.
  • Louis Pasteur (1861) — swan-neck flask experiment allowed air but excluded microbes, finally disproving spontaneous generation.
  • Rudolph Virchow (1858) — proposed biogenesis: living cells arise only from pre-existing living cells.

Germ theory of disease

Proposed during the golden age of microbiology (1857-1914), opposing the belief that disease was divine punishment. Joseph Lister proved pathogens spread in surgical wards via unwashed hands/unsterilised instruments.

Timeline of key discoveries

  • 1677Leeuwenhoek observes "little animals".
  • 1688Redi disproves spontaneous generation.
  • 1796Jenner — smallpox vaccination using cowpox scrapings from Sarah Nelmes into James Phipps; coined "vaccination" (Latin vacca, cow).
  • 1850Semmelweis advocates handwashing.
  • 1861-62Pasteur disproves spontaneous generation; shows microbes cause fermentation; develops pasteurisation, attenuated vaccines (chicken cholera, anthrax, rabies).
  • 1867Lister — antiseptic surgery with carbolic acid; first pure culture by serial dilution (Bacterium lactis).
  • 1876Koch — first proof of germ theory (B. anthracis); cultivates anthrax in blood serum. Tyndall develops fractional sterilisation (Tyndallization).
  • 1881-82Koch — growth on solid media (1881); Koch's postulates (1882). Ehrlich develops acid-fast staining.
  • 1883-84Metchnikoff discovers phagocytosis; Gram develops Gram staining.
  • 1885-87Pasteur — first rabies vaccination; Petri invents the Petri dish.
  • 1892-99Ivanovski discovers viruses (1892); Beijerinck recognises viral dependence on host cells (1899).
  • 1915/1917Twort and d'Herelle independently discover bacteriophages.
  • 1928-31Fleming discovers penicillin (1928); Ruska builds the first electron microscope (1931).
  • 1977/1983Gilbert & Sanger — DNA sequencing; Mullis — PCR.

Koch's postulates EXAM HOT

  1. PresenceThe agent must be present in every case of the disease.
  2. IsolationThe agent must be isolated and cultured in vitro.
  3. ReproductionThe disease must be reproduced when a pure culture is inoculated into a susceptible host.
  4. RecoveryThe agent must be recoverable from the experimentally infected host.

Chapter 02

Classification of Organisms & Morphology of Bacteria

Evolution of classification systems EXAM HOT

  • AristotleTwo groups — Plant / Animal (fungi included in plants).
  • 1735Carolus Linnaeus — binomial nomenclature; two kingdoms Plantae & Animalia.
  • 1866Ernst Haeckel — adds a third kingdom, Protista (single-celled organisms); bacteria grouped as Monera within Protista.
  • 1938Herbert Copeland — fourth kingdom, Monera, for bacteria alone.
  • 1957Robert H. Whittaker — fifth kingdom, Fungi.
  • 1990Carl Woese — proposes the Domain (above Kingdom), based on rRNA: Archaea, Bacteria, Eucarya. Archaea recognised as molecularly distinct, living in extreme hot/saline/acidic anaerobic environments.

Bacterial size

Bacteria are 0.5-1 μm in diameter, giving a very high surface area:volume ratio — nutrients diffuse to all parts of the cell without any circulatory mechanism.

Shape EXAM HOT

ShapeNameOrigin
SphericalCocci (coccus)Greek kokkos = berry
Straight rodsBacilli (bacillus)Latin bacillus = stick
Helically curved rodsSpirilla (spirillum)—

Variations: cocobacillary, ovoid, filamentous. Curved rods with less than one complete twist = vibrioid (e.g. Vibrio); one or more complete turns = helical. Spirilla are rigid helical bacteria; spirochetes are highly flexible.

Size grouping

  • Large — Spirochetes, Bacillus, Clostridium
  • Medium — Escherichia coli, Proteus
  • Small — Brucella, Pasteurella, Haemophilus
  • Very small — Rickettsia, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma

Bacilli: 2-5 μm long, 0.5-1 μm wide. Spirochetes: up to 20 μm long, 0.1-0.2 μm wide. Cocci: ~1 μm diameter.

Arrangement of cocci

  • Diplococci — divide in one plane, remain in pairs
  • Streptococci — divide in one plane, form chains
  • Tetracocci (tetrad) — divide in two planes, groups of four
  • Staphylococci — divide in three planes irregularly, form bunches
  • Sarcinae — divide in three planes regularly, cuboidal packets

Arrangement of bacilli

  • Mostly singly or in pairs (diplobacilli); some form chains (streptobacilli), e.g. Bacillus subtilis.
  • Trichomes — chain-like with high contact area between cells, e.g. Saprospira.
  • Palisade arrangement — cells lined up like match sticks at angles (Corynebacterium).
  • Streptomyces — form long, branched, multinucleate filaments (hyphae) forming a mycelium.
Chapter recap
  • Woese, 1990 — three-domain system (Archaea, Bacteria, Eucarya) from rRNA data.
  • Whittaker, 1957 — added Fungi as fifth kingdom.
  • Three basic shapes — cocci, bacilli, spirilla.
  • Cocci arrangements — diplo/strepto/tetra/staphylo/sarcinae based on plane of division.
  • Palisade arrangement — characteristic of Corynebacterium.
  • Very small bacteria — Rickettsia, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma.
Chapter 03

Structures External to the Cell Wall

Flagella EXAM HOT

Hair-like helical appendages (0.01-0.02 μm diameter) responsible for motility, thinner than eukaryotic flagella/cilia.

  • Monotrichous — single polar flagellum, e.g. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
  • Lophotrichous — cluster of polar flagella, e.g. Pseudomonas fluorescens
  • Amphitrichous — flagella at both poles, e.g. Aquaspirillum serpens
  • Peritrichous — surrounded by lateral flagella, e.g. Salmonella typhi
  • Atrichous — no flagella

Flagellum structure & motility

Three parts: basal body (Gram-negative = 4 rings — L, P, S, M; Gram-positive = 2 rings), a short hook, and a helical filament made of the protein flagellin. Rotation is analogous to a corkscrew, powered by the proton motive force.

  • Polar-flagellated bacteria swim back-and-forth, reversing rotation direction.
  • Peritrichous bacteria show runs (swimming) punctuated by tumbles (twiddles) when the bundle flies apart.
  • Spirochetes move via periplasmic flagella (axial filament/endoflagella) beneath the outer envelope — no external flagella.
  • Gliding motility (e.g. Cytophaga) occurs only in contact with a solid surface; mechanism unknown.

Pili (fimbriae)

Hollow, non-helical, filamentous appendages made of the protein pilin; shorter, thinner, more numerous than flagella and not involved in motility. Mediate attachment to respiratory/intestinal/genitourinary epithelium, resisting mucus flow. Sex pili mediate conjugation.

Capsule (glycocalyx)

Viscous envelope around the cell wall — slime layer (loosely bound, washes off) vs capsule (tightly attached, definite boundary; too thin to see by light microscopy = microcapsule).

  • Homopolysaccharide capsules — single sugar type, synthesised extracellularly.
  • Heteropolysaccharide capsules — multiple sugar types, synthesised intracellularly via a lipid carrier then exported.
  • Polypeptide capsule — e.g. Bacillus anthracis, a polymer of glutamic acid.

Capsule functions

  • Protects against desiccation by binding water.
  • Blocks bacteriophage attachment.
  • Antiphagocytic — inhibits engulfment by leukocytes, contributing to virulence.
  • Promotes surface attachment; charged capsules increase suspension stability by mutual repulsion.

Sheath, prosthecae, stalk

  • Sheath — hollow tube enclosing chains/trichomes in aquatic bacteria, sometimes iron/manganese-impregnated.
  • Prosthecae — semi-rigid extensions of cell wall/membrane, narrower than the cell.
  • Stalk — non-living, excreted ribbon/tube aiding attachment.
Chapter recap
  • Flagellar types — mono, lopho, amphi, peritrichous, atrichous.
  • Flagellin — the filament protein; motor powered by proton motive force.
  • Runs and tumbles — peritrichous swimming pattern.
  • Periplasmic flagella — unique motility mechanism of spirochetes.
  • Pili/fimbriae — attachment, not motility; sex pili mediate conjugation.
  • B. anthracis capsule — unusual polypeptide (poly-D-glutamic acid) capsule.
  • Capsule — chief antiphagocytic virulence factor across many genera.
Chapter 04

Cell Wall

Bacterial cell wall

A rigid structure external to the cytoplasmic membrane that gives shape and prevents osmotic lysis in hypotonic media; constitutes 10-40% of dry cell weight and is essential for growth and division.

Peptidoglycan (murein) EXAM HOT

The shape-determining, insoluble, cross-linked "bag-shaped macromolecule" found only in prokaryotes. Built from N-acetylglucosamine, N-acetylmuramic acid, L-alanine, D-alanine, D-glutamate and a diamino acid; continuously degraded and rebuilt by wall-associated hydrolytic enzymes.

Gram-positive vs Gram-negative walls

Gram-positive
  • Peptidoglycan up to 50%+ of dry weight
  • S. pyogenes — polysaccharides linked to peptidoglycan
  • S. aureus, S. faecalis — teichoic acids (ribitol/glycerol phosphate polymers) bind Mg2+, protect against thermal injury
  • Very little lipid, except Mycobacterium/Corynebacterium (high lipid; mycolic acids give acid-fastness; cord factor = trehalose dimycolate, toxic)
Gram-negative
  • Peptidoglycan only ~10% of dry weight
  • Outer membrane surrounds a thin peptidoglycan layer, rich in lipid (11-22% dry weight)
  • Attached to peptidoglycan by Braun's lipoprotein
  • Bilayer of phospholipids, proteins and LPS (endotoxin) = Lipid A + core polysaccharide + O antigen
  • Space between cytoplasmic and outer membrane = periplasmic space

Protoplast, spheroplast, L-forms

  • Protoplast — Gram-positive cell wholly stripped of cell wall (lysozyme or penicillin); soft, fragile, spherical; needs high osmotic-pressure medium to avoid bursting.
  • Spheroplast — Gram-negative equivalent; outer membrane persists, giving a cell with two membranes.
  • L-forms — bacterial variants with defective walls and irregular growth, arising from peptidoglycan degradation/synthesis inhibition or mutation.
Chapter recap
  • Peptidoglycan/murein — unique to prokaryotes; shape-determining.
  • Gram-positive — thick peptidoglycan + teichoic acids, little lipid.
  • Gram-negative — thin peptidoglycan + LPS-bearing outer membrane (endotoxin).
  • Braun's lipoprotein — anchors outer membrane to peptidoglycan.
  • LPS = Lipid A + core polysaccharide + O antigen; toxic, called endotoxin.
  • Mycolic acids — Mycobacterium/Corynebacterium; cord factor is toxic.
  • Protoplast vs spheroplast — Gram-positive vs Gram-negative wall-less cells.
Chapter 05

Structures Internal to the Cell Wall

Cytoplasmic membrane EXAM HOT

7.5 nm thick; ~20-30% phospholipid (bilayer, fluid), 60-70% protein (integral — removable only by detergents; peripheral — removable by osmotic shock). Acts as a hydrophobic barrier, hosts respiratory and cell-wall/capsule synthesis enzymes, is impermeable to protons and generates the proton motive force.

Mesosomes

  • Central mesosomes — penetrate deep, near mid-cell, attached to nuclear material.
  • Peripheral mesosomes — shallow, not centrally restricted, not nucleoid-associated.
  • Thought to assist cell division/new membrane synthesis, but may be an artefact of electron microscopy preparation.

Cytoplasm and inclusions

Ribosomes are 70S (50S + 30S subunits; eukaryotes are 80S = 60S + 40S).

  • Volutin (metachromatic) granules — polyphosphate; stain reddish-purple with methylene blue; phosphate reserve.
  • Poly-β-hydroxybutyrate (PHB) granules — chloroform-soluble lipid reserve in aerobes; stained with Nile blue.
  • Polysaccharide (glycogen) granules — stain brown with iodine.
  • Gas vacuoles — buoyancy in aquatic bacteria; hollow rigid cylinders with striated protein boundary.
  • Sulphur globules — in bacteria of H2S-rich environments.

Nuclear material (nucleoid)

No membrane-bound nucleus; DNA is highly coiled (>1000× cell length) in a central area seen by Feulgen staining (light microscope) or as a fibrillar light area (EM).

Endospores EXAM HOT

Thick-walled, highly retractile bodies unique to bacteria — Bacillus, Clostridium, Sporosarcina and a few others. Location: terminal (Clostridium tetani, distends the cell), central (Bacillus cereus), subterminal (Bacillus subtilis).

  • Impermeable to dyes — appear unstained in regular Gram stain; the Schaffer and Fulton stain colours spores green, vegetative cells red.
  • Resistant to desiccation, heat, radiation, disinfectants; most withstand 90°C for ≥10 minutes.
  • Dipicolinic acid (DPA) — unique to endospores, 10-15% of dry weight, complexed with calcium; central to heat resistance.

Sporulation & germination

Sporulation (~8 h): DNA replicates → spore septum forms → membrane pinches off around DNA (forespore) → DPA incorporated → peptidoglycan cortex forms → spore coat added. Germination = formation of a vegetative cell from a spore, with loss of heat/stain resistance, followed by outgrowth.

Structure of the endospore

From outside in: exosporium (thin protein layer) → spore coat (cross-linked keratin, spore-specific proteins) → cortex (loosely cross-linked peptidoglycan + DPA) → core (cell wall, membrane, nucleoid, cytoplasm; only 10-30% water content, gel state). SASPs (small acid-soluble spore proteins) bind DNA, protecting against UV, desiccation and dry heat, and serve as a carbon source during germination.

Exospores (e.g. Methylosinus) form by budding, are heat/desiccation resistant but lack DPA.

Chapter recap
  • 70S ribosome — 50S + 30S; proton motive force generated across the cytoplasmic membrane.
  • Mesosomes — possibly an EM artefact; may aid septum formation.
  • Volutin granules — polyphosphate reserve, stains with methylene blue.
  • Endospore location — terminal (C. tetani), central (B. cereus), subterminal (B. subtilis).
  • DPA — unique to endospores, key to heat resistance.
  • Schaffer and Fulton stain — spores green, vegetative cells red.
  • Endospore layers — exosporium, spore coat, cortex, core.

Bacterial Nutrition

Nutritional groupings EXAM HOT

TypeEnergyCarbonElectrons/H
PhotoautotrophsLightCO2Inorganic
PhotoheterotrophsLightOrganicOrganic
ChemoautotrophsChemical (inorganic)CO2Inorganic
ChemoheterotrophsChemical (organic)OrganicOrganic
  • Chemotrophs use chemical energy; phototrophs use radiant energy.
  • Lithotrophs use reduced inorganic electron donors (chemolithotrophs, photolithotrophs); organotrophs use organic donors.
  • Autotrophs use CO2 as the major/sole carbon source; heterotrophs need organic carbon.

Essential nutrients

  • Nitrogen — atmospheric N2, nitrates/nitrites/ammonium, or organic (amino acids).
  • Water — universal solvent; high specific heat buffers temperature swings; needed for hydrolysis reactions.
  • Sulphur — for cysteine, cystine, methionine; some use elemental S.
  • Phosphorus — nucleotides, nucleic acids, phospholipids, teichoic acids; supplied as phosphate.
  • Metal ions — K, Ca, Mg, Fe required; Zn, Cu, Mn, Ni, Co needed as trace elements (many act as enzyme cofactors).
  • Vitamins — function as coenzymes; some bacteria synthesise them, others are auxotrophic for them.

Media components

  • Chemically defined (synthetic) medium — known chemical composition; required for autotrophs, useful for defining heterotroph requirements.
  • Beef extract — water-soluble substances from lean beef (carbohydrates, N compounds, vitamins, salts).
  • Peptone — acid/enzyme digest of proteins (meat, casein, gelatin); principal organic-N source.
  • Agar — solidifying carbohydrate from marine algae; gels below 45°C; non-nutritive.
  • Yeast extract — rich source of B vitamins, organic N and C.
  • Special growth factors — Haemophilus needs X (haematin) & V (NAD) factors.

Types of media by consistency

  • Liquid (broth) — e.g. nutrient broth.
  • Semisolid — ≤0.5% agar; used for microaerophiles and motility testing.
  • Solid — 1.5-2% agar; used for isolation and colony characterisation.

Types of media by function EXAM HOT

  • General purpose/basal — nutrient broth & agar.
  • Enriched — blood/serum/egg-yolk added, for fastidious bacteria (blood agar, chocolate agar, serum agar).
  • Selective — suppresses some organisms while allowing others (Mannitol Salt Agar, MacConkey agar).
  • Enrichment — liquid media that raise the relative concentration of a target organism without total suppression of others (Selenite F broth, tetrathionate broth).
  • Differential — reveals metabolic differences, e.g. blood agar (haemolytic vs non-haemolytic).
  • Special — for specific organisms, e.g. Dorset egg medium (M. tuberculosis), PPLO agar (Mycoplasma), EMJH medium (Leptospira).
  • Transport media — Stuart's & Amie's (semisolid); charcoal neutralises inhibitory factors; prevent desiccation and overgrowth of contaminants/commensals.

Physical conditions — temperature

  • Psychrophiles — grow at 0-20°C.
  • Mesophiles — grow best at 25-40°C; all pathogenic bacteria are mesophiles (optimum ~37°C).
  • Thermophiles — grow best above 45°C; facultative thermophiles also grow in the mesophilic range; true/obligate thermophiles (stenothermophiles) cannot.

Gaseous requirements

  • Aerobic — require O2 (21%).
  • Anaerobic — O2 toxic; aerotolerant anaerobes tolerate low O2, strict/obligate anaerobes die on brief air exposure.
  • Facultative anaerobic — grow with or without O2.
  • Microaerophilic — need low O2, tolerate atmospheric levels.
  • Capnophilic — need 5-10% CO2.

pH

  • Most pathogens grow best at neutral/slightly alkaline pH (7.2-7.6).
  • Acidophiles pH 0-5.5 — Lactobacillus, Helicobacter pylori.
  • Neutrophiles pH 5.5-8.0 — most pathogens.
  • Alkalophiles pH >8.5 — Vibrio cholerae, Alcaligenes faecalis.
  • Yeasts and molds prefer acidic pH 4-6.
Chapter recap
  • Four nutritional classes — photo/chemo x auto/heterotrophs.
  • Peptone — main organic nitrogen source in media.
  • Agar — non-nutritive solidifying agent, gels below 45°C.
  • Selective vs enrichment — selective suppresses others on solid media; enrichment is liquid, boosts target relative abundance.
  • All pathogens are mesophiles — optimum ~37°C.
  • Obligate anaerobes — die on brief exposure to air; aerotolerant anaerobes survive low O2.
  • X and V factors — required by Haemophilus.
Chapter 07

Bacterial Growth and Cultivation

Reproduction

Primary mode = binary fission (asexual, transverse septum). Some reproduce by budding (e.g. Rhodopseudomonas acidophilia) or, in filamentous genera (Actinomyces, Nocardia), by fragmentation of filaments.

Before division: nucleic acid/protein/macromolecule synthesis, cell growth, new wall components, then septum formation triggered by completion of DNA replication. In Gram-negative cells, the outer membrane forms a pleb/fold at the septum site and invaginates only at the final stage.

Bacterial growth curve EXAM HOT

  • Lag phase — population unchanged; cells grow in mass, synthesise enzymes/RNA/protein. Duration depends on inoculum size, recovery from transfer shock, and time to synthesise needed enzymes.
  • Log (exponential) phase — regular binary fission, geometric increase; rate = generation (doubling) time. Preferred phase for antimicrobial sensitivity testing.
  • Stationary phase — cell number plateaus (nutrient exhaustion/toxic products; death balanced by division). Secondary metabolites (e.g. antibiotics) and sporulation genes activate here.
  • Death/decline phase — viable cell count falls exponentially.

Generation time

Formula
G = t / n
  • t = time interval
  • n = number of generations (doublings) in that interval

Continuous culture

Volume and cell concentration held constant by continuous inflow of fresh medium balanced by removal of spent medium + cells; the classic system is the chemostat.

Measurement of bacterial growth

  • Cell count
    • Direct microscopic count — Petroff-Hausser chamber (1/20,000 mm3 per square); phase-contrast microscopy.
    • Electronic enumeration — particle counter; resistance change as a cell passes an orifice; cannot distinguish live vs dead.
    • Plate count — colony count of viable organisms.
    • Membrane filter count — traps bacteria from large dilute volumes (air/water).
  • Cell mass
    • Turbidimetry — spectrophotometer/colorimeter; measures both live and dead cells.
    • Nitrogen content — ~14% N on dry-weight basis.
    • Dry weight — most direct quantitative approach, needs dense/washed suspensions.
  • Cell activity
    • Measurement of a specific chemical change (e.g. acid end-products) — indirect, special-purpose.

Cultural characteristics

  • Colony — size, margin, surface texture (smooth/rough/mucoid/wrinkled), elevation, consistency (butyrous/viscous/dry-brittle), emulsifiability, optical features.
  • Chromogenesis — insoluble intracellular pigment (S. aureus = gold, M. luteus = yellow); soluble diffusible pigment (P. aeruginosa = pyocyanin, blue); fluorescent pigment (P. aeruginosa = pyoverdin).
  • Broth culture — amount (scanty/moderate/abundant); distribution — uniform turbidity, surface pellicle, or sediment.
Chapter recap
  • Binary fission — primary mode of bacterial reproduction, triggered by completed DNA replication.
  • 4 growth phases — lag, log, stationary, death.
  • Log phase — best for antimicrobial sensitivity testing.
  • G = t/n — generation time formula.
  • Chemostat — continuous culture system.
  • Petroff-Hausser chamber — direct microscopic bacterial count.
  • Pyocyanin/pyoverdin — soluble/fluorescent pigments of P. aeruginosa.
Chapter 08

Distribution of Bacteria, Source & Transmission

Sources of infection

  • Animal sources
    • Normal flora
    • Animals in incubation period — may already excrete the pathogen
    • Animals with overt disease
    • Convalescent carriers — shed pathogen for weeks to months post-recovery
    • Contact/subclinical carriers — acquire the organism without developing disease
  • Inanimate sources (fomites)
    • Contaminated utensils, feed/water troughs, vehicles

Transmission

  • Direct contact — infectious discharges, coitus, or vertical (mother to offspring).
  • Indirect contact — via fomites (water, milk, food, litter, air/dust) or contaminated instruments.

Portals of entry

Inhalation, ingestion, inoculation through skin/mucosa, coitus/contaminated instruments/AI semen, transplacental (via umbilicus).

  • Nosocomial = hospital-acquired infection.
  • Iatrogenic = physician-induced infection.

Host-microorganism relationships EXAM HOT

  • Saprophytism — living on dead/decaying matter.
  • Parasitism
    • Commensalism — lives on host without disease
    • Symbiosis — mutually beneficial
    • Opportunistic pathogen — harmless normally, pathogenic in other sites/immunocompromise
    • Obligate pathogen — always causes disease

Infectivity = capacity to establish in host tissue (penetrate, survive defences, multiply, disseminate). Pathogenicity = capacity of a species to produce disease. Virulence = degree/measure of pathogenicity of a strain.

Bacteraemia, septicaemia, toxaemia

  • Bacteraemia — bacteria present in blood (e.g. Leptospira reaching the kidney).
  • Septicaemia — bacteria actively multiply in blood, producing toxins; anthrax can exceed erythrocyte numbers in blood. Often follows a localised infection becoming generalised (e.g. streptococcal pharyngitis, bubonic plague).
  • Toxaemia — presence of toxins in blood.

Exotoxins vs endotoxins EXAM HOT

FeatureExotoxinsEndotoxins
Chemical natureProteinLipopolysaccharide
Heat stabilityHeat labileWithstands autoclaving
ImmunogenicityConverts to toxoid, neutralised by antitoxinNo toxoid; neutralisation difficult/impossible

  • Exotoxin subtypes: neurotoxins (tetanus toxin), enterotoxin (Vibrio cholerae), cytotoxins (diphtheria toxin), leukocidins (kill leukocytes), haemolysins (lyse RBCs).
  • Formaldehyde inactivates toxigenicity but not antigenicity, producing toxoid (e.g. tetanus, diphtheria toxoids) which induces neutralising antitoxins.
  • Endotoxin (LPS) is released on Gram-negative cell lysis in late growth; causes pyrexia, blood changes, shock — pyrogenicity is the ability to raise body temperature.
Chapter recap
  • Convalescent vs contact carriers — post-recovery shedding vs asymptomatic acquisition.
  • Infectivity, pathogenicity, virulence — establishment ability, disease-causing capacity, degree of pathogenicity.
  • Obligate pathogen — always causes disease.
  • Septicaemia — active multiplication + toxin production in blood.
  • Toxoid — formaldehyde-inactivated exotoxin, retains antigenicity.
  • Endotoxin = LPS — heat stable, weakly immunogenic, uniform mechanism, large lethal dose.
  • Nosocomial vs iatrogenic — hospital-acquired vs physician-induced infection.

Pentose phosphate pathway

Present in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes; a shunt of glycolysis (hexose monophosphate shunt / phosphogluconate pathway). Provides NADPH2 (reducing power) and pentose phosphates for nucleotide synthesis, and can extract energy from 5-carbon sugars. Feeds back into glycolysis via fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate.

Net reaction
6 glucose-6-P + 12 NADP+ → 5 glucose-6-P + 6 CO2 + 12 NADPH2 + Pi

Entner-Doudoroff pathway

Found in aerobic and anaerobic prokaryotes but not in eukaryotes. Glucose → glucose-6-P → 6-phosphogluconic acid → dehydrated to 2-keto-3-deoxy-6-phosphogluconic acid (KDPG) → cleaved to pyruvic acid + glyceraldehyde-3-P; the latter is metabolised by EMP enzymes to give a second pyruvic acid.

Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle

An amphibolic cycle — functions in both catabolism and anabolism (intermediates feed amino acid, purine, pyrimidine biosynthesis).

Net reaction (per acetyl-CoA)
Acetyl-CoA + 3 H2O + 3 NAD + FAD + ADP + Pi → 2 CO2 + CoA + 3 NADH2 + FADH2 + ATP

Two acetyl-CoA form per glucose, so the overall yield doubles. Total ATP from complete oxidation of one glucose molecule = 38.

Electron transport & oxidative phosphorylation

Energy from substrates is released via dehydrogenase reactions; passage of H/electrons through oxidation-reduction carriers (NAD/FADH2 → cytochromes → cytochrome oxidase → O2 → H2O) generates ATP — oxidative phosphorylation.

Chemiosmotic hypothesis EXAM HOT

Proposed by Peter Mitchell (1961): electron flow through carrier molecules pumps H+ across the membrane, generating a pH gradient and electric potential gradient — the proton motive force (pmf). Re-entry of H+ through membrane-bound ATPase drives ATP synthesis.

Chapter recap
  • EMP pathway — universal, O2-independent glycolysis; nets 2 ATP + 2 NADH2 per glucose.
  • Pentose phosphate pathway — source of NADPH2 and pentoses for nucleotide synthesis.
  • Entner-Doudoroff — prokaryote-only, via KDPG intermediate.
  • TCA cycle — amphibolic; total glucose oxidation yields 38 ATP.
  • Mitchell, 1961 — chemiosmotic hypothesis, proton motive force.
  • ATP synthesis — driven by H+ re-entry via membrane ATPase.

Catabolism of lipids

Lipids are converted to glycolytic/TCA intermediates, minimising the extra enzymes needed. Triglycerides are split by lipases into glycerol + fatty acids.

Chapter 11

Bacterial Genetics

Mutation types EXAM HOT

  • Point mutation — single nucleotide substitution
    • Transition — purine↔purine or pyrimidine↔pyrimidine
    • Transversion — purine↔pyrimidine
    • Outcomes: missense (altered protein), nonsense (premature stop codon), neutral (no protein change)
  • Frameshift mutation — insertion/deletion of nucleotide(s), shifting the reading frame, usually producing non-functional protein

Mutagens

  • UV light — causes pyrimidine (thymine) dimers via cross-linking.
  • X-rays — break the phosphodiester backbone.
  • Base analogues (e.g. 5-bromouracil) — substitute during replication, lack normal H-bonding, introduce errors.
  • Intercalating agents (acridine orange, proflavin, nitrogen mustards) — distort structure, cause replication errors.
  • Transposons — cause deletions or inversions.

DNA repair mechanisms

  • Photoreactivation — enzyme PRE (induced by visible light) splits UV-induced dimers.
  • Excision repair — endo/exonucleases cut out damage; polymerases and ligases repair the gap.
  • Inducible (SOS) repair — used by E. coli; diverse coordinately regulated responses.
  • Reverse mutation can also correct a mutation.

Phenotypes of bacterial mutants

Antibiotic/drug resistance, nutritional (auxotrophic) mutants, altered colony form/pigment, antigenic mutants, phage resistance, altered spore/capsule/flagella production, cryptic mutants (retain intracellular enzyme activity but lose permease/uptake function), conditionally expressed mutants, altered fermentation ability.

Bacterial recombination — three mechanisms EXAM HOT

Transformation

Griffith (1928) — mice given live rough (non-capsulated) + heat-killed smooth (capsulated) pneumococci died with live smooth bacteria recovered, showing a transferable "transforming factor". Avery, MacLeod & McCarty (1944) identified this transforming principle as DNA. Naked/cell-free DNA is transferred; one strand degrades, the other base-pairs with the recipient chromosome (needs closely related strains). Occurs naturally in Haemophilus, Neisseria, Xanthomonas, Bacillus, Rhizobium, Staphylococci.

Transduction

Transfer of bacterial DNA between bacteria via bacteriophage — discovered by Zinder & Lederberg (1952) in Salmonella. Phages show lytic (produce and release new phage, lysing the cell) or lysogenic replication (integrate as a prophage; induction by UV/chemicals triggers the lytic cycle).

Generalized transduction
  • Any bacterial DNA fragment can enter the phage head, usually without viral DNA
Specialized (restricted) transduction
  • Only genes adjacent to the prophage are transduced, carried as part of the phage genome

Conjugation

DNA transfer between a mating pair of cells, then separation; large DNA fragments can transfer. Donor (male, F+) cells carry an extrachromosomal sex factor (F factor); recipient (female, F-) cells lack it. Sex pili bind and retract the F- cell; the F+ cell replicates its sex factor and transfers a copy, converting F- to F+.

Plasmids

  • Double-stranded, closed circular, autonomously replicating DNA (exception: linear plasmid of Borrelia burgdorferi); size 1-200 kb.
  • Episomes — plasmids that can integrate into the chromosome (e.g. F factor).
  • Conjugative vs non-conjugative plasmids — able/unable to mediate their own transfer.
  • Cryptic plasmids — no identifiable function beyond self-replication.
  • Resistance (R) plasmids — confer multi-drug resistance (up to 7+ agents on one R factor); major driver of antibiotic resistance spread among normal flora and pathogens.
Chapter recap
  • Griffith, 1928 / Avery et al., 1944 — discovered transformation; identified DNA as the transforming principle.
  • Zinder & Lederberg, 1952 — discovered transduction in Salmonella.
  • Generalized vs specialized transduction — random fragment vs prophage-adjacent genes.
  • Conjugation — F+ (donor, has F factor) transfers DNA to F- via sex pili.
  • R plasmids — carry multi-drug resistance, major clinical concern.
  • Photoreactivation vs excision repair — visible-light dimer splitting vs enzymatic cut-and-patch.
Chapter 12

Classification & Nomenclature of Bacteria

Taxonomic hierarchy EXAM HOT

  • Strain — all descendants of a pure culture (a succession of cultures from one initial colony).
  • Species — a collection of similar strains; the basic taxonomic group. Type strain = permanent reference specimen for the species.
  • Genus — a collection of similar species; one is the type species (permanent example of the genus).
  • Family — group of similar genera
  • Order — group of similar families
  • Class — group of similar orders
  • Division — group of similar classes
  • Kingdom — group of similar divisions

Methods of classification

  • Intuitive method — an expert's judgement based on long familiarity; subjective, characteristics weighted differently by different people.
  • Numerical taxonomy — 100-200 characteristics, each equally weighted; percent similarity %S = NS/(NS+ND) calculated between strains (NS = same characters, ND = different characters); relatively unbiased, gives high stability/predictability.
  • Genetic relatedness — the most objective method
    • DNA homology — strands separated by heat, mixed to form heteroduplexes; degree of pairing measures relatedness (useful at species level).
    • Ribosomal RNA homology — rRNA sequence is highly conserved; measures relatedness above the species level.

Nomenclature

Governed by the international code of nomenclature for bacteria. Names are Latin/latinised binomials — genus (capitalised) + species (lower case), both italicised in print/underlined in writing, e.g. Escherichia coli.

Bergey's Manual & kingdom Prokaryotae

David Bergey and colleagues (University of Pennsylvania) compiled Bergey's Manual of Determinative Bacteriology (1923; 8 editions). Renamed Bergey's Manual of Systematic Bacteriology in 1984, adopting a more systematic approach. Prokaryotes were placed in kingdom Prokaryotae, divided into four divisions:

DivisionCharacter
GracilicutesGram-negative cell wall
FirmicutesGram-positive cell wall
TenericutesLack a cell wall
MendosicutesLack peptidoglycan in the cell wall

The classification/nomenclature committee is ICTB/ICNB — International Committee for Taxonomy/Nomenclature of Bacteria.

Chapter recap
  • Hierarchy — strain < species < genus < family < order < class < division < kingdom.
  • Type strain / type species — permanent reference specimens.
  • %S formula — numerical taxonomy's percent similarity calculation.
  • Genetic relatedness — most objective; DNA homology (species level), rRNA homology (higher levels).
  • Binomial nomenclature — Genus capitalised, species lower case, both italicised.
  • Bergey, 1923/1984 — Determinative → Systematic Bacteriology manuals.
  • 4 divisions of Prokaryotae — Gracilicutes, Firmicutes, Tenericutes, Mendosicutes.
Chapter 13

Morphology of Fungi

Fungi

Eukaryotic, chemoorganotrophic organisms lacking chlorophyll; occur as unicellular yeasts or multicellular, filamentous moulds (thallus filaments 5-10 μm diameter, branched).

Morphological groups EXAM HOT

  • Yeast — simplest form; ovoid/ellipsoid, unicellular; bacteria-like colonies at 37°C. E.g. Cryptococcus neoformans.
  • Yeast-like fungi — resemble yeast but show pseudohyphae; colonies have fungal fringes at 37°C. E.g. Candida albicans.
  • Moulds/filamentous fungi — "true fungi", produce true hyphae/mycelia; optimum 22-25°C. E.g. Aspergillus fumigatus.
  • Dimorphic fungi — yeast(-like) at 37°C, mould at 22-25°C. E.g. Sporothrix schenckii, Histoplasma capsulatum.

Yeast cells: 1-5 μm breadth, 5-10+ μm length; no flagella or locomotor organelle.

Hyphal structure

The mould thallus = mycelium (complex of hyphae) + spores. Each hypha (5-10 μm diameter) has an outer tube-like wall enclosing a protoplasm-filled lumen, bounded by a double-layered plasmalemma. Cross-walls (septa) form by centripetal invagination, usually with a central pore allowing cytoplasmic/nuclear streaming between cells.

Three forms of hyphae

  • Nonseptate/coenocytic — no septa.
  • Septate, uninucleate — single nucleus per cell.
  • Septate, multinucleate — more than one nucleus per compartment.

Mycelia are vegetative (penetrate medium for nutrients) or reproductive (extend into air, form spores). Soluble nutrients absorbed through the wall; insoluble nutrients digested extracellularly first.

Cell wall, capsule, membrane

  • Cell wall — determines shape/morphogenesis; microfibrils of hemicellulose + chitin embedded in a protein/lipid matrix; shared and unique polysaccharide antigens aid classification.
  • Capsule — some fungi have a slime/capsule coat of amorphous polysaccharide; may be antigenic and antiphagocytic.
  • Cytoplasmic membrane — contains ergosterol and zymosterol (vs cholesterol in mammalian membranes) — the basis of antifungal drug selectivity.
Chapter recap
  • 4 morphological groups — yeast, yeast-like, mould, dimorphic.
  • Dimorphic fungi — mould at 22-25°C, yeast at 37°C (host temperature).
  • Three hyphal forms — coenocytic, septate uninucleate, septate multinucleate.
  • Cell wall — chitin + hemicellulose microfibrils, key to morphogenesis.
  • Ergosterol/zymosterol — fungal membrane sterols, target of antifungal drugs.
Chapter 14

Reproduction in Fungi & Classification

Asexual reproduction EXAM HOT

No sex cells/organs, no nuclear union. Occurs by fission, budding, fragmentation of hyphal cells, or spore formation (produced in large numbers).

  • Sporangiospores — single-celled, formed within sporangia at the tip of sporangiophores; motile forms = zoospores (flagellated).
  • Conidiospores (conidia) — small (microconidia) or large multinucleate (macroconidia); formed at hypha tip/side.
  • Arthrospores (oidia) — single-celled, formed by disjointing hyphal cells.
  • Chlamydospores — thick-walled, single-celled, resistant to adverse conditions, formed from vegetative hyphal cells.
  • Blastospores — formed by budding.

Sexual reproduction

Fusion of compatible nuclei from two parents: plasmogamy (cell/protoplast fusion) → karyogamy (haploid nuclei fuse to diploid) → meiosis (restores haploid number). Sex organs = gametangia (male = antheridium, female = oogonium, if morphologically distinct).

  • Gamete copulation — fusion of naked gametes (one/both motile).
  • Gamete-gametangial copulation — male nucleus migrates via a fertilisation tube into the female gametangium.
  • Gametangial copulation — two gametangia/protoplasts fuse, forming a zygote resting spore.
  • Somatic copulation — fusion of vegetative cells.
  • Spermatization — a spermatium unites with a receptive female structure.

Sexual spores

  • Ascospores — in a sac (ascus), typically 8 per ascus.
  • Basidiospores — borne on a club-shaped basidium.
  • Zygospores — large, thick-walled, from fusion of compatible hyphal tips.
  • Oospores — formed in the oogonium after fertilisation by antheridial gametes.

Classification of fungi EXAM HOT

Based mainly on sexual spore/fruiting-body characteristics. Incompletely-described (imperfect) fungi go into form-class Deuteromycetes. Standard endings: Division -mycota, Subdivision -mycotina, Class -mycetes, Subclass -mycetidae, Order -ales, Family -aceae.

  • Zygomycotina — nonseptate; both asexual and sexual reproduction
    • Order Mucorales — sporangial asexual spores, bread moulds (Rhizopus, Absidia, Mucor)
    • Order Entomophthorales — conidial asexual spores
  • Ascomycotina — septate mycelium, sexual reproduction by ascus
  • Basidiomycotina — septate mycelium, sexual reproduction by basidium
  • Deuteromycotina (Fungi imperfecti) — septate, sexual stage not found
    • Class Blastomyces — imperfect yeasts (Candida, Malassezia, Trichosporon)
    • Class Hyphomycetes — septate hyphae, conidial reproduction, most pathogenic fungi (Aspergillus, Blastomyces, Histoplasma, Microsporum, Trichophyton, Penicillium)

Classification by disease

  • Superficial mycoses — outermost skin/hair layers only.
  • Cutaneous mycoses — deeper epidermis, hair and nail.
  • Subcutaneous mycoses — dermis, subcutaneous tissue, muscle, fascia.
  • Systemic mycoses — originate in lung, spread to multiple organ systems.
Chapter recap
  • 5 asexual spore types — sporangiospores, conidia, arthrospores, chlamydospores, blastospores.
  • 4 sexual spore types — ascospores, basidiospores, zygospores, oospores.
  • Deuteromycetes — form-class for fungi with no known sexual stage.
  • Ending conventions — -mycota, -mycotina, -mycetes, -mycetidae, -ales, -aceae.
  • 4 disease-based classes — superficial, cutaneous, subcutaneous, systemic mycoses.
Chapter 15

Growth and Nutrition of Fungi

Growth conditions EXAM HOT

Fungi tolerate higher sugar concentrations and more acidic conditions than most bacteria, giving media a partial selective action against bacterial contaminants. Mostly aerobic; some yeasts are facultative (aerobic + anaerobic). Optimum temperature: saprophytic fungi 22-30°C; pathogenic fungi 30-37°C; some grow even at 0°C, spoiling refrigerated meat/vegetables.

Fungi are heterotrophic — cannot use CO2 as sole carbon source; need an organic source such as glucose. Some use inorganic nitrogen, but all can use organic nitrogen.

Because fungi grow more slowly than bacteria, isolation media favour fungal but not bacterial growth — acidic, high-sugar media are tolerated by moulds but not most bacteria.

Types of mycotic media

  • Natural media — presterilised natural substances: rice, straw, sugar cane bagasse, hair, paddy, grains, dung.
  • Semisynthetic media — natural + synthetic substances: Potato Dextrose Agar, Corn Meal Agar.
  • Synthetic media — fully synthetic organic substances: Sabouraud's Dextrose Agar (SDA) — formulated by Sabouraud, contains maltose and peptone as main constituents; one of the oldest, best-known fungal media.

Emmon's modification reduces the glucose concentration, is widely used for isolating moulds/yeasts, and for growing pathogenic fungi from infected fluids/exudates. Selective action derives from high sugar concentration and low pH (5.6-6.4).

Inoculation technique

  • A small mycelial fragment on the medium is enough to start a colony; a stiff, flat-tipped transfer needle cuts the mycelium.
  • For yeasts, an ordinary bacteriological inoculating loop suffices.
  • Point inoculation — used for moulds; streak plate — used for yeast isolation.
Chapter recap
  • Fungi are heterotrophic — cannot use CO2 as sole carbon source.
  • Optimum temperature — saprophytes 22-30°C; pathogens 30-37°C.
  • Sabouraud's Dextrose Agar — classic synthetic fungal medium, maltose + peptone.
  • Selective mechanism — high sugar + low pH (5.6-6.4) suppresses bacteria.
  • Point inoculation (mould) vs streak plate (yeast).
Chapter 16

General Properties of Viruses

Virus

Obligate intracellular, ultramicroscopic parasites that reproduce only in living cells. Simplest form = nucleic acid genome enclosed in a protein capsid; some have an outer lipid-bilayer envelope (host-derived, studded with viral proteins) that mediates host-cell recognition and entry.

Symmetry types EXAM HOT

  • Icosahedral — 20 triangular faces, 12 vertices, 30 edges; 5-fold symmetry at vertices, 3-fold at faces, 2-fold at edges.
  • Helical — capsomeres set helically around the genome nucleic acid.
  • Complex — e.g. poxviruses, with different proteins/lipoproteins in a complex virion.
  • Binal — e.g. bacteriophages, with an icosahedral head and helical body/tail.

Bacteriophages

Viruses infecting bacteria, discovered independently by Frederick Twort (England, 1915) and Felix d'Herelle (Pasteur Institute, 1917). Twort observed transmissible colony lysis; d'Herelle rediscovered the phenomenon and coined "bacteriophage" (bacteria eater).

Lytic vs lysogenic infection EXAM HOT

Lytic
  • Phage replicates, progeny assembled
  • Bacterial cell lysed to release phage
Lysogenic
  • Viral DNA integrates as a prophage
  • Bacterium multiplies normally, transmitting prophage to daughters
  • Spontaneous induction can trigger removal + lytic cycle

Lysogeny is detected by resistance to superinfection by the same/related phage and by induction (UV/chemicals) to release progeny phage.

Viroids

Low-molecular-weight (1.1-1.3 x 10^5) nucleic-acid-only infectious agents, the smallest known agents of infectious disease; no protein coat; short covalently-closed-circular or linear ssRNA. Cause plant diseases (potato spindle tuber, cucumber pale fruit); mechanism thought to involve interference with host gene regulation.

Prions

Composed of protein only — no detectable nucleic acid. Examples: Kuru, Gerstmann-Straussler (humans); Scrapie (sheep); Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy/BSE (cattle).

Chapter recap
  • 4 symmetry types — icosahedral, helical, complex, binal.
  • Bacteriophages — binal symmetry, icosahedral head + helical body.
  • Twort 1915 / d'Herelle 1917 — independent discovery of bacteriophages.
  • Prophage — integrated viral DNA in the lysogenic cycle.
  • Viroids — smallest infectious agents, RNA only, no protein coat.
  • Prions — protein-only agents; BSE, scrapie, Kuru.
Chapter 17

Virus Classification, Haemagglutination & Interference

Classification criteria EXAM HOT

Early classification used host range and trophism (e.g. neurotropic viruses). Modern classification uses:

  • Nucleic acid — DNA/RNA, single/double stranded, segmented or not, positive/negative sense, molecular weight.
  • Virion structure — symmetry, envelope status, capsomere number/diameter.
  • Site of replication — nucleus or cytoplasm.
  • Host range, trophism, transmission mode, surface antigenic structures.

Baltimore classification (1971)

David Baltimore grouped viruses into 6 classes by genome type and mRNA transcription strategy, based on whether the genome is single/double stranded and whether an intermediate form precedes mRNA production. The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) — formed as ICNV in 1966 — oversees formal nomenclature.

Viral haemagglutination

Some viruses bind and agglutinate RBCs of certain species via viral capsid/envelope protein-receptor interaction; one virion bridging two RBCs, repeated, forms a lattice aggregate. Influenced by pH and temperature; useful for concentrating/purifying viruses and detecting virus in suspected material.

Interference & interferons EXAM HOT

Interference = prevention of virus multiplication, mediated by defective interfering particles or interferons (cell-secreted proteins induced by dsRNA formed during RNA-virus replication).

  • Alpha/beta interferon — produced by most virus-infected cells; diffuse to neighbouring cells, trigger antiviral state.
  • Gamma interferon — produced mainly by T cells/NK cells, triggered by immune-response molecules (e.g. IL-2).

Mode of interferon action

  • Activates dsRNA-dependent protein kinase R and RNase L genes.
  • Stimulates MHC-I and proteasome production, enhancing viral peptide presentation to T cells.
  • Activates NK cells.
  • Induces apoptosis in infected cells.
Chapter recap

  • ICTV — formed as ICNV, 1966; formal viral taxonomy authority.
  • Haemagglutination — virus protein bridges two RBCs; used for virus concentration/detection.
  • Interferons — triggered by dsRNA; alpha/beta from infected cells, gamma from T/NK cells.
  • Interferon actions — PKR/RNase L activation, MHC-I boost, NK activation, apoptosis induction.
Chapter 18

Cultivation of Viruses

Substrates for virus cultivation EXAM HOT

  • Animals (host/experimental)
  • Embryonated eggs
  • Tissue (cell) cultures

Animal inoculation

Used when a virus cannot be cultivated in cell culture/eggs. Mice, guinea pigs, rabbits allow observation of symptoms, lesions and histopathology, but require containment facilities, raise ethical issues, and show variable host susceptibility.

Embryonated eggs

Devised in 1931; one of the most economical and convenient methods. Routes of inoculation: yolk sac, allantoic, amniotic, chorioallantoic, intravenous. Different embryonic cell types support different viruses. Widely used for vaccine production, but not all animal viruses grow this way, and maternal antibody in eggs can interfere with poultry-virus growth.

Tissue (cell) cultures

The preferred method today — convenient, economical vs animals, shows cytopathic effect, and cells can be chosen for susceptibility to a given virus.

TypeFeature
Primary cultureFirst monolayer from normal tissue; limited subculturing before cell death (few to 50-100 divisions; embryonic tissue divides more than adult tissue)
Diploid cell strainsFrom primary culture of a single tissue type (e.g. lung/kidney, embryonic origin); normal diploid karyotype; 50-100 divisions; used for viral studies and human vaccine production
Continuous cell linesInfinite divisions; arise from mutation or malignant tissue; aneuploid karyotype; morphologically distinct, less fastidious, some grow in suspension. E.g. Vero, BHK21, HeLa

Cytopathic effects (CPE) EXAM HOT

  • Cell death, giant cell formation, inclusion bodies.
  • Inclusion bodies — aggregates of unassembled virus subunits or intact virus; intracytoplasmic or intranuclear, acidophilic or basophilic. E.g. Negri bodies in rabies — intracytoplasmic, acidophilic.
  • Plaque — infected area surrounded by normal cells in a monolayer culture.
  • Pock — infected area surrounded by normal tissue on the chorioallantoic membrane.
Chapter recap
  • 3 cultivation substrates — animals, embryonated eggs, cell cultures.
  • Egg inoculation routes — yolk sac, allantoic, amniotic, chorioallantoic, IV.
  • Cell culture is preferred — convenience, economy, CPE observation, susceptibility choice.
  • 3 cell culture types — primary, diploid strains, continuous lines.
  • Negri bodies — pathognomonic intracytoplasmic inclusion of rabies.
  • Plaque vs pock — monolayer culture vs chorioallantoic membrane lesion.
Chapter 19

Replication of Viruses

Steps of viral replication EXAM HOT

  1. AttachmentVirion surface protein binds a specific host-cell receptor (and sometimes a coreceptor).
  2. EntryNaked viruses enter via a membrane pore or endocytosis; enveloped viruses enter via membrane fusion or endocytosis + endosomal fusion.
  3. UncoatingComplete/partial capsid removal to release the genome (at cell surface, cytoplasm, nuclear pore, or within nucleus).
  4. Early transcription/translationEarly mRNAs and proteins made.
  5. Genome replicationNew genome copies synthesised.
  6. Late transcription/translationLate mRNAs and structural proteins made.
  7. AssemblyGenome packaged with late proteins.
  8. Maturation & releaseVirions mature and leave the cell.

Attachment & receptors

Highly specific recognition; some viruses need a second coreceptor. Receptors/coreceptors are usually glycoproteins (e.g. ICAM-1, CD155, CD4, sialic-acid glycoproteins, SLAM). Naked-virus attachment sites sit on the capsid (sometimes specialised fibres/spikes); enveloped-virus sites sit on envelope glycoproteins. Binding starts reversible (few receptors), becomes irreversible as more receptors engage.

Eclipse phase

The period from penetration until mature progeny virions appear, during which no virions are demonstrable inside the cell — the virus "disappears".

Baltimore transcription classes EXAM HOT

ClassGenomemRNA route
Ids DNA→ mRNA
IIss DNA→ ds DNA → mRNA
IIIds RNA→ mRNA
IV+ ss RNAgenome = mRNA directly (plus/positive-sense)
V- ss RNA→ mRNA (minus/negative-sense; needs virion transcriptase)
VI+ ss RNA (retro)→ ds DNA (reverse transcription) → mRNA
VIIds DNA (pararetrovirus)Reverse transcription used, added later to accommodate hepadnaviruses

Ambisense genomes mix positive and negative polarity within one strand (e.g. ssDNA geminiviruses in plants; ssRNA arenaviruses in animals). Nuclear-transcribing viruses (retro + many DNA viruses) use cellular RNA polymerase II; cytoplasmic DNA viruses use a virus-encoded transcriptase.

Capping & polyadenylation

  • Capping — most viral mRNAs get a 5' cap via cellular guanylyl/methyl transferases; some cytoplasmic viruses (pox, reo, corona) encode their own capping enzymes; segmented negative-sense viruses (e.g. influenza) "cap-snatch" from host mRNAs. Picornaviruses do not cap their mRNA.
  • Polyadenylation — adenosine tail added to most eukaryotic/viral mRNA 3' ends, aiding stability and translation initiation; reoviruses do not polyadenylate.

Genome replication & priming strategies

  • Most DNA virus genomes replicate in the nucleus; some (e.g. poxviruses) in the cytoplasm. Most RNA virus genomes replicate in the cytoplasm; minus-strand segmented RNA viruses replicate in the nucleus. Retro/pararetroviruses: RNA→DNA in cytoplasm, DNA→RNA in nucleus.
  • Some ssDNA viruses (parvoviruses) self-prime via a 3' hairpin.
  • Some DNA viruses use cellular primase (polyomaviruses) or their own primase (herpesviruses).
  • Retroviruses use cellular tRNA to prime DNA synthesis, then the 3'-OH of a partly degraded positive RNA template to prime positive-strand DNA synthesis.
  • Some viruses use a protein hydroxyl group (serine/tyrosine) as primer — DNA (adeno), RNA (picorna). Hepadnaviruses use protein to prime negative DNA, RNA primer for positive DNA.

Assembly & release

DNA viruses (except poxviruses) assemble in the nucleus; RNA viruses generally assemble in the cytoplasm. Release: naked virions by cell lysis; enveloped animal viruses by budding through host membranes; a few viruses leave via cytoplasmic channels without destroying the host cell.

Chapter recap
  • 7-8 step replication cycle — attachment through maturation/release.
  • Eclipse phase — no demonstrable virions between penetration and progeny appearance.
  • Baltimore classes I-VII — genome type dictates transcription strategy.
  • Class IV vs V — positive-sense genome = mRNA directly; negative-sense needs virion transcriptase.
  • Cap-snatching — influenza-type mechanism using host mRNA caps.
  • Retrovirus priming — cellular tRNA primes reverse transcription.
  • Budding — release mechanism unique to enveloped viruses.
Chapter 20

Viral Genetics

Mutation

  • Spontaneous mutations — arise naturally during replication; RNA viruses are less stable genetically than DNA viruses, likely due to lack of RNA error-correction.
  • Induced mutations — chemical (nitrous acid — direct base action; base analogues — indirect, mispairing) or physical (UV, X-rays).

Phenotypic mutant classes EXAM HOT

  • Conditional lethal mutants — grow under some conditions, not others (wild type grows in both). E.g. temperature-sensitive mutants (grow at 31°C, not 39°C — altered protein loses functional conformation at high temperature).
  • Host range mutants — grow only in a subset of cell types.
  • Plaque size mutants — larger/smaller plaques than wild type.
  • Drug-resistant mutants
  • Enzyme-deficient mutants
  • Hot mutants — grow better than wild type at elevated temperature.
  • Attenuated mutants — cause milder or no symptoms vs the parental virus.

Recombination

  • Genetic recombination — breaking/rejoining nucleic acid bonds; common in DNA and retroviruses (cellular recombination systems available). Rare in RNA viruses; picornaviruses show low-efficiency "copy-choice" recombination (polymerase template switching), also seen in coronaviruses. Not reported to give viable progeny in negative-strand RNA viruses.
  • Genetic reassortment — a non-classical recombination: dual infection by two variants of a segmented-genome virus (e.g. Orthomyxovirus) produces progeny with segments mixed from both parents.

Complementation

Functional-level interaction with no exchange of genetic material. Two ts mutants with lesions in different genes, co-infecting a cell, each supply the other's missing function so both replicate — but progeny remain temperature-sensitive.

Multiplicity reactivation

UV-inactivated dsDNA viruses infecting a cell at very high multiplicity can still yield live progeny, because genes inactivated in one virion may still be active in another, cooperating to reconstitute a functional genome.

Phenotypic mixing & pseudotypes EXAM HOT

Co-infecting viruses can produce progeny with coat components from both parents, without any genetic alteration — the progeny's identity is determined by which genome is packaged, not the coat. Can occur between related or unrelated viruses; when the coat is entirely from the other virus, it is called a pseudotype (pseudovirion), e.g. a retrovirus nucleocapsid in a rhabdovirus envelope — behaves externally like the rhabdovirus but produces retrovirus progeny after infection.

Defective viruses & DI particles

Defective viruses lack a full gene complement and need a helper virus. Defective interfering (DI) particles compete with the helper virus for its functions, decreasing helper replication (interference); not all defective particles interfere, and DI particles can modulate natural infection.

Antigenic drift vs shift

  • Antigenic drift — minor antigenic changes from point mutations.
  • Antigenic shift — major antigenicity change from genetic reassortment.
Chapter recap
  • RNA viruses mutate faster — lack DNA-level error correction.
  • Conditional lethal (ts) mutants — classic tool for studying essential viral genes.
  • Reassortment — segment mixing in dual infection by segmented-genome viruses.
  • Complementation — functional rescue, no genetic exchange; progeny remain mutant.
  • Pseudotype — genome of one virus in another's coat; behaviour follows the packaged genome.
  • DI particles — defective, compete with helper virus, cause interference.
  • Drift vs shift — point mutation (minor) vs reassortment (major) antigenic change.
